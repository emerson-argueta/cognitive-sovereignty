Phase Three · Grade 9

Still Finding the Calling

Track intensity increases — but no student locks in before Grade 11.

Phase Three is where the tools get applied with genuine stakes and real autonomy. But Grade 9 is explicitly not the year for premature specialization. The adolescent identity is still forming. Students who are pushed into a permanent track at 14 often discover at 18 that it was the wrong one.

Grade 9 is high-intensity breadth: every student experiences all four track orientations in depth, while beginning to invest more time in the one or two that feel most alive.

Track movement in Grade 9 requires nothing more than a conversation. No tests, no formal re-enrollment, no stigma. The Orchestrator makes lateral moves easy.

The Studio Model

Compelled Labor vs Chosen Labor

The day ends at 1 PM. The building stays open until 5 PM.

8:00 AM – 1:00 PM · Core Instruction

  • All required instruction, seminars, and guided deep work
  • The 35/15 rhythm continues
  • All required work completed here — Zero Homework honored
  • Cross-Track Fluency module included every day

1:00 PM – 5:00 PM · The Resourced Studio

  • Facilities remain open as mentored project spaces
  • Staff present — available but not directing
  • Attendance entirely voluntary
  • Technical assistance and safety oversight provided
  • A student who stays until 5 PM because they can't put down what they're building is showing you their calling.
Track Orientations

Four Directions, One Year

All four tracks in Grade 9 — breadth before depth.

Academic

Construction of knowledge
  • Philosophy of science: how knowledge is built, tested, overturned
  • Formal logic and proof theory at introductory depth
  • Rhetoric and the history of argument
  • Research methodology: study design, peer review, replication

Vocational

Skilled trades · agriculture · craft
  • Applied logic in real trade contexts: contracts, specs, safety docs
  • Financial literacy: predatory terms, loans, pricing structures
  • Introduction to apprenticeship with real mentors
  • Systems thinking: how a building, farm, or machine works as a whole

Systems & Engineering

Doers who are also thinkers
  • Systems modeling: how complex systems fail and why
  • Design under constraint: iterative building, prototyping, failure analysis
  • Computational thinking: algorithms, data structures, logic of machines
  • Hands-on engineering introduction projects

Civic & Ethical

Thread through all tracks
  • Policy reasoning: evaluating proposals with logic tools, not tribal affiliation
  • Institutional analysis: how governments, courts, and media distort incentives
  • Moral philosophy as rigorous questions, not answers
  • Democratic participation: a real local civic issue researched and presented
Cross-Track Fluency

No Track Is an Island

Every student in every track maintains fluency across all four — every semester.

Cross-track fluency is the structural guardrail against the system hardening into class stratification. The 'thinkers' and the 'doers' model is exactly what this curriculum was designed to prevent.

Civic Track — All Students

Democratic Participation as Practice

Not as a subject. As an act.

Grade 9 Civic Project — All Students

  • Every student, in every track, identifies a real local civic issue
  • Research the issue using all Phase Two tools: source evaluation, statistical analysis, argument mapping
  • Present a formal analysis and position to a real external audience — not a class, a real audience
  • The project is not graded on the position taken — it is graded on the quality of the reasoning
Operational Layer · Grade 9

Evaluation Primitives

Each capability is only real when it can be demonstrated on an unfamiliar problem under no-hint conditions.

Task 1 · Cross-Domain Reasoning Transfer

Setup: Present a complex problem from a domain outside the student's primary track focus. A student in the Academic track gets an engineering failure scenario; a Vocational track student gets a policy analysis problem. They must apply their Phase Two reasoning tools — Toulmin structure, quantifier analysis, source evaluation — to the unfamiliar domain without track-specific scaffolding.

  • Pass: Student applies reasoning tools accurately to the unfamiliar domain. The analysis is structurally sound even if domain knowledge is limited. Student notes where domain ignorance limits their confidence without abandoning the analytical framework.
  • Fail: Student refuses to engage with unfamiliar domain content, or produces analysis that only works within their track's familiar framing. Reasoning tools abandoned when content is unfamiliar.
  • Failure mode to watch: Students who perform excellent analysis within their track context but have quietly treated their track as the limit of their reasoning — they've learned to think well about one kind of problem, not to think well, period.

Task 2 · Blind Spot Identification

Setup: Student reads two analyses of the same situation — one that aligns with their known values or track orientation, one that does not. They must identify at least two ways their own prior orientation could have distorted their reading of the material, with specific examples from the text.

  • Pass: Student identifies genuine blind spots — specific ways their orientation produced selective attention, charitable reading of aligned evidence, or heightened scrutiny of misaligned evidence. The examples are specific and self-aware without being performative.
  • Fail: Student produces generic self-criticism ("I might be biased") without specific examples. Student identifies the other analysis's weaknesses without applying equal scrutiny to the aligned one.
  • Failure mode to watch: Students who have learned the vocabulary of self-awareness and use it to perform epistemic humility without actually examining their specific analytical choices — they describe the concept of blind spots without locating any of their own.

Task 3 · Genuine Inquiry Generation

Setup: On a topic related to the student's civic project, they must produce five questions they genuinely do not know the answer to — questions that would require real investigation to resolve, not questions that are actually disguised assertions or that the student already knows how to answer.

  • Pass: Questions are genuinely open — the student cannot answer them from current knowledge and cannot predict the answer. Questions are specific enough to be investigable. At least one question challenges an assumption the student holds.
  • Fail: Questions are rhetorical, have obvious answers, or are actually thesis statements in question form. Student cannot produce a question that genuinely challenges their existing position on the topic.
  • Failure mode to watch: Students who produce well-formed questions on topics they have no personal stake in, while being unable to generate genuine inquiry about the civic topic they actually care about — curiosity without courage.

Task 4 · Civic Reasoning Under Real Conditions

Setup: Student presents their civic project analysis to a real external audience — not a class, not a simulation. The audience may include community members, local officials, or subject-matter stakeholders. The analysis must be structured, evidence-based, and responsive to audience questions without fallacy.

  • Pass: Student presents a structured argument with evidence. Responds to challenging questions by engaging the substance, not by restating the original position. Acknowledges the limits of the evidence when pressed.
  • Fail: Presentation is completed but audience questions produce evasion, fallacy, or collapse into mere opinion. Student cannot distinguish between "you disagree with my conclusion" and "you've given me a reason to revise my analysis."
  • Failure mode to watch: Students who perform competently in the presentation but treat challenging questions as attacks rather than as analytical opportunities — the adversarial reflex replacing the epistemic one.
Operational Layer · Grade 9

Gating Criteria

What a student must demonstrate — not just complete — before advancing to Grade 10.

Required Demonstrations

  • Applies Phase Two reasoning tools accurately to a domain outside their primary track — without scaffolding and without abandoning the tools when content is unfamiliar
  • Identifies specific, non-performative blind spots in their own analysis of a topic where they hold a prior position
  • Produces genuine inquiry — questions they do not know the answer to, including at least one that challenges their own working assumptions
  • Completes the civic project with a real external audience presentation that holds up under genuine (not simulated) challenge
  • Demonstrates cross-track fluency: can engage meaningfully with problems from all four track orientations, not just their primary one
  • Has identified a primary track direction through demonstrated engagement — not through test results or external assignment

What Does Not Count as Mastery

  • Strong reasoning within a familiar track context that does not transfer when the domain changes
  • Generic epistemic humility statements without specific examples of one's own analytical errors
  • A civic project presented only to the class or to a simulated audience
  • Cross-track module completion without evidence of genuine engagement — worksheets done, understanding absent
  • Track direction stated as a preference without a body of evidence (voluntary studio time, project choices, self-directed inquiry) to support it
  • Questioning as a performance — producing formally correct inquiry without genuine curiosity driving it

The gating question: When this student encounters a real problem in an unfamiliar domain that touches their actual values and commitments, do they reach for their reasoning tools — or do they reach for their conclusions first and reasoning second?

Operational Layer · Grade 9

Adversarial Exercises

Sovereignty requires training against the actual conditions that undermine independent thinking.

Exercise 1 · The Real Civic Manipulation

Setup: Provide a real local political flyer, petition, or ballot measure summary — not a textbook example, but an actual piece of civic communication that contains misleading framing, selective statistics, or loaded language. Students must evaluate it using full Phase Two tools and produce a corrected version that makes the same argument honestly.

Abstract manipulation examples are easy to analyze because students have no stake in them. The real test of civic reasoning is whether the tools still work when the student encounters manipulation in service of a cause they support. Grade 9 is the first year where students regularly encounter real civic content that touches their actual values. If the reasoning tools are only applied to "the other side," they are not reasoning tools — they are rationalizations with extra steps. This exercise makes that dynamic explicit by deliberately including materials from across the political spectrum, including positions students are likely to find sympathetic.

What this trains: Applying the same analytical standard to all civic communication regardless of whether the conclusion is one the student would endorse — the foundational move of intellectual integrity in democratic life.

Exercise 2 · Ideological Capture Detection

Setup: Provide each student with content from their own cultural or political tribe — material they are likely to encounter in their own home environment, social media, or community — that contains identifiable logical fallacies. Students must apply the same fallacy analysis they would apply to ideologically opposed content.

The human brain is an expert at detecting manipulation from sources it is already suspicious of and nearly blind to identical manipulation from sources it trusts. This asymmetry is not a character flaw — it is a cognitive architecture. The adversarial exercise makes the architecture visible. A student who can identify an appeal to fear in a political ad from the opposing party but cannot identify the same appeal in content from their own community has not developed critical thinking — they have developed sophisticated in-group justification. Real cognitive sovereignty requires the tools to work on yourself.

What this trains: The specific ability to apply analytical scrutiny to ideologically familiar content — to notice when the emotional warmth of in-group communication is carrying logical weight that the evidence cannot actually support.

Exercise 3 · The Expert Who Wandered Off the Map

Setup: Present an argument by a credentialed expert in their field making claims in a domain adjacent to but distinct from their expertise — a climate scientist making claims about economic policy, a physician making claims about educational research, an engineer making claims about sociology. Students must identify where the expertise ends and the extrapolation begins.

Credentialism is one of the most powerful manipulation tools in an information environment precisely because it is usually correct — experts are, in fact, more reliable within their domain than outside it. The problem is the transfer: "this person is an expert" is an existential claim (∃ domain of expertise), not a universal one (∀ claims they make). Grade 9 students are particularly susceptible to this because they have spent three years being trained to respect evidence and expertise. That respect is appropriate and must be maintained — but it must be calibrated to domain, not extended reflexively to any claim from any credentialed source.

What this trains: Domain-specific trust calibration — the predicate logic move of asking "what is the actual scope of this person's expertise?" before weighting their claim as authoritative.

Why adversarial training matters: Grade 9 is the first year where students engage with real civic life — real audiences, real stakes, real manipulation from sources they may actually trust. The adversarial exercises are not designed to produce cynicism or distrust; they are designed to produce calibrated trust. A student who exits Grade 9 knowing how to identify manipulation in content they are sympathetic to is more valuable to a democracy than one who can only spot it from the opposition. Sovereignty is not a weapon you point outward. It is a standard you apply to everything that enters your mind, including the things you want to believe.
