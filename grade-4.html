Phase One · Grade 4

Data Is Not Proof

It is evidence. And evidence must be interpreted — which is where most errors happen.

Grade 4 introduces a fundamental distinction: a graph that shows two things moving together does not prove that one caused the other. Ice cream sales and drowning rates rise together every summer. That does not mean ice cream causes drowning. Correlation is a pattern. Causation is a mechanism. Finding one does not establish the other.

Argument maps grow a new feature this year: branches. A branching flowchart shows not just a single chain of reasoning but the points where counterarguments enter — and how they are addressed.

Core Domain · Data

Reading Data Honestly

What charts show, what they hide, and how to tell the difference.

What Students Practice

  • Reading bar charts, line graphs, and pie charts — what question does each type answer best?
  • Identifying what a chart shows AND what it doesn't show
  • Truncated axes: a graph that starts at 90% instead of 0% makes a small difference look huge
  • Sample size: '3 out of 4 dentists' — but how many dentists were asked?
  • The question: 'What would I need to know to trust this data?'
Core Domain · Logic

Correlation ≠ Causation

The most common error in everyday reasoning about data.

What Students Practice

  • The ice cream / drowning example — what's the hidden third variable? (Summer)
  • Generating their own silly correlations: shoe size and reading ability in children (both increase with age)
  • The three conditions for causation: correlation, correct time order, and no better explanation
  • Identifying alternative explanations for correlations in news headlines
  • Writing: 'This data shows ___ and ___ are correlated. This might be caused by ___. But it could also be caused by ___.'

By the end of this unit, students should be permanently suspicious of headlines that say 'X causes Y' based on a single study. That suspicion is one of the most useful cognitive tools a person can have.

Core Domain · Fallacies

Five Fallacies Recognized

Adding Slippery Slope and Anecdotal Evidence to the set.

New This Year

  • Slippery Slope: 'If we allow X, then Y will happen, then Z, then everything falls apart.' Students map the chain and ask: does each step actually lead to the next? Where is the real evidence for each link?
  • Anecdotal Evidence: 'My grandmother smoked and lived to 90.' One example is not a pattern. Students learn to ask: 'How many cases would we need to see before this counts as evidence?'
Core Domain · Visualization

Argument Flowcharts With Branches

The argument map gains the ability to handle disagreement.

What Students Practice

  • Adding a counterargument branch to the argument map: the main claim, the objection, the response
  • Decision flowcharts with multiple conditions and outcomes
  • Drawing the Slippery Slope as a chain — and identifying which links are broken
  • Correlation vs causation diagram: two parallel tracks with a 'hidden variable?' box between them
Core Domain · Writing

Counterargument — First Appearance

A strong argument does not pretend the other side doesn't exist.

What Students Practice

  • Identifying the strongest objection to their own claim — not the weakest one
  • Writing a concession sentence: 'It is true that ___, however ___'
  • The structure: Claim → Reason → Evidence → Counterargument → Response to counterargument
  • The question: 'What would someone who disagrees with me say? Is their point fair?'
Operational Layer · Grade 4

Evaluation Primitives

Each capability is only real when it can be demonstrated on an unfamiliar problem under no-hint conditions.

Task 1 · Correlation or Causation?

Setup: Present the student with two new correlation examples they have not seen in class — for example, "Cities with more libraries have lower crime rates" or "Students who eat breakfast score higher on tests." Ask: does this data show that one thing causes the other? How do you know?

  • Pass: Student correctly identifies the relationship as correlation, proposes at least one plausible third variable, and states what additional evidence would be needed to establish causation.
  • Fail: Student accepts the correlation as proof of causation, or rejects any connection without reasoning, or can only say "correlation doesn't equal causation" as a slogan without applying the distinction.
  • Failure mode to watch: Student correctly says "correlation, not causation" for both examples but cannot name a third variable or explain the mechanism — has learned the phrase without the underlying logic.

Task 2 · Read a Chart They Have Never Seen

Setup: Show a chart the student has not encountered — a bar graph with a truncated Y-axis, or a line graph covering a cherry-picked time range that makes a trend look more dramatic than it is. Ask: "What is this chart trying to show? Is there anything about how it's drawn that affects your reading of it?"

  • Pass: Student describes what the chart claims to show, identifies the specific visual distortion (truncated axis, selective range), and explains what the chart would look like if drawn honestly.
  • Fail: Student reads the chart at face value and accepts the implied conclusion; or notices "something looks off" without being able to name what or why.
  • Failure mode to watch: Student correctly identifies the truncated axis when axis labels are visible but misses it when the distortion is subtler — has learned to look for the specific example taught rather than the general pattern.

Task 3 · Draw a Flowchart With a Counterargument Branch

Setup: Give the student a position they have not argued before — "Schools should start at 9am instead of 8am" — and ask them to draw a flowchart with: the main claim, one piece of evidence, one counterargument, and a response to that counterargument.

  • Pass: Flowchart includes all four components with accurate directional arrows; the counterargument is a genuine objection (not a weak strawman); the response engages the objection rather than dismissing it.
  • Fail: Flowchart stops at claim and evidence; or counterargument is trivially weak ("some people might not like it"); or response ignores the counterargument.
  • Failure mode to watch: Student draws the correct shape of the flowchart but populates the counterargument branch with the weakest possible objection — has learned the structure without the intellectual honesty it requires.

Task 4 · Identify the Fallacy in a Newspaper Headline

Setup: Present three unfamiliar headlines — one containing Slippery Slope, one Anecdotal Evidence, and one that is a legitimate (non-fallacious) claim. Ask the student to categorize each and explain.

  • Pass: Student correctly identifies the two fallacies and explains the reasoning error in each; correctly identifies the legitimate claim and explains why it does not commit a fallacy.
  • Fail: Student labels all three as fallacies, or mislabels the legitimate claim, or correctly labels a fallacy but cannot explain why it fails.
  • Failure mode to watch: Student labels the Slippery Slope as Anecdotal or vice versa — can spot "something is wrong" but cannot distinguish between the two patterns, suggesting the names were memorized without the underlying logic.
Operational Layer · Grade 4

Gating Criteria

What a student must demonstrate — not just complete — before advancing to Grade 5.

Required Demonstrations

  • Distinguishes correlation from causation on unfamiliar examples, naming a plausible third variable in each case
  • Identifies at least one specific chart-reading problem (truncated axis, sample size, cherry-picked range) in a chart they have not seen before
  • Identifies all five Grade 4 fallacies by pattern in new material — not just examples used during instruction
  • Draws a complete argument flowchart with a genuine counterargument branch on a topic assigned at evaluation time
  • Writes a concession sentence that accurately represents the opposing position before responding to it

What Does Not Count as Mastery

  • Completing correlation-vs-causation worksheets using the ice cream/drowning example or any example taught in class
  • Identifying chart problems only on charts that were shown as examples during instruction
  • Reciting "correlation doesn't equal causation" without being able to apply the distinction to a new case
  • Flowcharts produced in group work where the counterargument branch was contributed by another student
  • Concession sentences that misrepresent the opposing position to make it easier to dismiss

The gating question: Can this student look at a graph they have never seen before, name what it is trying to claim, identify how the visual framing might distort that claim, and tell me what additional data would be needed before accepting it?

Operational Layer · Grade 4

Adversarial Exercises

Sovereignty requires training against the actual conditions that undermine independent thinking.

Exercise 1 · The Alarming Chart

Setup: Show a chart with a truncated Y-axis where the visual jump looks enormous but represents a 2% change in the actual data. Present it with the caption: "Dramatic rise in [metric] shocks researchers." Ask: what does this chart actually show? What would you need to redraw it honestly?

Charts with manipulated scales are among the most common tools used to manufacture alarm or confidence in everyday media. This exercise trains students to override the visceral impression of a "huge spike" or "dramatic drop" by reading the numbers rather than the shape of the line.

What this trains: Separating visual impact from numerical reality — the discipline of reading the axis before reading the trend.

Exercise 2 · The Vivid Story vs. the Invisible Data

Setup: Present two pieces of evidence side by side. First: a short, emotionally compelling story — "My neighbor started exercising and lost 40 pounds in three months." Second: a summary of a large study — "A meta-analysis of 12,000 participants found that the average weight change from this exercise program was 3 pounds over six months." Ask: which evidence is stronger? Which feels stronger? Why is there a gap?

Vivid single stories reliably outweigh statistical data in most people's intuitive judgment, even when the data is orders of magnitude more informative. Students need to feel this gap in themselves — not just know that it exists — before they can guard against it.

What this trains: Noticing the pull of anecdotal evidence and consciously weighting statistical data more heavily — the specific move that the Anecdotal Evidence fallacy exploits.

Exercise 3 · Cherry-Picked Time Range

Setup: Show two versions of the same dataset. Version A covers five years and shows a steady upward trend. Version B covers twenty years and shows that the five-year trend is a recovery from a larger drop — the situation is actually worse than it was fifteen years ago. Both charts are accurate. Ask: are both charts honest?

Selecting a favorable time window is one of the most common ways that accurate data is used to support misleading conclusions. This exercise trains students to ask "what would this look like over a longer period?" as a standard question whenever they encounter a trend claim.

What this trains: Asking about the time horizon of any trend claim — and understanding that two people can both show you accurate data that leads to opposite conclusions.

Why adversarial training matters at Grade 4: Data feels objective. A chart looks authoritative. The adversarial exercises at this grade exist specifically to break that automatic deference — to make students feel, not just know, that numbers can be arranged to mislead without any single number being false. That feeling is the beginning of real data literacy.

Embodied Sovereignty · Grade 4

Systems and Infrastructure

A human being has a small number of genuine physical needs. This year, students trace each one to its source.

What Students Do

  • Water as a system — filtration without equipment, reading watershed geography, what makes a water source safe or unsafe
  • Food as a full cycle — seed to grow to harvest to preserve to replant, student completes the whole chain with one crop
  • Shelter thinking: what keeps a body warm and dry? What materials are available here?
  • Compass and orienteering: navigate a 500m course using compass and map
  • Food preservation: drying and salting as methods without refrigeration

The Systems Connection

  • Cognitive Grade 4 introduces correlation and systems thinking. In embodied sovereignty, the same frame is applied to physical needs: these are not separate skills — they are components of a single system. What happens if one part fails? What are the dependencies?
  • A student who can trace their water from sky to body has done systems analysis. (Kimmerer, 'Braiding Sweetgrass,' 2013)

Assessment

  • Can the student trace the full lifecycle of one food source from seed to table, including preservation?
  • Can they navigate a 500m course with compass and map independently?
  • Can they filter water using available natural materials and explain why each step matters?

The framing question for Grade 4: 'What does a human being actually need to live for one week without a store?' This is not a fear exercise — it is a systems audit. Students who answer it discover that the list is shorter than they expected and that most items on it are learnable.
