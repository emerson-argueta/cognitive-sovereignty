Tier 5
Should Our City Ban Plastic Bags?

Tier 5 is the last year before tracks and the year every tool gets used together. The plastic bag ban question is chosen because it is genuinely contested — serious people hold opposing views for substantive reasons. Students must research, steelman the opposing side, build a full Toulmin argument, and defend it publicly. By the end, the answer matters less than the quality of the reasoning used to reach it.

Unit Design · Tier 5

What This Unit Builds

Five weeks synthesizing every tool from Band I into a complete, structured, publicly defended argument on a genuine policy question.

The plastic bag question is chosen because it has genuine complexity on both sides. Environmental arguments favor bans; economic arguments about burden on low-income shoppers complicate them; alternatives have their own environmental footprint; enforcement questions raise civic governance issues. A student who can navigate this complexity — understanding why thoughtful people disagree — is ready for middle school's more abstract debates.

The Toulmin Model is introduced this week as the formal name for something students have been doing since Tier 1: making claims, giving reasons, pointing to evidence, acknowledging rebuttals. The new elements — warrants, backing, qualifiers — give students language for parts of an argument they have been using informally. The model does not impose a new structure; it names a structure students already understand.

What you will see by Week 5: Students who can articulate the strongest version of the opposing argument before stating their own position. Students who include qualifiers — "given the current evidence," "in most circumstances" — rather than making absolute claims. Students who can be asked "what would change your mind?" and give a specific, honest answer.

Week 1 · Tier 5 Unit

What Is the Problem? — Research and Framing

Before taking a position, understand the landscape. What are the actual facts? What is genuinely disputed?

LensDaily FocusWhat Students DoReasoning Connection
Literacy & WritingReading three sourcesStudents read three sources on plastic bags: a municipal government report on a bag ban, an environmental NGO's advocacy page, and a retail industry association's position statement. Apply the Tier 3 source evaluation checklist. Write: "What does each source agree on? What does each dispute? What is each source's interest in the outcome?"Source interest — who benefits from a particular conclusion — is a key evaluation criterion that complements the technical credibility criteria from Tier 3. An expert with a financial stake in a conclusion requires more corroboration than an independent expert without one.
MathematicsScale of the problemStudents work with real-order-of-magnitude statistics: plastic bags used per year nationally, percentage that end up in landfills vs. recycled, estimated years to decompose, comparison to carbon footprint of alternatives (reusable cotton bags require 131 uses to offset production impact). Students build a one-page data summary.Policy arguments require quantitative grounding. Students who say "plastic bags are terrible for the environment" without understanding the scale of the problem — and the tradeoffs with alternatives — are making rhetoric, not argument.
VisualizationStakeholder mapStudents draw a stakeholder map: who is affected by a plastic bag ban? Consumers, retailers, bag manufacturers, environmental groups, waste management companies, low-income shoppers (for whom free bags are a practical resource), city governments. For each stakeholder: does a ban help or hurt them? By how much?A stakeholder map is a visual version of perspective-taking applied to a policy question. Complex policy always has winners and losers — students who can identify all the stakeholders before taking a position are less likely to ignore affected parties whose experiences differ from their own.
Project WorkWhat do we need to know?Before researching further, students write a "research agenda": five questions they would need to answer to take a well-informed position. Example questions: How much does a plastic bag ban actually reduce plastic waste? What happens to low-income shoppers who relied on free bags? Are reusable bags actually more environmentally friendly?A research agenda written before researching is a guard against confirmation bias — the tendency to search only for evidence that supports a pre-existing position. Students with a research agenda are more likely to find disconfirming evidence because they are looking for it.
Argument/ReasoningInitial position — before researchStudents write their initial position on the question privately, before the research unit begins. Sealed in an envelope. Will be compared to Week 5 position. Key instruction: "You are not committed to this position. The point is to see whether the research changes your mind."Recording an initial position before research is a metacognitive anchor. If students do not record where they started, they cannot notice how they changed — or fail to change — in response to evidence. The "I used to think / now I think" structure from Tier 1 is now applied to policy reasoning.

By end of Week 1

  • Has evaluated three sources using the Tier 3 checklist plus source-interest criterion
  • Has compiled a data summary with key statistics and tradeoffs
  • Has drawn a stakeholder map identifying at least five affected parties
  • Has written and sealed an initial position statement
Week 2 · Tier 5 Unit

What Do Both Sides Actually Say?

Full research week. Both sides given equal and serious treatment before any position is taken.

LensDaily FocusWhat Students DoReasoning Connection
Literacy & WritingTwo-column research notesStudents research and record in two columns: "Arguments for a ban" and "Arguments against a ban." For each argument, note: the source, the evidence cited, and the underlying value (environmental protection vs. economic freedom vs. equity). By end of week: at least four substantive arguments per column.Recording arguments for both sides before choosing is harder than it sounds — students will feel resistance to writing good arguments for the side they disagree with. This resistance is worth naming: if you can't write a strong version of the opposing argument, you don't understand the debate.
MathematicsComparing interventionsData comparison: cities with plastic bag bans vs. without. Did bans reduce plastic bag litter? By how much? What happened to total plastic waste? Students analyze whether bans achieve their stated environmental goals using data. Key finding to surface: some bans reduced bag litter significantly while total plastic waste barely changed.Policy effectiveness is an empirical question distinct from whether the policy is a good idea. A policy that is well-intentioned but ineffective is still bad policy. Students who can separate "was this intended to help?" from "did it actually help?" are reasoning about policy at an adult level.
VisualizationArgument comparison matrixStudents build a comparison matrix: rows = key arguments, columns = for ban / against ban. For each argument: evidence quality (strong/moderate/weak), values it assumes (environment/economy/equity), stakeholder it primarily serves. This matrix is the research infrastructure for the Week 4 Toulmin argument.The argument matrix makes the full debate landscape visible in one view. Students who can see the whole matrix before choosing a position are less likely to cherry-pick evidence — the matrix forces them to account for all the arguments, including the strong ones on the other side.
Project WorkExpert interview preparationStudents prepare five questions for a hypothetical expert (or real local official, if accessible): a city environmental officer, a grocery store manager, a waste management professional. Questions must be genuinely open — designed to learn, not to confirm. "What has the data shown in cities that tried this?" not "Don't you agree that bans are bad?"Open vs. leading questions is the Tier 5 version of the confirmation bias lesson. A question designed to elicit a predetermined answer is not inquiry — it is rhetoric. Students who can tell the difference can also recognize when they are being asked leading questions.
Argument/ReasoningSteelmanning introducedTeacher introduces steelmanning: "State the strongest possible version of the argument you most disagree with." Students practice: pick the opposing side's weakest-seeming argument from their two-column notes. Now strengthen it: add evidence, acknowledge the legitimate values behind it, remove rhetorical excess. "The argument is now stronger. Does it change how confident you are in your position?"Steelmanning is harder and more valuable than strawmanning. A student who can genuinely strengthen the opposing argument before rebutting it is engaging in good faith reasoning — and will produce a more robust final argument because they have tested it against the best available challenge.

By end of Week 2

  • Has at least four substantive arguments recorded per side of the debate
  • Has analyzed policy effectiveness data for cities with and without bans
  • Has built an argument comparison matrix
  • Has produced a steelmanned version of the opposing side's strongest argument
Week 3 · Tier 5 Unit

Building the Toulmin Argument

The full six-component Toulmin structure introduced and practiced. Students build the skeleton of their final argument.

LensDaily FocusWhat Students DoReasoning Connection
Literacy & WritingToulmin componentsTeacher introduces the six components with examples: Claim (our city should ban plastic bags), Grounds (the data showing X% of plastic bag waste), Warrant (the principle: municipalities should reduce preventable environmental harm), Backing (why this warrant is itself justified), Rebuttal (yes, there are economic concerns, AND here is how we address them), Qualifier (in cities over 100,000 population where alternatives are accessible). Students identify each component in a sample argument before writing their own.Warrant and Qualifier are the new Tier 5 elements. A warrant is the general principle that connects grounds to claim — the "why should we accept that evidence as a reason for this conclusion?" A qualifier limits the scope of the claim to what the evidence actually supports. Both prevent overclaiming.
MathematicsQuantifying the rebuttalStudents research the economic counterargument quantitatively: what is the cost of providing alternatives (paper bags, subsidized reusables) relative to the cost of plastic bag litter cleanup and environmental damage? Build a simple cost-benefit comparison. "Is the economic objection a show-stopper, a significant concern, or a manageable tradeoff?"A rebuttal is strengthened by quantification. "The economic concerns are real but manageable" is a weak rebuttal. "Subsidizing reusable bags for low-income shoppers would cost approximately $X per year citywide, compared to $Y in annual cleanup costs" is a strong rebuttal because it engages the concern specifically.
VisualizationToulmin diagramStudents draw a Toulmin diagram — the standard visual representation: Grounds → (via Warrant, backed by Backing) → Claim, with Rebuttal feeding in from the side and Qualifier attached to Claim. Fill in all six components for their argument. Arrows show the logical connections.The Toulmin diagram is the Tier 5 extension of the Tier 1 three-box argument map. The same logical structure — support flows toward conclusion — is now more complex, with the warrant (the invisible principle that justifies the inference) made explicit.
Project WorkArgument skeleton draftStudents write their argument skeleton: one sentence per Toulmin component. Not a polished argument — just the six sentences that will be expanded into the Week 5 policy brief. Share with a partner for a "skeleton check": is each component present? Is the qualifier specific enough?A skeleton draft is a planning document. Writing the six sentences forces students to commit to a specific claim, specific grounds, a named warrant, and a real qualifier — not vague gestures toward each. Vagueness in the skeleton reveals gaps in the argument before they are hidden by polished prose.
Argument/ReasoningWhere is my argument weakest?Students self-evaluate their argument skeleton: "Which component is weakest? Where would a smart opponent attack first? What evidence would make that component stronger?" Students write a one-paragraph "argument vulnerability assessment."Self-identifying the weakest component of your own argument is the highest-level reasoning task in Week 3. Students who can honestly assess where their argument is most vulnerable are practicing the intellectual honesty that separates genuine inquiry from advocacy.

By end of Week 3

  • Can identify all six Toulmin components in a sample argument
  • Has drawn a Toulmin diagram for their own argument
  • Has written a six-sentence argument skeleton and received peer feedback
  • Has identified the weakest component of their argument and named what would strengthen it
Week 4 · Tier 5 Unit

The Strongest Version of the Other Side

Before the debate, students must be able to argue the opposing position as well as they can argue their own.

LensDaily FocusWhat Students DoReasoning Connection
Literacy & WritingWrite the opposing argumentStudents write a full Toulmin skeleton for the opposing position — using the same six-component structure. This is not a parody or a strawman. It must be the strongest honest version of the argument they disagree with. Partner review: "Is this as strong as you can make it, or did you give the other side a weaker version than it deserves?"Writing a strong opposing argument requires genuine engagement with the values and evidence that motivate the other side. Students who can do this honestly are demonstrating intellectual empathy — the ability to understand a position from the inside, not just describe it from the outside.
MathematicsWhat the numbers say for the other sideStudents find the data that best supports the opposing position: jobs in bag manufacturing, consumer cost burden of reusable bags for low-income families, evidence that bans sometimes displace plastic use to other products. Build a mini data summary for the opposing case.Finding the best data for the opposing position is the research equivalent of steelmanning. It requires genuine intellectual effort — most students will naturally look for data that supports their own position. The data for the other side exists; finding it honestly is the task.
VisualizationDebate preparation mapStudents draw a debate map: their main argument in the center, branches showing: anticipated attacks from the opposition, prepared rebuttals for each attack, and the evidence they will cite for each rebuttal. The map is their debate preparation tool for Week 5.A debate preparation map makes the adversarial structure of debate visible before the student is in it. Students who have mapped anticipated attacks are less likely to be caught off guard and more likely to respond to the actual argument rather than the emotionally threatening experience of being challenged.
Project WorkPolicy brief draftStudents write a complete draft of their policy brief: a one-page argument addressed to "the city council" making a recommendation on the plastic bag ban. Must include: clear recommendation (the claim), three pieces of evidence (grounds), the principle behind the recommendation (warrant), acknowledgment of the strongest counterargument (rebuttal), and the conditions under which the recommendation applies (qualifier).A policy brief is an argument in a professional genre. It is addressed to decision-makers, not peers — which requires students to think about their audience's needs: what does a city councilmember need to know, and what can they be assumed to know? Audience-awareness is a rhetorical skill built on top of argument structure.
Argument/ReasoningWhat would change your mind?Students write: "I currently believe ___. I would change my position if ___." The "if" must be specific — not "if someone gave me a good reason" but "if evidence showed that plastic bag bans in comparable cities did not reduce total plastic waste by at least X%." Specific falsification criteria are the mark of a genuine argument rather than a fixed conclusion.Specifying falsification criteria is the most intellectually honest move in the unit. A student who cannot specify what would change their mind is not holding a belief based on evidence — they are holding a conviction. The difference is not which position they hold, but whether it is responsive to evidence.

By end of Week 4

  • Has written a full Toulmin skeleton for the opposing position
  • Has found the strongest data available for the opposing case
  • Has drawn a debate preparation map with anticipated attacks and rebuttals
  • Has written a complete policy brief draft with all six Toulmin components
  • Has specified concrete falsification criteria for their position
Week 5 · Tier 5 Unit

The Debate and the Brief

Public defense. Peer challenge. Final policy brief submitted. Initial position unsealed and compared.

LensDaily FocusWhat Students DoReasoning Connection
Literacy & WritingFinal policy briefStudents finalize and polish their policy brief. One page maximum. All six Toulmin components must be identifiable. Peer editors check: "Can I find the claim? The grounds? The warrant? The rebuttal? The qualifier?" Brief submitted to teacher as the unit's primary written deliverable.The one-page constraint is a discipline: the argument must be strong enough to be compelling in limited space. Verbosity often hides weak reasoning. The best arguments are dense — every sentence does work.
MathematicsOpening statisticsEach student prepares a 60-second data opening for the debate: three key numbers, explained and contextualized. Practice delivering the statistics without reading from notes. Teacher evaluates: are the numbers accurate? Are they contextualized? Do they genuinely support the claim being made?Delivering data orally — with context, accuracy, and relevance — is a communication skill distinct from writing about data. Many people can write accurate statistics but stumble when asked to explain them aloud under questioning.
VisualizationClass debate — structured formatStructured fishbowl debate: inner circle (four students — two per side) argues the plastic bag ban question while outer circle observes and evaluates using a structured rubric: Did the speaker address the actual argument or a strawman? Did they use evidence? Did they acknowledge counterarguments? Did they use a qualifier? Rotate after 10 minutes.The fishbowl format separates debate participants from evaluators — which creates a new skill: watching an argument and evaluating it in real time. Students in the outer circle are practicing the argument audit skills they will use throughout Band II.
Project WorkUnseal and compareStudents unseal their Week 1 position statement and compare it to their final position. Write: "My position changed / did not change because ___. The most important evidence that affected my thinking was ___. If I am being honest, is there anything I avoided looking at closely because I didn't want to change my mind?"The final question — "is there anything I avoided?" — is the unit's deepest metacognitive task. Intellectual honesty about motivated reasoning is one of the hardest human cognitive achievements. Introducing it at Tier 5 does not produce perfection; it introduces the habit of asking the question.
Argument/ReasoningThinking Journal — Band I synthesisSince this is the last unit of Band I, students write a longer Thinking Journal entry: "The thinking tool I learned that surprised me most was ___. The habit I think I have now that I didn't have before Tier 5 is ___. The question I ask first now when someone tells me something is ___."Band I culminating reflection: students articulate the meta-level of what they have learned — not the content about plastic bags, but the thinking tools they now carry. This is the preparation for Band II, where those tools are formalized and applied at greater depth.

By end of Week 5

  • Has submitted a final one-page policy brief with all six Toulmin components
  • Has participated in the fishbowl debate (inner or outer circle)
  • Has compared initial and final positions and reflected honestly on what changed
  • Has written the Band I synthesis Thinking Journal entry
Unit Assessment · Tier 5

What Mastery Looks Like

Policy Brief Evaluation

  • Pass: All six Toulmin components identifiable, grounds are evidenced and cited, rebuttal engages the strongest (not the weakest) counterargument, qualifier is specific rather than vague ("in most cases").
  • Key failure mode: A brief that is eloquent but whose rebuttal strawmans the opposing position — addressing a weak version of the counterargument rather than the strongest. Rhetorical skill without intellectual honesty is the failure mode to watch in Tier 5.

Steelman Transfer Task

  • Setup: Present a new contested question (e.g., "Should schools require uniforms?"). Ask: "Without taking a position yet, state the strongest argument on each side."
  • Pass: Student produces two substantive arguments — one per side — without telegraphing which they personally prefer. Both arguments include a reason and a piece of evidence.
  • Fail: One side is clearly stronger or more developed than the other, indicating the student has already taken a position and is not genuinely engaging the other side.
Gating Connection · Tier 5

How This Unit Connects to Gating Criteria

Gating Criteria Addressed

  • Constructs a complete Toulmin argument with all six components — built across Weeks 3–5, formalized in the policy brief.
  • Can state the strongest version of an opposing argument — steelmanning introduced in Week 2, practiced in Week 4, evaluated in the transfer task.
  • Specifies what evidence would change their position — Week 4's falsification criteria activity directly addresses this criterion.
  • Participates in structured debate without strawmanning — the fishbowl debate and its rubric directly evaluate this in practice.

The Tier 5 gating question — and the question that defines readiness for Band II: Can this student engage a position they disagree with at full strength, build the best possible version of the opposing argument, and then make their own case honestly without overclaiming?
