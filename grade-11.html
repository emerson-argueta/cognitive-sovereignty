Phase Three · Grade 11

The Calling Takes Over

From guided instruction to mentored self-direction.

Grade 11 marks a structural shift: instruction gives way to mentored self-direction. The morning seminar provides context, tools, and cross-track connection. The rest of the day belongs to the student and their Capstone project.

The advisor relationship — not the teacher relationship — becomes primary. An advisor does not direct the student's work. They ask questions, surface blind spots, and ensure the work is meeting the standards the student has committed to.

A student who spends four hours in the Studio every afternoon because they can't put down their project is not being worked. They are being called. That distinction is the entire point of the Studio Model.

Structural Model

Self-Directed Work — What It Looks Like

Structure without direction. Support without control.

The Self-Directed Week

  • Students set their own weekly goals for their Capstone project at the start of each week
  • The advisor reviews goals and asks: 'What would prevent you from achieving these? What do you need?'
  • Mid-week check-in: progress against goals — self-reported, self-evaluated
  • End of week: what was completed, what changed, what was learned from what didn't work
  • Peer review sessions: other students in the track review and challenge the work
  • No grade on process — grade on demonstrated mastery at Capstone Defense
Academic Track · Grade 11

Epistemology & Original Research

What can we know, and how do we know we know it?

What Students Practice

  • Epistemology: the philosophical foundations of knowledge — rationalism, empiricism, constructivism
  • Popper, Kuhn, Lakatos: falsifiability, paradigm shifts, and research programs — how science actually progresses
  • Capstone begins: an original research question chosen by the student, methodology designed, data collection begins
  • Engaging with real academic literature: reading and critiquing actual peer-reviewed papers in the Capstone's field
  • Writing the literature review: what is already known, what is contested, where the gap is
Vocational Track · Grade 11

Full Apprenticeship

Real projects. Real clients. Real consequences.

What Students Practice

  • Minimum 3 days per week in active apprenticeship — the school day bends to accommodate the trade's schedule
  • Taking on a project with a real client and real deliverables — not a simulation
  • Advanced regulatory literacy: permits, codes, compliance documentation in the student's specific trade
  • Business reasoning: full job costing, quoting, scheduling, and margin analysis on a real project
  • Capstone begins: documentation of a complete real project from brief to delivery — every decision recorded
Engineering Track · Grade 11

Advanced Design & Failure Analysis

The build that matters — with formal accountability.

What Students Practice

  • A full-year Capstone design project: defined problem, defined constraints, iterative design, build, test, redesign
  • Advanced failure analysis: studying three to five real engineering failures at depth — reconstructing the full reasoning chain that led to each
  • Cross-disciplinary systems thinking: the student's Capstone must touch at least two systems domains (e.g., mechanical + software, agricultural + logistics)
  • Formal constraint documentation: every design decision mapped against the constraints it satisfies or violates
  • Peer design review: other engineering students formally challenge the design before build
Civic Track · Grade 11

Legal Reasoning & Advanced Policy Design

Reading case law. Constructing legal arguments. Designing defensible policy.

What Students Practice

  • Legal reasoning: how courts interpret statutes, how precedent works, how cases are argued
  • Reading and analyzing real case law in the student's area of interest
  • Advanced policy design: a real policy proposal with full evidence base, stakeholder analysis, objection response, and implementation plan
  • Defending the proposal before a real external critic with a genuine stake in the outcome
  • Applied ethics: working through real moral dilemmas in the civic domain without predetermined answers
Operational Layer · Grade 11

Evaluation Primitives

Each capability is only real when it can be demonstrated on an unfamiliar problem under no-hint conditions.

Task 1 · Self-Assessment Calibration

Setup: The student submits a written self-assessment of their Capstone work-in-progress: what is strong, what is weak, and what remains unresolved. An external evaluator then assesses the same work independently. The two assessments are compared.

  • Pass: The student's self-assessment identifies the same primary weaknesses the external evaluator identifies, even if the student hasn't yet resolved them. The student demonstrates that they can see what is actually weak — not just what is uncomfortable to acknowledge.
  • Fail: The student's self-assessment is uniformly positive, or their identified weaknesses are low-stakes peripheral issues while the central structural problems go unacknowledged.
  • Failure mode to watch: Students who have learned to perform self-criticism by identifying minor flaws as a way to appear self-aware while protecting the central claims from scrutiny.

Task 2 · Blind Spot Identification Without Prompting

Setup: During an advisor check-in, the student is asked: "What are you not seeing in your own work right now?" The evaluator has already reviewed the work and identified at least one genuine blind spot. The student is given no additional prompts.

  • Pass: Student identifies at least one genuine blind spot — a category of question they haven't asked, a source of evidence they haven't considered, or an assumption they've been treating as settled.
  • Fail: Student either reports no blind spots or identifies only surface-level gaps ("I need more sources") without engaging with structural or conceptual gaps in their reasoning.
  • Failure mode to watch: Students who have been coached to say "I know I have blind spots I can't see" as a way to satisfy the requirement without actually doing the cognitive work of locating them.

Task 3 · Resistance vs. Genuine Difficulty

Setup: The student reviews their self-directed work log from the past month and is asked to identify two instances: one where they struggled because the work was genuinely hard, and one where they were slow or avoidant because of resistance. They must distinguish between the two.

  • Pass: Student can articulate specific differences in their internal state and behavior between the two categories — genuine difficulty produces engagement and sustained effort; resistance produces displacement activity and rationalization.
  • Fail: Student characterizes all their productive time as genuine difficulty and all their unproductive time as legitimate rest, without identifying the middle category where they were present but avoiding the hard part of the work.
  • Failure mode to watch: The student who has learned the correct vocabulary ("that was resistance") without being able to give a behavioral account of what the resistance actually looked like — what they did instead, and why.

Task 4 · Goal Quality Assessment

Setup: The student's weekly Capstone goals from the past month are reviewed. The evaluator asks: "Looking at these goals as a set — are they moving toward the defense, or are they comfortable labor that preserves the feeling of progress without advancing the work?"

  • Pass: Student can distinguish which of their past goals were high-leverage (directly advancing the work's defensibility) and which were low-leverage (genuine effort but not aimed at the hard remaining problems).
  • Fail: Student defends all past goals as appropriate, or cannot explain the connection between specific goals and specific gaps that need to close before the defense.
  • Failure mode to watch: Students who set productive-sounding goals that avoid the hardest remaining question in their Capstone — a systematic pattern of comfortable progress.
Operational Layer · Grade 11

Gating Criteria

What a student must demonstrate — not just complete — before advancing to Grade 12.

Required Demonstrations

  • Can accurately identify the central weakness in their own Capstone work without external prompting — and has taken a documented action to address it
  • Has operated in self-directed mode for a full year with a demonstrated ability to distinguish productive from avoidant behavior in their own work log
  • Has a Capstone project that is far enough along for the defense structure to be visible: a defensible central claim, a methodology, and an honest account of what remains unresolved
  • Can identify at least one genuine blind spot in their work — a question they haven't asked, not just a gap they plan to fill
  • Has set weekly goals for the full year and can evaluate their own goal quality — identifying which goals were high-leverage and which were comfortable labor

What Does Not Count as Mastery

  • Completing the self-directed work cycle — setting goals, checking in, reviewing — without evidence that the self-assessment is accurate rather than merely performed
  • A Capstone that has accumulated volume without advancing its central argument — more sources, more research, more documentation that doesn't sharpen the claim
  • Identifying blind spots only when an advisor prompts the student to look for them — the Grade 12 standard requires finding them without prompting
  • Treating the advisor relationship as a source of direction rather than challenge — a student whose work stalls whenever the advisor is unavailable has not developed self-direction
  • Characterizing all slow periods as legitimate rest without being able to identify the specific pattern of avoidance behavior that distinguishes rest from resistance

The gating question: Can this student accurately assess the state of their own work — including its genuine weaknesses — without needing an external evaluator to tell them what is wrong?

Operational Layer · Grade 11

Adversarial Exercises

Sovereignty requires training against the actual conditions that undermine independent thinking.

Exercise 1 · The Seductive Tangent

Setup: The student encounters a genuinely interesting direction — a related question, a new methodology, a compelling adjacent problem — that would require significantly redirecting their Capstone work. They must make a documented decision within 48 hours: pursue it, note it for later, or set it aside entirely.

The seductive tangent is dangerous precisely because it is intellectually legitimate. It is not distraction — it is genuine curiosity pointed at something real. But Grade 11 is the year the Capstone must reach critical mass, and every major redirection resets that accumulation. This exercise trains the student to distinguish between "this is interesting" and "this is the right next move for the work I have committed to."

What this trains: Governing intellectual attention — the capacity to recognize genuine interest, honor it without being redirected by it, and return to the committed direction without feeling that the interesting thing was wasted.

Exercise 2 · The Validation Audit

Setup: The student reviews their last four advisor check-ins and peer review sessions. For each piece of positive feedback received, they must answer: "What specific evidence was this based on? Was the feedback calibrated to the actual quality of the work, or to the effort and enthusiasm I brought to the session?"

Social validation is a particularly effective substitute for rigorous self-evaluation in a model built on advisor relationships and peer review. Students who are engaged, articulate, and obviously working hard tend to receive positive feedback regardless of the current quality of their central argument. This exercise trains the student to separate the quality of their presentation of the work from the quality of the work itself.

What this trains: Distinguishing between being perceived as doing good work and actually doing good work — a distinction that the Capstone Defense will make brutally visible if the student hasn't made it themselves first.

Exercise 3 · The Confirming Advisor

Setup: The student is given a written response to their Capstone draft from a simulated "advisor" who agrees with all of their central positions and raises only minor technical concerns. The student must then identify: what would a genuinely adversarial reader challenge in this work that this advisor did not?

The advisor who confirms what the student already believes is one of the most difficult sovereignty threats to resist, because the confirmation feels like validation of careful thinking rather than a failure of scrutiny. In Grade 11, when students have invested a full year in a direction, the temptation to accept confirming feedback as sufficient is at its peak.

What this trains: Actively generating the adversarial case against one's own work even when the social environment is not providing it — a capability that will be essential in Grade 12 when the Defense panel will not be confirming.

Why adversarial training matters at Grade 11: Self-direction is only sovereign if the self doing the directing can evaluate its own work honestly. Grade 11 is the year when the absence of external evaluation structure creates the highest risk of undetected self-deception — and adversarial training is the mechanism that keeps self-direction from becoming self-confirmation.

Embodied Sovereignty · Grade 11

Domain Specialization

Students who have identified their primary pillar go deep — the same logic as cognitive track specialization. A student going deep in food systems is making a different commitment than one going deep in field medicine or navigation.

Grade 11 Focus

  • Chosen domain deepened through continued practitioner work and self-directed study
  • Teaching others: students begin introducing younger grades to their domain — the surest test of whether knowledge is real
  • Cross-domain fluency maintained: specialization does not mean the other five pillars are dropped
  • Capstone framing begins: what will the Grade 12 self-sufficiency project be, and how does it connect to the intellectual framework?

Connection to Adversarial Training

Grade 11's adversarial exercises in the cognitive curriculum — testing whether students can evaluate their own work honestly — apply here too. A student who believes they can navigate without GPS is tested. The question is not whether they are right. The question is whether they can find out honestly.
