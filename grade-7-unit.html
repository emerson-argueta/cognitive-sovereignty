Tier 7
Should Cities Require Electric Vehicles by 2035?

The EV mandate question sits at the intersection of environmental policy, economic fairness, infrastructure capacity, and technological uncertainty. It is contested among serious people for substantive reasons. Tier 7 uses it to formalize the Toulmin Model as a precision tool, apply Boolean logic to compound claims, map stakeholder positions with Venn diagrams, and run a structured fishbowl debate where participants must defend positions they may personally oppose.

Unit Design · Tier 7

What This Unit Builds

Five weeks deepening argument structure from the Tier 5 Toulmin introduction to precise, attack-tested, Boolean-evaluated debate.

Tier 7's theme is Structure — moving from recognition and use of argument tools to active mapping, evaluation, and construction at higher precision. The EV mandate question is designed to generate genuinely compound claims: "EVs are better for the environment AND more expensive for low-income buyers AND not viable where charging infrastructure doesn't exist." Students must break down compound claims into their Boolean components, evaluate each independently, then recombine.

The fishbowl debate in Week 5 uses assigned positions — students must argue for the side they are assigned, regardless of personal preference. This is not designed to be unfair; it is designed to force students to find the strongest version of a position they may not hold, which is the highest-level application of steelmanning in Band II.

What you will see by Week 5: Students who break down a multi-clause claim into its Boolean components before evaluating it. Students who can attack a Toulmin argument at its weakest link — the warrant — rather than at the most obvious surface-level objection. Students who can argue persuasively for a position they personally oppose, and who understand why that skill matters.

Week 1 · Tier 7 Unit

What Is the Claim Structure Here?

Mapping the full argument landscape before taking any position. What are the actual claims being made, and by whom?

LensDaily FocusWhat Students DoReasoning Connection
Grammar & WritingDecomposing compound claimsStudents receive five policy claims about EV mandates. Each contains multiple sub-claims joined by AND, OR, or implicit conjunctions. Task: break each compound claim into its elementary component claims. Example: "EVs are environmentally superior and economically accessible and technologically ready for a 2035 mandate." → Three separate claims, each independently evaluable.Compound claims are harder to evaluate than simple ones because they allow an opponent to attack the weakest link and an advocate to hide behind the strongest. Decomposition makes each component visible and independently checkable. This is Boolean analysis applied to natural language.
MathematicsClaim truth tablesStudents build truth tables for the decomposed EV claims. If "EVs are environmentally superior" = TRUE and "EVs are economically accessible" = FALSE and "EVs are technologically ready" = TRUE — what is the truth value of the compound claim joined with AND? With OR? Students see: one false component makes an AND-claim false; OR only needs one true component.Truth tables at Tier 7 are no longer about simple toy examples — they are applied to actual policy arguments. A mandate requiring that EVs be simultaneously superior on all three dimensions is an AND-claim: all three must be true. If any one fails, the mandate's strongest justification fails.
VisualizationClaim mapStudents draw a claim map: the main policy claim at the center, all decomposed sub-claims arranged around it with Boolean connectors labeled. Color code: green = supported by evidence reviewed so far, yellow = unclear, red = disputed. This map will be updated throughout the unit.A living claim map — updated as new evidence arrives — externalizes how a complex argument changes in response to evidence. Students who update their map are practicing belief revision at the argument-structure level, not just the conclusion level.
Project WorkSource landscapeStudents map the source landscape: who is producing claims about EV mandates? Auto manufacturers (what interest?), environmental NGOs (what interest?), electric utilities (what interest?), consumer groups (what interest?), government agencies (what interest?). For each: what claims would they be motivated to make, and what claims would they be motivated to suppress?Interest analysis at Tier 7 is more sophisticated than Tier 5's source-interest criterion: students now map the entire ecosystem of interested parties and identify the structural incentives that shape which claims get amplified and which get suppressed.
Argument/ReasoningThe warrant questionTeacher introduces the warrant question as the most important challenge in the Toulmin model: "Even if your grounds are true and your claim is true, why should we accept that this evidence is a reason for this conclusion?" For EV mandates: "Even if EVs produce fewer emissions, why should that mean the government can require their purchase?" Students identify the underlying principle (warrant) for three different arguments about the policy.Attacking the warrant is the most sophisticated form of argument challenge — it does not dispute the evidence or the conclusion; it disputes the principle connecting them. Students who can identify the warrant can find the deepest vulnerability in any argument.

By end of Week 1

  • Has decomposed five compound claims into Boolean components
  • Has completed truth tables for at least two compound AND/OR claims
  • Has drawn a claim map with color-coded evidence status
  • Has identified the warrant for at least three arguments about the EV mandate
Week 2 · Tier 7 Unit

What Does Boolean Logic Say About These Claims?

Evaluating each decomposed component claim independently with evidence — then recombining.

LensDaily FocusWhat Students DoReasoning Connection
Grammar & WritingEvidence evaluation per componentStudents research each of the three main EV mandate sub-claims: (1) environmental superiority (lifecycle emissions data), (2) economic accessibility (cost comparison, subsidies, low-income impact data), (3) infrastructure readiness (charging station density data by region). Write a one-paragraph evaluation of each, rating evidence quality strong/moderate/weak/disputed.Evaluating each component separately before combining allows students to see that the AND-claim's truth depends entirely on its weakest component. Environmental superiority may be well-evidenced while economic accessibility is much more contested — the compound claim cannot be stronger than its weakest component.
MathematicsData comparison — EV vs. combustion engineStudents compare real-order-of-magnitude data: lifecycle CO2 emissions (EV vs. gas by region — depends on electricity grid), total cost of ownership over 10 years (EV vs. gas by income bracket), percentage of US land with charging stations within 50 miles. Build a three-row data table. "Based only on this data, which sub-claim has the strongest support?"Comparing data quality across sub-claims allows students to see which parts of the policy argument are well-grounded and which are speculative — a distinction that is invisible when the compound claim is evaluated as a unit.
VisualizationVenn diagram — stakeholder agreementsStudents draw a four-circle Venn diagram: auto manufacturers, environmental groups, consumer advocates, electric utilities. Where do their positions overlap? Are there claims that all four agree on? Claims that only one group makes? The overlap regions reveal where consensus exists and where the real dispute lives.Stakeholder Venn diagrams reveal the structure of a policy debate. Claims in the intersection of all four circles are on firmer rhetorical ground than claims made only by one group with an obvious interest. The structure of agreement is itself evidence about which claims are more credible.
Project WorkWeakest link identificationBased on the evidence evaluation, students identify which component claim is least supported by current evidence. Write: "The weakest link in the pro-mandate argument is ___ because ___. The weakest link in the anti-mandate argument is ___ because ___." The weakest links will be the targets in the Week 4 argument attack exercise.Identifying weak links before building an argument allows the argument to be strengthened at the points of greatest vulnerability. Students who have found their own weakest link before their opponent does are less likely to be caught off-guard and more likely to have prepared a response.
Argument/ReasoningRecombining with Boolean honestyStudents update their claim map from Week 1, now with evidence ratings for each component. Recombine: "Given that sub-claim 1 is strongly supported, sub-claim 2 is moderately supported, and sub-claim 3 is weakly supported, what is the overall truth value of the AND-compound claim?" Students write an honest assessment: "The compound mandate argument is not fully supported because ___."Honest recombination — acknowledging that one weak component undermines the whole AND-claim — is the Boolean honesty the unit is building toward. Students who can say "the compound argument fails because one component is unsupported" are reasoning at a higher level than students who evaluate the whole argument as a unit and form a general impression.

By end of Week 2

  • Has evaluated each of the three main sub-claims with evidence and quality rating
  • Has completed the four-circle stakeholder Venn diagram
  • Has identified the weakest link in both the pro- and anti-mandate arguments
  • Has updated the claim map with evidence ratings and written an honest compound assessment
Week 3 · Tier 7 Unit

Who Are the Stakeholders and What Do They Each Want?

Perspective mapping — understanding why different people hold different positions on the same evidence.

LensDaily FocusWhat Students DoReasoning Connection
Grammar & WritingWriting from four perspectivesStudents write four short paragraphs — one from each stakeholder perspective — addressing the same EV mandate question. Each paragraph must represent the stakeholder's values accurately (not a strawman) and use the evidence most relevant to that stakeholder. A low-income family's paragraph will focus on purchase cost; an environmental NGO's will focus on lifecycle emissions.Writing from multiple perspectives — simultaneously and at full strength — is a high-level empathy exercise with a critical thinking function: it forces students to understand how the same evidence can be weighted differently based on different underlying values. Different positions often reflect different values, not different facts.
MathematicsDistributional analysisStudents analyze who bears the costs and who receives the benefits of an EV mandate. Using simplified distributional data: upper-income households (benefit more from tax credits, can afford EVs more easily), lower-income households (face greater financial burden, may live in areas with poor charging infrastructure). "Is an EV mandate regressive?" Students calculate the effective cost as a percentage of income for different income brackets.Distributional analysis — identifying who pays and who benefits, and in what proportion of their income — is the mathematical tool for evaluating equity arguments. A policy that costs everyone $1,000 is very different for a household earning $30,000 vs. $300,000. Percentage of income makes the comparison honest.
VisualizationValues mapStudents draw a values map: different stakeholders connected to different underlying values (environmental protection, economic efficiency, personal freedom, equity, innovation). Lines connect each stakeholder to their primary values. Where the same policy scores differently for different values — that is where the genuine disagreement lies.Most policy debates are ultimately about values, not facts. Once facts are agreed upon, disagreement persists because people weight different values differently. Making the values map explicit allows students to see that reasonable people can look at the same facts and reach different conclusions — because they disagree about what matters most, not what is true.
Project WorkStakeholder role preparationStudents are assigned a stakeholder role for the Week 5 debate — they may not get their preferred role. This week: research the assigned stakeholder's actual position in depth. What evidence does this group cite? What values motivate them? What objections do they raise? Students build a research dossier on their assigned stakeholder.Role assignment that may conflict with personal preference is deliberate. Students who must argue for a position they personally oppose are forced to engage it at full strength — which builds both steelmanning ability and genuine understanding of why the position is held by real people.
Argument/ReasoningValue-based vs. evidence-based disagreementStudents identify two arguments about the EV mandate: one where the disagreement is primarily about facts (disputed emissions data) and one where it is primarily about values (should government mandate consumer choices?). Write: "This disagreement cannot be resolved by more data because ___." Understanding when a dispute is normative vs. empirical is a crucial distinction for political and ethical reasoning.Not all disagreements can be resolved by more evidence. Some are about values — what matters, what is fair, what rights people have — which cannot be settled empirically. Students who can identify which type of disagreement they are in can target their arguments more precisely and avoid the frustration of providing evidence to someone who disagrees about values.

By end of Week 3

  • Has written four stakeholder perspective paragraphs on the same question
  • Has completed a distributional analysis showing who pays and who benefits
  • Has drawn a values map connecting stakeholders to underlying values
  • Has built a research dossier on their assigned debate stakeholder
Week 4 · Tier 7 Unit

Building and Attacking the Toulmin Argument

Full Toulmin construction for the assigned stakeholder position — then systematic attack at the warrant.

LensDaily FocusWhat Students DoReasoning Connection
Grammar & WritingToulmin argument for assigned stakeholderStudents write a complete Toulmin argument from their assigned stakeholder's position — with all six components. The warrant must explicitly state the underlying principle: "Because governments have both the right and the obligation to regulate markets when externalities cause significant harm" or "Because individual economic freedom should not be overridden by environmental policy goals." The warrant is the most important component to make explicit.Stakeholder-assigned Toulmin arguments force students to construct a good-faith argument for a position that may conflict with their personal values — which requires them to genuinely understand the underlying warrant that makes the argument coherent from the inside. You cannot write a good argument for a position you only understand from the outside.
MathematicsAttack the quantitative claimStudents exchange arguments with a partner. Task: attack the other argument's quantitative claims. Where is the data weakest? Where are the numbers most context-dependent? Where could they be interpreted differently? Write a one-paragraph numerical attack — not "that's wrong" but "that number is misleading because ___" or "that data only applies under the condition ___."Attacking quantitative claims specifically — rather than qualitative ones — is harder and more valuable. Numbers often appear more certain than they are, and a student who can show that a specific number is context-dependent or misapplied has made the strongest possible evidence attack.
VisualizationAttack the warrantStudents draw their partner's Toulmin diagram and mark the warrant. Then write a "warrant attack": "I accept your grounds (the evidence) and I accept your claim as a possibility, but I reject your warrant because ___." Warrant attacks do not need to prove the claim is false — they only need to show that the evidence does not require the conclusion even if the evidence is true.A warrant attack is the most sophisticated challenge in Band II. It accepts the evidence while rejecting the inference — which forces the argument-maker to articulate and defend the principle that connects evidence to conclusion. Most people have never been asked to explicitly defend their warrants; it is cognitively demanding and very revealing.
Project WorkPrepare three rebuttalsStudents prepare three written rebuttals for anticipated attacks on their argument: one rebuttal for a data attack, one for a warrant attack, and one for a counterexample. Each rebuttal must be specific — not "that's a good point but..." but "that attack assumes ___, which is false because ___" or "that attack applies in context X but my claim is qualified to context Y."Prepared rebuttals make the debate argument dynamic — students who have anticipated attacks can respond to the actual argument rather than being caught off-guard and deflecting. Deflection under pressure is a reliable sign that the argument was not well-prepared.
Argument/ReasoningMetacognitive argument auditStudents review their argument and ask: "Is my warrant the kind of thing a reasonable person could genuinely accept? Or am I relying on a principle that only someone who already agrees with me would accept?" Students revise their warrants to be as widely accessible as possible — not changing their position, but making the reasoning more accessible to people who start from different values.Making a warrant accessible to someone who holds different values is the most sophisticated rhetorical and argumentative challenge in Band II. It requires understanding the other person's values well enough to frame your warrant in terms that connect to theirs — without misrepresenting either.

By end of Week 4

  • Has written a complete Toulmin argument for assigned stakeholder with explicit warrant
  • Has attacked a partner's quantitative claim with a specific numerical challenge
  • Has drawn a warrant attack diagram and written the warrant challenge in plain language
  • Has prepared three written rebuttals for anticipated attacks
Week 5 · Tier 7 Unit

Structured Fishbowl Debate

Assigned positions. Real-time warrant attacks. Class evaluates argument quality, not conclusion preference.

LensDaily FocusWhat Students DoReasoning Connection
Grammar & WritingOpening argument — 3 minutes eachEach inner-circle debater delivers a 3-minute opening argument from their assigned stakeholder position. Must include: the compound claim broken into Boolean components, evidence for each component, and an explicit warrant statement. Outer circle records: "I heard the following components. I heard the following warrant. What was missing?"The 3-minute opening with a required warrant statement forces economy — students must choose which evidence is most important, which is the highest-level argument construction skill. Time constraints reveal what students actually understand vs. what they can produce with unlimited preparation.
MathematicsReal-time fact-checkingTwo outer-circle students are designated fact-checkers. Their job: track any specific numbers cited in the debate and note: is that number accurate? In context? Misleadingly framed? They report at the end of the debate on three specific numerical claims made.Real-time fact-checking under time pressure develops a critical skill for evaluating spoken arguments: the ability to hold specific claims in working memory while simultaneously listening to new claims and evaluating both. This is the hardest cognitive mode of argument evaluation.
VisualizationLive argument mapTwo outer-circle students map the debate live: recording claims, recording attacks, recording rebuttals. By debate's end, a live argument map shows the structure of the exchange. "Which attacks were answered? Which were deflected? Which were conceded?" Post the map for class review.A live argument map makes the structure of a debate visible after the emotional experience of participating in it. Students who can see which of their attacks landed and which were deflected have the structural feedback to improve their debate performance next time.
Project WorkPost-debate briefAfter the debate: every student writes a 200-word post-debate brief. "The most persuasive argument made was ___ because ___. The weakest argument made was ___ because ___. My personal view on the mandate, after hearing all arguments, is ___ and it [changed / did not change] from my Week 1 position because ___."The post-debate brief separates argument quality evaluation from personal conclusion — students must evaluate which arguments were strongest independently of which conclusion they prefer. This is the most important evaluative skill the debate develops.
Argument/ReasoningThinking Journal — Structure synthesisThinking Journal: "The most important thing I learned about argument structure in Tier 7 is ___. A warrant attack is different from an evidence attack because ___. When I encounter a compound 'AND' claim in the future, I will ___."The synthesis reflection anchors the unit's three key contributions: warrant attacks, Boolean decomposition of compound claims, and the distinction between evidence disputes and value disputes. These are the structural additions Tier 7 makes to the Toulmin foundation laid in Tier 5.

By end of Week 5

  • Has participated in fishbowl debate with a 3-minute opening including Boolean decomposition and explicit warrant
  • Has performed real-time fact-checking or live argument mapping as an outer-circle function
  • Has written a post-debate brief evaluating argument quality independently of personal preference
  • Has written a Structure synthesis Thinking Journal entry
Unit Assessment · Tier 7

What Mastery Looks Like

Warrant Attack — New Argument

  • Setup: Present a short Toulmin argument on an unfamiliar topic. Ask: "What is the warrant? Attack it — without disputing the evidence or the claim directly."
  • Pass: Student correctly identifies the implicit warrant, articulates it explicitly, then challenges the principle ("I accept that X causes Y, but I reject the principle that Y is sufficient grounds for policy Z because..."). Does not attack the evidence.
  • Fail: Student attacks the evidence instead of the warrant — which is technically easier but misses the specific skill. Or student says "the warrant is wrong" without explaining what is wrong with the principle it represents.

Boolean Compound Claim — New Example

  • Setup: "Cities should ban gas stoves because they pollute indoor air AND increase greenhouse gas emissions AND alternatives are readily available." Evaluate this compound AND-claim.
  • Pass: Student decomposes into three components, notes which are well-evidenced and which are contested, and concludes that the AND-claim's overall strength is limited by its weakest component — specifying which that is.
Gating Connection · Tier 7

How This Unit Connects to Gating Criteria

Gating Criteria Addressed

  • Decomposes compound claims into Boolean components and evaluates each independently — Weeks 1 and 2; assessed in transfer task.
  • Identifies and attacks the warrant of an argument — distinct from attacking the evidence — Weeks 1, 4, and 5; assessed in warrant attack transfer task.
  • Constructs a Toulmin argument for an assigned position (not necessarily their own) — Week 4's assigned stakeholder argument.
  • Distinguishes value-based from evidence-based disagreement — Week 3's values map and normative vs. empirical distinction exercise.

The Tier 7 gating question: Can this student identify the warrant — the invisible principle connecting evidence to conclusion — in a Toulmin argument, and challenge it without attacking the evidence? This is the deepest structural move in Band II argumentation.
