Core Skills · Grade 6 · Disciplinary Literacy

Literacy — No Longer a Separate Block

The K–5 literacy sequence is complete. Reading and writing are now the medium of everything.

At Grade 6, dedicated literacy instruction ends — not because reading and writing are complete skills, but because they have become the instruments through which all other learning happens. Shanahan and Shanahan's research on disciplinary literacy identifies this transition precisely: "basic literacy" (decoding, fluency, general comprehension) gives way to "disciplinary literacy" — reading and writing practices specific to how different fields construct and evaluate knowledge. (Shanahan, T. & Shanahan, C., 2008. "Teaching disciplinary literacy to adolescents: Rethinking content-area literacy." Harvard Educational Review, 78(1), 40–59. One of the most cited papers in middle school literacy research.)

The Five Lenses structure of Phase Two is disciplinary literacy in practice. Reading & Comprehension sessions analyze real texts for argument structure, bias, and evidence quality. Grammar & Writing sessions produce precise written arguments using the week's Core Problem as subject matter. A student reading a scientific claim and an editorial about the same topic is practicing disciplinary reading. A student writing a policy argument and a data analysis on the same problem is practicing disciplinary writing. These are not separate from literacy — they are literacy at its most sophisticated.

What Disciplinary Literacy Looks Like in Grade 6

  • Reading for argument structure: Every text in the Reading & Comprehension sessions is analyzed with the tools built in Grades 1–5 — What is the claim? What is the evidence? What is missing? Who wrote this and why? The Toulmin map can be drawn for any argumentative text encountered
  • Writing for precision: Grammar & Writing sessions focus on sentence-level accuracy — the difference between "shows," "suggests," "proves," and "implies" is a logical distinction, not just stylistic. Students choose words deliberately
  • Extended revision: Written arguments are revised across multiple sessions, not produced in one sitting. Revision is where most of the learning happens. (Graham, S. & Perin, D., 2007. Writing Next. Alliance for Education. Meta-analysis of 123 studies: writing process instruction — including revision — has among the highest effect sizes in writing research.)
  • Cross-disciplinary reading: The same Core Problem generates a scientific text, a news article, and a policy document in the same week. Reading all three means reading three different argument structures with different evidence standards — the core move of disciplinary literacy

The K–5 literacy sequence was preparation for this moment. A student who decoded fluently by Grade 2, wrote structured arguments by Grade 3, annotated argumentative texts by Grade 4, and synthesized multiple sources by Grade 5 arrives at Phase Two's disciplinary demands fully equipped. The skill is now invisible — which means it is fully internalized.

Core Skills · Grade 6 · Mathematical Reasoning

Mathematics — Embedded in the Pattern Recognition Lens

Ratios, algebra, and statistical thinking are now one of the Five Lenses — not a separate subject.

At Grade 6, dedicated mathematics instruction also ends as a separate block. Mathematical reasoning becomes the Pattern Recognition lens of the Core Problem structure — applied to each week's real-world question rather than taught as an isolated subject. NCTM identifies the Grade 6 focal points as ratios and proportional relationships, early expressions and equations, and statistical thinking. All three are directly applicable to Core Problem analysis. (NCTM, 2006. Curriculum Focal Points. National Council of Teachers of Mathematics. Grade 6 focal points: ratios and proportional relationships; expressions, equations, and inequalities; statistical thinking.)

The transition from arithmetic to algebraic thinking — from specific numbers to generalized relationships — is one of the most cognitively demanding shifts in mathematics education. Students who have strong proportional reasoning (from Grade 5) and understand variables conceptually navigate this transition successfully. Those with only procedural arithmetic face a significant wall. (Sfard, A., 1991. "On the dual nature of mathematical conceptions." Educational Studies in Mathematics, 22(1), 1–36.)

What Mathematical Reasoning Looks Like in Grade 6

  • Ratios and proportional reasoning: The Pattern Recognition lens applied to quantitative relationships — finding the rule that governs a relationship and generalizing it. Every Core Problem can be examined for quantitative relationships
  • Expressions and equations: Variables as placeholders for unknown quantities in real situations drawn from Core Problems — not abstract symbol manipulation disconnected from meaning
  • Statistical thinking — distributions: Not just mean and median, but spread, outliers, and what a distribution's shape means. Extends the existing curriculum's misleading-statistics work to include the full picture of what a dataset says and does not say
  • Negative numbers in context: Temperature below zero, debt, position relative to a reference point — conceptual grounding precedes arithmetic rules
  • Coordinate geometry: Graphing relationships — the visual connection between an equation and a line on a plane. Spatial reasoning applied to algebraic thinking. (Uttal, D.H. et al., 2013. Psychological Bulletin, 139(2). Spatial skills are highly trainable and strongly predict algebraic achievement.)

The K–5 mathematics sequence was preparation for this moment. A student who built number bonds in Kindergarten, understood multiplication conceptually in Grade 3, reasoned with fractions in Grade 4, and worked with variables in Grade 5 arrives at Grade 6 algebra ready to generalize what they already understand. Mathematical reasoning is now one of five analytical lenses — which is exactly where it belongs.

Phase Two · Grade 6

The Structure Changes

From subject-by-subject instruction to one Core Problem, five lenses, every week.

Grade 6 marks the transition from Phase One's developmental foundation to Phase Two's interdisciplinary Deep Work model. The 35/15 focus-break rhythm begins. Core Problems replace subject-isolated lessons. The Orchestrator pre-links all five lenses to each week's problem.

The Internal Heatmap — practiced since Pre-K — is now named, formalized, and taught as an explicit analytical tool. Students learn to describe what has always been a habit.

Nothing in Phase One was wasted. Every fallacy, every cause-and-effect diagram, every Thinking Journal entry — it all feeds directly into what Grade 6 formalizes.

Structural Model

The Deep Work Block

8:00 AM – 1:00 PM. One problem. Five angles. No context-switching.

Each week is anchored by a Core Problem — a real-world question that all five lenses address simultaneously. The five lenses are: Reading & Comprehension, Grammar & Writing, Pattern Recognition, Visualization, and Project Work.

The Vertical Thread

The Internal Heatmap — Formally Named

What has been practiced since Pre-K is now an explicit analytical protocol.

🌡 The Heatmap Protocol — Grade 6 Version

Students are now taught to describe their Heatmap practice in explicit terms: Before I engage with this claim, I notice what my body is doing. A spike in heart rate or a feeling of immediate agreement or outrage is a signal that a manipulation lever may have been pulled.

The Logic Gate protocol: when a strong emotional response is detected, the Logic Gate stays closed until the emotional pulse subsides. Then — and only then — are analytical tools applied.

The Thinking Journal gains a new section this year: the weekly Heatmap log. 'This week I noticed a strong response to ___. The feeling was ___. After pausing, my analysis was ___.'

Core Domain · Fallacies

Four Formal Fallacies

The six from Phase One plus four new ones — introduced in the context of Core Problems.

Carried From Phase One (Now Formally Named)

  • Appeal to Popularity (Ad Populum) · Strawman · False Dichotomy · Slippery Slope

New in Grade 6

  • Appeal to Authority (Ad Verecundiam): Citing an expert as proof rather than as one piece of evidence. 'A doctor said it' does not end the inquiry.
  • Circular Reasoning (Begging the Question): 'The Bible is true because the Bible says so.' The conclusion is hidden in the premise.
  • Hasty Generalization: Concluding from too few cases — 'I met two rude people from that city, so everyone there is rude.'
  • Post Hoc (False Cause): 'I wore my lucky socks and we won — my socks caused the win.' Sequence is not causation.
Core Domain · Visualization

Mind Maps to Logic Diagrams

The visualization progression for Phase Two begins.

What Students Practice

  • Mind Maps: central concept, branching ideas, connections — used to explore a Core Problem before analyzing it
  • Argument Diagrams: Toulmin structure (from Grade 5) now drawn consistently for every major claim
  • Bias Heatmaps: visual annotations on a text showing where emotional language is concentrated
  • Simple Boolean logic gate diagrams: AND/OR/NOT circuits drawn for real decision problems
Branching Competency

Tracks Are Introduced

Orientations, not assignments. The signal is curiosity, not test scores.

In Grade 6, students encounter all three track orientations through the Core Problem structure — but no student is assigned to a track. The Orchestrator begins recording engagement signals: where does a student linger, ask unsolicited questions, or produce work beyond the requirement?

By the end of the year, teachers initiate a conversation — not an assessment — about which kinds of problems felt most alive.

Operational Layer · Grade 6

Evaluation Primitives

Each capability is only real when it can be demonstrated on an unfamiliar problem under no-hint conditions.

Task 1 · Run the Heatmap Protocol on a Live Argument

Setup: Read aloud a short persuasive passage — a political appeal, an advertisement, or an opinion piece — that the student has not seen before. Ask them to narrate their Heatmap process in real time: Notice, Name, Gate, Map.

  • Pass: Student describes a specific physiological or attentional signal, names the type of response (e.g., "I felt pulled to agree immediately"), reports pausing before analyzing, and then produces an argument map of the passage — in that sequence.
  • Fail: Student jumps directly to argument analysis without narrating a Notice or Name step; or describes the content of the passage rather than their internal response to it; or applies the Heatmap label retrospectively after analysis is complete.
  • Failure mode to watch: Student performs the four steps as a verbal checklist ("I noticed, I named, I gated, I mapped") without any genuine pause or self-observation — mimicking the protocol rather than using it.

Task 2 · Identify Four Grade 6 Fallacies in New Context

Setup: Present four short paragraphs — each containing exactly one of the new Grade 6 fallacies (Appeal to Authority, Circular Reasoning, Hasty Generalization, Post Hoc) — drawn from contexts not used in instruction. Ask the student to identify each fallacy, name it, and explain the specific reasoning error.

  • Pass: Student correctly identifies and names all four fallacies, gives an accurate description of the error in each, and does not confuse any with the Phase One fallacies.
  • Fail: Student misidentifies one or more fallacies, or correctly names them but cannot explain the structural error — only that the argument "doesn't seem right."
  • Failure mode to watch: Student confuses Appeal to Authority with Ad Hominem (both involve the speaker rather than the argument), or confuses Post Hoc with Correlation/Causation — indicating the pattern hasn't been internalized distinctly enough to survive surface-level similarity.

Task 3 · Convert a Mind Map to a Logic Diagram

Setup: Provide a pre-drawn mind map on an unfamiliar topic — five or six nodes connected loosely. Ask the student to identify which connections represent logical relationships (cause → effect, premise → conclusion) and redraw those connections as a structured argument diagram with labeled connectors.

  • Pass: Student correctly identifies at least three logical relationships, draws them with direction and labeled connectors (e.g., "therefore," "because," "but"), and distinguishes associative connections (things that are related) from inferential ones (things that follow).
  • Fail: Student redraws the mind map with the same structure, just adding arrow labels; or cannot distinguish associative from inferential connections.
  • Failure mode to watch: Student draws a visually clean diagram that uses all the right vocabulary but applies connectors randomly — the labels are decorative rather than structural.

Task 4 · Produce a Thinking Journal Entry With Heatmap Log

Setup: At the end of a Core Problem week, ask the student to write the Heatmap log section of their Thinking Journal without prompting the specific format. The entry should describe a moment that week when they noticed a strong response, name it, and trace what happened to their analysis after the gate.

  • Pass: Entry contains a specific moment (not a general reflection), a named response type, an account of what the gate decision was, and a comparison between the initial impulse and the post-gate analysis.
  • Fail: Entry summarizes the week's content rather than a specific internal moment; or describes the emotion without any analytical follow-through; or is written in the passive voice of a report rather than first-person observation.
  • Failure mode to watch: Entry is well-written and uses all the Heatmap vocabulary but describes a moment of intellectual engagement ("I was excited by this topic") rather than a genuine bias or emotional pull — performing self-awareness without the self-examination.
Operational Layer · Grade 6

Gating Criteria

What a student must demonstrate — not just complete — before advancing to Grade 7.

Required Demonstrations

  • Executes the full Heatmap protocol (Notice → Name → Gate → Map) in real time on an unfamiliar persuasive text, narrating each step with specificity
  • Correctly identifies and distinguishes all four new Grade 6 fallacies in material not used during instruction
  • Converts a mind map to a structured logic diagram with accurately labeled directional connectors on an unfamiliar topic
  • Produces at least three Thinking Journal Heatmap log entries across the year that meet the specificity standard: named response, gate moment, and comparison of pre- and post-gate analysis
  • Applies all Phase One tools (source evaluation, correlation/causation, truth tables, argument flowcharts) to at least one Core Problem without prompting

What Does Not Count as Mastery

  • Completing the Heatmap log on familiar topics where the student already knows their position
  • Identifying fallacies only in examples used during instruction
  • Argument diagrams that are structurally correct but populated with weak or fabricated counterarguments
  • Thinking Journal entries that describe intellectual interest rather than emotional or attentional pulls that could distort analysis
  • Track engagement signals produced in group projects where individual contribution cannot be isolated

The gating question: Can this student, when confronted with a persuasive argument that triggers a strong positive or negative response, notice that response, pause, and produce an analysis that would be the same regardless of which direction the response pulled?

Operational Layer · Grade 6

Adversarial Exercises

Sovereignty requires training against the actual conditions that undermine independent thinking.

Exercise 1 · The Heatmap Exploit

Setup: Present a carefully constructed argument designed to produce a strong positive physiological response — not through logical strength but through resonant language, appealing framing, and alignment with values the student is known to hold. The argument should be logically flawed (ideally committing a Hasty Generalization or Appeal to Authority). Ask the student to run the Heatmap protocol and report what they notice.

This exercise is the hardest one in the curriculum to date. The Heatmap protocol is designed to catch manipulative arguments, but a well-crafted argument that feels right — that aligns with the student's values, uses their language, and appeals to authorities they respect — will pass through the gate if the student is not specifically trained to notice positive pulls as well as negative ones. Most students are trained to distrust what repels them. Very few are trained to be equally suspicious of what attracts them.

What this trains: Treating strong agreement with the same scrutiny as strong disagreement — recognizing that the Heatmap logic gate must close for positive emotional spikes, not only outrage or alarm.

Exercise 2 · Circular Reasoning From an Authority

Setup: Present a statement structured as follows: "Leading researchers in the field agree that this is the established consensus. The consensus has been validated by repeated expert review. Therefore, we can be confident this is true." Ask: what is missing from this argument? What would make it a valid rather than circular claim?

This exercise targets the interaction between Appeal to Authority and Circular Reasoning — two fallacies that are individually recognizable but become nearly invisible when combined. The "experts agree → therefore it's true → because experts who know the evidence agree" loop is one of the most common structures in media, institutional communication, and everyday persuasion. Students need to see it operating at full speed, not just as a textbook example.

What this trains: Identifying circular reasoning embedded within apparent appeals to evidence — asking "what is the underlying evidence that is not 'experts agree with each other'?" as the diagnostic question that breaks the loop.

Exercise 3 · The Vivid Single Case as Policy Argument

Setup: Present a compelling narrative — a single detailed story of someone harmed or helped by a policy, product, or practice. The story is emotionally specific, the person is sympathetic, the outcome is clear. Then ask: what would you need to know before deciding this story is evidence for a general conclusion? Draft the study design that would give you that evidence.

Vivid single cases are the primary mechanism through which Hasty Generalization operates in real-world argumentation. A single person's story — especially a sympathetic one — activates narrative reasoning, which is faster, more emotionally compelling, and more resistant to revision than statistical reasoning. By asking the student to design the study that would actually test the generalization, this exercise forces the transition from narrative to analytical mode explicitly.

What this trains: Recognizing the gap between "this happened to one person" and "this is what generally happens" — and being able to specify, concretely, what evidence would close that gap rather than just asserting that the gap exists.

Why adversarial training matters at Grade 6: Phase One tools are powerful when applied to obviously flawed arguments. The Grade 6 adversarial exercises are designed to test whether those tools survive contact with arguments that are compelling, well-constructed, and emotionally resonant. A student who can identify a Strawman in a textbook but not in a speech they want to agree with has not learned to think — they have learned to perform thinking. The adversarial exercises are the test of whether the tools are real.

Embodied Sovereignty · Grade 6

Ecology as Infrastructure

Skills are now understood as systems. The natural world is not a backdrop — it is the original infrastructure.

What Students Do

  • Field medicine introduced: wound care, infection recognition, sanitation — framed as 'understanding how the body heals'
  • Water sanitation: boiling, solar purification, assessing source quality from watershed position
  • Low-tech agriculture: a multi-season garden project starts this year and runs through Grade 8 — students plan, plant, and maintain it
  • Ecology as systems: food webs, water cycles, and climate patterns studied as interdependent infrastructure, not as separate biology topics

The Systems Thinking Connection

  • Cognitive Phase 2 builds systems thinking — understanding how complex structures function and fail. In embodied sovereignty, the same frame is applied to living systems: what are the inputs, outputs, and dependencies of a local watershed? Of a food web? Of a human community in a specific place?
  • Ecology is systems thinking with the highest possible stakes. (Wohlleben, 'The Hidden Life of Trees,' 2015; NOLS expedition curriculum)

Assessment

  • Can the student explain a local ecosystem as a system with inputs, outputs, and failure points?
  • Can they demonstrate basic wound care correctly — cleaning, dressing, signs of infection to watch for?
  • Can they describe three methods of water purification and explain when each is appropriate?

The multi-season garden project is the connective tissue of embodied sovereignty in Phase 2. It is not a one-year exercise — it is a commitment. Students who plant in Grade 6 harvest in Grade 7, save seeds in Grade 8. The system only makes sense over time.
