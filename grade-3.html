Core Skills · Tier 3

Literacy — Fluency, Comprehension Strategies & Multi-Paragraph Writing

30 minutes daily. "Learning to read" is complete. "Reading to learn" begins.

Tier 3 marks the transition Chall identified as the most consequential in all of reading development: from decoding as the primary task to comprehension as the primary task. Most students who were struggling decoders in Tier 2 have either caught up or been identified for additional support. What remains for all students is building the comprehension skills that allow complex texts — texts with arguments, conflicting sources, and inferential gaps — to be read critically. (Chall, J.S. & Jacobs, V.A., 2003. "Poor children's fourth-grade slump." American Educator, 27(1), 14–15. The "fourth grade slump" — a widely documented dip in reading comprehension around Tier 4 — is predicted by insufficient comprehension instruction in Grades 2–3, not by decoding failure.)

Writing shifts this year from paragraph-length to multi-paragraph composition. The SRSD (Self-Regulated Strategy Development) framework provides explicit strategies for planning and organizing extended writing — the same gradual release approach used since Tier 1. (Graham, S. & Harris, K.R., 1989 onwards. SRSD. Journal of Educational Psychology. The most replicated writing intervention in the research literature.)

SegmentTimeWhat HappensSource
Comprehension Strategy Practice12 minOne strategy practiced per week with a new text: activating prior knowledge, monitoring comprehension (noticing when understanding breaks down), generating questions, visualizing, summarizing. Students apply the strategy and name what they are doing. Connecting to the reasoning curriculum: "generating questions" is the same as asking "what's the evidence?" — now applied to reading.Duke, N.K. & Pearson, P.D. (2002). "Effective practices for developing reading comprehension." In Farstrup & Samuels, What Research Has to Say About Reading Instruction. The five strategies with the strongest evidence bases. Explicit strategy instruction — naming what skilled readers do and teaching it directly — produces the largest and most durable comprehension gains.
Vocabulary — Tier 2 Words6 minThree Tier 2 words introduced or reviewed per week: high-frequency academic words that appear across disciplines (conclude, contradict, sufficient, relevant, infer). Semantic mapping: connecting the new word to related words the student already knows.Beck, I.L., McKeown, M.G. & Kucan, L. (2013). Bringing Words to Life: Robust Vocabulary Instruction. Guilford Press. Tier 2 words — high-frequency academic vocabulary across domains — produce the highest instructional return. Semantic mapping outperforms definitional instruction for retention and transfer.
Multi-Paragraph Writing12 minStudents write 2–3 paragraph opinion or informational pieces on the week's reading or Core Problem. Explicit instruction in paragraph organization: topic sentence, supporting details, transition to next paragraph. Students plan before writing using a simple graphic organizer — not as a requirement but as a thinking tool.Graham & Harris SRSD. Graham, S. & Perin, D. (2007). Writing Next. Alliance for Education. Literacy Gating — End of Tier 3

  • Reads an unfamiliar grade-appropriate text and correctly identifies the main claim and two pieces of supporting evidence — without prompting
  • Applies a named comprehension strategy (e.g., "I'm monitoring — I lost track of the argument here") to a text they have not read before
  • Writes a 2–3 paragraph opinion piece with a clear topic sentence, supporting evidence, and a closing — planned before written
  • Uses at least 3 Tier 2 vocabulary words correctly in written work
Core Skills · Tier 3

Mathematics — Multiplication, Division & Fractions as Quantities

30 minutes daily. Multiplication is understood before it is memorized. Fractions are quantities, not procedures.

Tier 3 is the year multiplication becomes a central focus — and also the year when rote memorization of times tables, without conceptual grounding, can create lasting fragility. The research is clear: conceptual understanding of multiplication (as repeated addition, as arrays, as scaling) must precede and accompany fluency practice, not follow it. Students who only memorize isolated facts cannot reconstruct them when forgotten and cannot apply them flexibly in novel problems. (Willingham, D.T., 2009. Why Don't Students Like School? Jossey-Bass. Memory is the residue of thought — facts understood deeply are retained more reliably than facts drilled in isolation.)

Fractions are introduced as quantities — points on a number line, parts of a set, parts of a whole — not as a procedure for comparing numerators and denominators. The CPA sequence applies: fractions are physical (fold paper, share objects) before they are drawn (fraction bars, number lines) before they are symbolic (3/4). (Bruner, J.S., 1966. CPA sequence. NCTM, 2006. Curriculum Focal Points. Fractions are the most critical Tier 3 mathematical focal point — and the most common source of later mathematical difficulty when introduced procedurally.)

Daily 30-Minute Block — Internal Structure

SegmentTimeWhat HappensSource
Multiplication — Conceptual & Fluency12 minFirst half of year: multiplication as arrays, as repeated addition, as equal groups — always concrete or pictorial before abstract. Second half: spaced fluency practice using games and interleaved problems (not worksheets). Students compare strategies: "Is 6×8 easier to think of as 6 eights or as double 3×8?" Strategy comparison deepens understanding.Rittle-Johnson & Star (2007). Strategy comparison produces deeper understanding than practicing one method. Cepeda, N.J. et al. (2006). "Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks." Psychological Bulletin, 132(3), 354–380. Spaced repetition produces dramatically better long-term retention than massed drill. Boaler, J. (2015). Mathematical Mindsets. Jossey-Bass. Timed times-table tests activate math anxiety — all fluency practice is untimed.
Division — As Inverse of Multiplication8 minDivision introduced as "how many groups?" and "how many in each group?" — two distinct interpretations of the same operation. Always connected to multiplication: if 4×6=24, then 24÷6=4. Division is not a separate operation; it is multiplication with an unknown.Clements & Sarama (2009). Learning and Teaching Early Math. Routledge. Understanding division as the inverse of multiplication — rather than as a separate procedure — enables flexible reasoning and prevents the common error of treating the two operations as unrelated.
Fractions as Quantities10 minFractions on the number line: where does 3/4 go? Fractions as equal parts of a shape. Comparing fractions: is 2/3 more or less than 3/4, and how do we know? Physical materials (fraction tiles, folded paper) before drawn representations before abstract notation.Bruner (1966) CPA. Siegler, R.S. et al. (2011). "Fractions: The new frontier for theories of numerical development." Trends in Cognitive Sciences, 15(5), 201–210. Fraction understanding at this stage is one of the strongest predictors of algebra achievement in secondary school. Conceptual fraction instruction now prevents algebraic difficulty later.

Mathematics Gating — End of Tier 3

  • Explains why 6×7=42 using at least two different representations (array, repeated addition, or decomposition)
  • Solves a division problem by connecting it to a multiplication fact — not by a separate procedure
  • Places fractions on a number line and explains why 3/4 is greater than 1/2
  • Demonstrates multiplication fluency through 10×10 — not by counting on fingers, not by a memorized procedure alone but by genuine understanding of the relationships
Band I · Tier 3

Where Did That Come From?

The claim-reason-evidence structure now includes a fourth question: who said it?

Every piece of information has an origin. Tier 3 teaches students to trace claims back to their source and ask three questions: Who made this claim? What did they want? What evidence did they provide? These are the questions that, by Tier 8, will become a full credibility evaluation protocol.

Truth tables arrive this year — not as abstract math, but as a visual tool for tracking when AND/OR/NOT conditions are satisfied. Students discover that logic can be drawn, not just spoken.

Core Domain · Source Evaluation

Who Said It and Why

The three questions that begin every source evaluation.

What Students Practice

  • The three source questions: Who wrote this? Why did they write it? What evidence do they give?
  • Primary vs secondary sources: the difference between the original claim and a report of it
  • Recognizing bias in simple terms: 'This company is saying their own product is the best — is that surprising?'
  • Comparing two sources on the same topic: what do they agree on? What do they disagree on?
  • The question 'how do we know?' applied to every claim encountered in reading

The goal is not skepticism of everything — it is the habit of checking. 'I wonder where that came from' is the sentence this year is designed to make automatic.

Core Domain · Fallacies

Four Fallacies This Year

Two carried from Tier 2, two new ones introduced through story.

Carried From Tier 2 (deepened)

  • Appeal to Popularity: Now applied to more complex examples — social media trends, peer pressure in stories
  • Ad Hominem: Distinguishing personal attacks from legitimate challenges to credibility

New This Year

  • Strawman: Introduced through a story where a character misrepresents what their friend said — 'That's not what I meant!' The lesson: attack the real argument, not a weaker version of it
  • False Dichotomy: 'You're either with me or against me' — the story explores the middle ground that the false choice erases. Students map the full spectrum of options.
Core Domain · Logic

Truth Tables & Logic Gates

Boolean logic becomes visual — and verifiable.

What Students Practice

  • Simple truth tables: two inputs (A and B), one output — AND, OR, NOT
  • Filling in a truth table: systematically testing all possible combinations
  • Logic gate symbols introduced: AND gate (D-shape), OR gate (curved), NOT gate (triangle with bubble)
  • Drawing a simple logic circuit for a two-condition rule
  • Real-world application: 'The door opens if card is valid AND time is between 8am and 5pm' — draw this as a gate
Core Domain · Visualization

Argument Maps With Sources

Adding a source layer to the claim-reason-evidence diagram.

What Students Practice

  • Extending the Tier 1/2 argument map to include a source box: where did this evidence come from?
  • Simple flowcharts: question → search → evaluate source → accept or reject → update claim
  • Side-by-side source comparison: two boxes, same claim, different sources — what's different?
  • Logical connector map: claims connected by 'because,' 'therefore,' 'however,' and 'but'
The Vertical Thread

Heatmap + Source Bias

Connecting the emotional response to where a claim came from.

🌡 New Layer: Source Bias and the Body

This year the Heatmap practice expands: students notice not just their response to a claim, but their response to who made the claim. Do you trust a doctor more than a random person? Do you distrust a company when it praises itself?

The Thinking Journal prompt: 'This week I noticed I trusted ___ more than ___ because ___. Was that fair?'

This is the first time students connect the physiological trust response — 'this person seems credible' — to the question of whether that response is justified by evidence.

Operational Layer · Tier 3

Evaluation Primitives

Each capability is only real when it can be demonstrated on an unfamiliar problem under no-hint conditions.

Task 1 · Source Protocol on a Cold Text

Setup: Give the student a short passage they have never seen — a paragraph from a children's magazine, a product label, or a brief news item. Ask them to apply the three source questions without any prompting.

  • Pass: Student identifies who made the claim, proposes at least one reason the author might have had for writing it, and names the type of evidence provided (or notes its absence) — unprompted.
  • Fail: Student summarizes the content of the passage instead of evaluating its source; or applies the questions only after being reminded to do so.
  • Failure mode to watch: Student recites the three questions by rote ("Who wrote it, why, what evidence") without actually applying them to the specific passage — performing the checklist without engaging it.

Task 2 · Fallacy in an Unfamiliar Story

Setup: Read aloud a short story the student has not heard before that contains exactly one of the four Tier 3 fallacies embedded in a character's dialogue. Ask: "Is there something wrong with how this character is arguing?"

  • Pass: Student identifies that there is a reasoning error, names the fallacy or describes its pattern accurately, and explains why the argument fails — not just that it "feels wrong."
  • Fail: Student says the argument is fine, or identifies a fallacy incorrectly, or can only say it feels wrong without explanation.
  • Failure mode to watch: Student names the fallacy correctly but misattributes it — e.g., labels a Strawman as "Ad Hominem" because the character seems mean. Naming without pattern recognition.

Task 3 · Complete a Truth Table for a New Rule

Setup: Present a rule the student has not seen before — for example, "The alarm sounds if the door is open AND it is after 9pm." Provide a blank truth table with columns labeled Door and Time. Ask the student to fill in all four rows.

  • Pass: Student fills all four rows correctly and explains why each row produces the output it does.
  • Fail: Student fills in rows by guessing or skips the impossible-seeming combination (e.g., "the door is always closed at night so I didn't include that").
  • Failure mode to watch: Student gets the right answers but cannot explain the logic — has memorized the AND-gate output pattern without understanding what truth tables are for.

Task 4 · Draw an Argument Map Including a Source Box

Setup: Give the student one spoken claim with stated evidence: "You should drink this brand of juice — a nutritionist on TV said it has the most vitamins." Ask them to draw the argument map including the source layer.

  • Pass: Map includes: the claim, the evidence, a source box identifying who the nutritionist is and noting a potential bias (TV advertisement context), and a connector showing the evidence links to the claim.
  • Fail: Map shows only claim and evidence with no source box; or student draws the source box but does not raise any question about it.
  • Failure mode to watch: Student adds a source box that just echoes the evidence ("source: nutritionist said vitamins") without any evaluative layer — treating the source box as a label rather than a question.
Operational Layer · Tier 3

Gating Criteria

What a student must demonstrate — not just complete — before advancing to Tier 4.

Required Demonstrations

  • Applies the three source questions spontaneously to an unfamiliar text, without prompting
  • Identifies at least three of the four Tier 3 fallacies when embedded in new material
  • Completes a truth table for a two-input AND, OR, or NOT rule with full accuracy and explanation
  • Draws an argument map that includes a source layer with at least one evaluative note about source credibility
  • Produces one Thinking Journal entry that connects a trust response ("I automatically believed this because…") to a reasoned evaluation of whether that trust was justified

Literacy & Mathematics — Required Demonstrations

  • Reads an unfamiliar grade-appropriate text and identifies the main claim and two pieces of supporting evidence without prompting
  • Writes a 2–3 paragraph opinion piece with topic sentence, evidence, and closing — planned before written
  • Uses at least 3 Tier 2 academic vocabulary words correctly in written work
  • Explains why 6×7=42 using at least two different representations
  • Solves a division problem by connecting it to a multiplication fact
  • Places a fraction on a number line and explains why it is greater or less than 1/2

What Does Not Count as Mastery

  • Completing source-evaluation worksheets on familiar texts used during instruction
  • Reciting the three source questions without applying them to a specific text
  • Correctly labeling fallacies in examples they have seen before in class
  • Filling in a truth table that was modeled step-by-step alongside them
  • Group argument maps where the source-layer evaluation was contributed by another student

The gating question: Can this student pick up any unfamiliar claim, ask where it came from, and tell me whether they should trust the source — without being told to?

Operational Layer · Tier 3

Adversarial Exercises

Sovereignty requires training against the actual conditions that undermine independent thinking.

Exercise 1 · Trusted Source, Questionable Claim

Setup: Present the student with a claim made by a figure they love and trust — "Your teacher says that this is the best book ever written" or "Your parent says this food is healthy because it's natural." Ask: "Should we believe this? Let's use the three questions."

Most students apply source skepticism easily to obvious bad actors — an advertisement, a stranger, a cartoon villain. This exercise forces the same rigor when the source is beloved. The discomfort of questioning a trusted person is the exact moment this skill is hardest to apply and most necessary.

What this trains: Applying the source protocol uniformly, regardless of emotional attachment to the source — the reflex that makes skepticism real rather than selective.

Exercise 2 · The Popular Book Test

Setup: Choose a claim from a popular, widely-praised book the student knows well. Present it without the book title and ask the student to evaluate it. Then reveal the source. Ask: "Does knowing it came from this book change your answer? Should it?"

This exercise targets the trust halo around familiar, culturally approved sources. Students often reverse their critical evaluation the moment a trusted title is revealed. The goal is to notice that reversal happening and ask whether it is justified.

What this trains: Separating the evaluation of a claim from the prestige of its source — holding the analysis stable even when the source is a cultural authority.

Exercise 3 · False Dichotomy From a Friend

Setup: Role-play a scenario where a friend (played by the teacher) presents a false choice: "You either come to my party or you don't care about our friendship." Ask the student to map the full range of options that the false dichotomy erases.

Children are especially vulnerable to false dichotomies presented by people they care about. Social pressure converts a logical error into an emotional ultimatum. This exercise trains students to recognize the pattern even when refusing it feels costly.

What this trains: Identifying the False Dichotomy fallacy when it arrives with emotional force, not just in abstract examples — and naming the erased middle ground aloud.

Why adversarial training matters at Tier 3: Students this age apply source skepticism readily to strangers and advertisements, but freeze when the source is a parent, a teacher, or a beloved book. The adversarial exercises specifically target that freeze. Skepticism that only works on suspicious-looking sources is not skepticism — it is selective distrust, which is just a different kind of bias.

Embodied Sovereignty · Tier 3

First Practical Skills

Skills introduced as projects and adventures — never as emergency preparation.

What Students Do

  • Three functional knots with real uses (the use is the frame, not the knot name)
  • Fire starting with flint and steel, practitioner present — framed as 'the oldest human technology: you are connecting to 400,000 years of knowledge'
  • Map reading: orienting a paper map to physical terrain, matching landmarks
  • Foraging introduction: 3 regionally specific edible plants confirmed with a practitioner
  • Compass basics: find north, orient to a bearing

The Source Evaluation Connection

  • How do you know this plant is safe to eat? Who confirmed it, and how do they know? This is cognitive Tier 3's source evaluation ('who wrote this and why?') applied to the land
  • The practitioner is a primary source. The field guide is a secondary source. Your own observation is evidence. All three are required. (Elpel, 'Botany in a Day,' 2004)

Assessment

  • Can the student correctly identify 3 edible plants in the field — not from a photo, from the actual plant?
  • Can they orient a map to terrain and point to their current location?
  • Can they tie 3 knots and explain what each is for?

At Tier 3, children enter Sobel's 'exploration stage' — they are developmentally ready to range, map, and understand place as infrastructure. Skills introduced before this stage tend to feel like chores. Introduced now, they feel like adventure. Timing is pedagogy.
