Core Skills · Grade 1

Literacy — Phonics, Word Study & Argument Writing

25 minutes daily. Phonics deepens, writing becomes the reasoning scaffold.

By Grade 1, students move from individual letter-sounds to patterns — vowel-consonant-e words, consonant blends, short versus long vowels. The shift is from "which sound does this letter make?" to "what pattern does this word follow?" Word study replaces rote spelling lists: students sort words by their patterns rather than memorizing them individually. (Bear, D.R., Invernizzi, M., Templeton, S., & Johnston, F., 2012. Words Their Way: Word Study for Phonics, Vocabulary, and Spelling Instruction. Pearson. Word sorting engages deep processing of sound-pattern-meaning connections; sorting is more effective than traditional spelling tests because it produces understanding, not just recall.)

Writing becomes a reasoning scaffold this year. Vygotsky's research establishes that written language is not just recorded thought — it is a tool for thought. The act of writing "I think the bear was scared because the wolf was loud" requires the writer to commit the causal structure to a form that can be inspected, tested, and revised. Writing and reasoning reinforce each other directly. (Vygotsky, L.S., 1934/1986. Thought and Language. MIT Press.)

The Thinking Journal — Scaffolded

The Thinking Journal introduced in Grade 1 requires writing support. In the first semester, students draw and dictate to the teacher who writes their words. In the second semester, students write their own entries using their developing phonics knowledge — invented spelling accepted. By end of Grade 1, entries should include at least one complete sentence. The journal exists to externalize reasoning, not to demonstrate spelling accuracy.

Literacy Gating — End of Grade 1

  • Reads simple decodable words using phonics patterns taught this year (short vowels, consonant blends, vowel-consonant-e)
  • Sorts words by pattern correctly — can articulate what pattern makes words belong together
  • Writes at least one complete "I think X because Y" sentence independently on an unfamiliar topic
  • Thinking Journal contains entries across the year — some drawn, some written
Core Skills · Grade 1

Mathematics — Number Bonds, Place Value & the Number Line

25 minutes daily. Addition and subtraction as composing and decomposing — not as procedures to memorize.

Grade 1 mathematics is defined by a critical transition: from counting (one object at a time) to composing and decomposing numbers (understanding that 7 is 3+4, and also 5+2, and also 1+6). This is the conceptual foundation of all arithmetic. Children who make this transition understanding what they are doing — rather than executing a memorized procedure — are dramatically better equipped for fractions, algebra, and everything that follows. (Clements, D.H. & Sarama, J., 2009. Learning and Teaching Early Math: The Learning Trajectories Approach. Routledge. The most comprehensive research-to-practice synthesis on early mathematics development.)

The CPA progression continues: concrete manipulatives lead, pictorial representations (number bonds, ten frames, number lines) follow, and abstract notation (7 = 3 + 4) comes last. Students who only encounter the abstract notation are working with a symbol whose meaning they cannot reconstruct when they forget the procedure. (Carbonneau, K.J. et al., 2013. Journal of Educational Psychology, 105(2). Meta-analysis: manipulatives improve outcomes when explicitly connected to abstract representations.)

Math anxiety prevention starts here. Boaler, J. (2015). Mathematical Mindsets. Jossey-Bass. All fact fluency practice in this curriculum uses games, patterns, and spaced repetition — never timed tests. The growth mindset framing established in this curriculum ("changing your mind is a sign of thinking, not weakness") applies directly to mathematics: confusion during a math problem means the brain is working on something hard, which is exactly how it gets stronger.

Mathematics Gating — End of Grade 1

  • Can produce all number bond families to 10 — quickly, not by counting from 1
  • Places any number 0–20 accurately on a blank number line and explains why it goes there
  • Builds a two-digit number with base-ten blocks and explains what each digit means
  • Adds and subtracts within 20 using number bonds or ten frames — not finger counting
Phase One · Grade 1

The Three-Part Argument

Every formal logic structure in this curriculum is an elaboration of this.

The Toulmin Model. Predicate logic. Formal debate. These are all sophisticated versions of one simple move: making a claim, giving a reason, and pointing to evidence. Grade 1 is where that move is learned for the first time — in sentences, through stories, and in daily conversation.

The Thinking Journal is introduced this year. It is the earliest version of the metacognitive record that students will maintain through 12th grade — a private space to track what they believe, why they believe it, and what changes their mind.

Core Domain · Reasoning

Claim → Reason → Evidence

The first formal argument structure. Simple enough for a 6-year-old. Powerful enough for a Supreme Court brief.

What Students Practice

  • Claim: 'I think the bear went to sleep because it was winter.'
  • Reason: 'Bears hibernate when it gets cold.'
  • Evidence: 'The book said the forest got very cold and the bear started yawning.'
  • Distinguishing between 'I think' (claim) and 'because' (reason) in speech and writing
  • Recognizing when a reason is missing: 'She said the sky is blue, but she didn't say why she thinks that'
  • The difference between a reason and an example: a reason explains, an example shows

'I think X because Y, and I know this because Z.' If a student can say this sentence clearly and correctly about any topic, they have mastered the most fundamental unit of rational argument.

Core Domain · Emotional Logic

Feelings as the First Variable

The Heatmap practice deepens — feelings are now connected to reasoning chains.

What Students Practice

  • Why a character made a decision — tracing the emotional cause behind an action
  • Two people, same event, different feelings — and both can be right
  • Noticing their own feeling before reacting — the morning Heatmap check-in continues
  • The question: 'Did your feeling change after you found out more?'
  • Writing: 'I felt ___ because ___' — the claim-reason structure applied to emotion
Core Domain · Pattern & Logic

If/Then Enters the Room

Conditional reasoning through games, stories, and daily routine.

What Students Practice

  • If/then through daily rules: 'If it is raining, then we stay inside'
  • If/then in stories: 'If the wolf comes, then the pig will hide'
  • Completing if/then sentences: 'If I eat my lunch, then ___'
  • Finding the pattern in a sequence and identifying the rule that governs it
  • Recognizing when an if/then rule was broken — 'But it didn't happen that way — why not?'
  • Simple algorithms as ordered steps: 'First do this, then do this, then do this'
Core Domain · Visualization

Drawing the Argument

Externalizing reasoning onto paper — seeing the structure of a claim.

What Students Practice

  • Two-box diagrams: CLAIM box → REASON box, connected by an arrow labeled 'because'
  • Adding a third box: EVIDENCE — 'I know this because ___'
  • Before/after reasoning strips: the situation, the action, the result
  • Simple decision trees: 'If it rains → stay inside. If it's sunny → go outside'
  • Character emotion timelines — extending the Kindergarten feeling map
The Metacognitive Thread

The Thinking Journal — Year One

Private. Low-stakes. The beginning of a lifelong practice.

The Thinking Journal is introduced in Grade 1 as a simple notebook where students write or draw one thing they believed, one thing they noticed, or one question they had. It is never graded. It is never read aloud without the student's permission. It is private.

Weekly Prompts (Age-Appropriate)

  • 'Draw something you learned this week that surprised you'
  • 'Write: I used to think ___, but now I think ___'
  • 'Draw a time this week when you felt a strong feeling. What was it? What caused it?'
  • 'Did you change your mind about anything this week? Draw it or write it.'

Changing your mind is a sign of thinking, not weakness. This is stated explicitly to students in Grade 1 — and repeated every year through Grade 12.

Operational Layer · Grade 1

Evaluation Primitives

Each capability is only real when it can be demonstrated on an unfamiliar problem under no-hint conditions.

Task 1 · Claim-Reason-Evidence Construction

Setup: Give the student an unfamiliar topic (not one discussed in class this year). Ask: "What do you think about this? Tell me why." Listen for the full three-part structure.

  • Pass: Student produces a complete "I think X because Y, and I know this because Z" — or its spoken equivalent — without being prompted to add each component.
  • Fail: Student states a claim and stops. Gives a reason when prompted but doesn't do it spontaneously. States evidence that is actually a repetition of the claim.
  • Failure mode to watch: Students who have memorized the sentence template but can't apply it to a new context — they repeat the phrase correctly but fill it with "I don't know" or circular content.

Task 2 · Gap Detection

Setup: Read the student a short statement: a claim with no reason given, stated confidently. Ask: "Is anything missing from what they said?"

  • Pass: Student identifies that no reason was given — "they said what they think but not why."
  • Fail: Student says "no, that sounds right" because it was stated confidently. Student says "I don't know" without attempting to evaluate the structure.
  • Failure mode to watch: Students who accept confident delivery as a substitute for reasoning — the most important failure mode at this age.

Task 3 · If/Then on a New Scenario

Setup: Give an unfamiliar if/then scenario ("If you leave ice outside in summer, then ___"). Ask the student to complete it and explain the rule.

  • Pass: Correctly completes the if/then and can state the rule behind it in their own words.
  • Fail: Guesses a random completion. Completes it correctly but cannot explain why (pattern matching without reasoning).
  • Failure mode to watch: Students who complete the pattern visually or from prior knowledge but can't articulate the conditional structure — completion ≠ understanding of conditionality.

Task 4 · Argument Map Drawing

Setup: Give the student a simple argument they just heard. Ask them to draw it as three connected boxes.

  • Pass: Correctly labels the three boxes (Claim, Reason, Evidence or their own words for each) and connects them with directional arrows.
  • Fail: Draws boxes but doesn't connect them, or reverses the direction, or puts all content in one box.
  • Failure mode to watch: Students who copy the visual format without understanding what goes in each box — label matching rather than structural understanding.
Operational Layer · Grade 1

Gating Criteria

What a student must demonstrate — not just practice — before advancing to Grade 2.

Required Demonstrations

  • Produces a complete Claim → Reason → Evidence structure spontaneously on an unfamiliar topic
  • Identifies a missing reason in a confident-but-incomplete argument
  • Completes and explains an unfamiliar if/then rule
  • Draws a three-box argument map with correct labels and directed arrows
  • Has maintained the Thinking Journal through the year — existence verifiable, content private
  • Can say "I used to think ___, but now I think ___" about something from this year

Literacy & Mathematics — Required Demonstrations

  • Reads a simple decodable sentence using phonics patterns taught this year
  • Sorts a set of words by pattern and articulates the rule that groups them
  • Writes one "I think X because Y" sentence independently on an unfamiliar topic
  • Produces all number bond families to 10 without counting from 1
  • Places any number 0–20 on a blank number line and explains its position
  • Builds a two-digit number with base-ten blocks and explains what each digit represents

What Does Not Count as Mastery

  • Reciting "I think X because Y" as a memorized phrase without producing it spontaneously
  • Completing familiar argument examples from class — transfer to new topics is the standard
  • Correct if/then completions without being able to explain the rule
  • Drawing argument maps for arguments the teacher gave them, not ones they generated

The gating question: Can this student notice when a reason is missing — even when the speaker sounds confident?

Operational Layer · Grade 1

Adversarial Exercises

One exercise — gentle, age-appropriate. The goal is noticing, not defense.

Exercise 1 · The Confident Claim

Setup: A puppet, story character, or the teacher states a claim loudly and confidently with no reason: "The red team always wins. That's just how it is." Then pause. Ask: "What do you notice?"

The point is not to argue with the confident claim. The point is to notice that confidence is not a reason. A loud voice, a certain tone, an authoritative posture — none of these are evidence. The student's job is only to notice the missing piece: "They didn't say why."

This should be repeated with different speakers across the year — the teacher, a popular character, a peer. The authority of the speaker should not change whether the student notices the missing reason.

What this trains: Decoupling speaker confidence from argument quality. This is the earliest form of the skill that, by Grade 6, becomes the full Internal Heatmap protocol.

Why this matters at Grade 1: The most durable obstacle to independent reasoning is not malice — it is deference to confident delivery. Six-year-olds are already learning to read authority signals. The goal here is not to make students argumentative but to preserve the "why?" reflex they already have naturally, before school teaches them to suppress it.

Embodied Sovereignty · Grade 1

Observation Leads to Inference

The same move as Claim → Reason → Evidence, applied to the natural world.

What Students Do

  • Sit-spot practice introduced — 10 minutes in one outdoor location, same spot each week, drawing what has changed
  • Animal tracking basics: reading disturbance in environment (bent grass, footprints, disturbed soil)
  • Growing one crop from seed to harvest
  • Basic weather pattern observation — what does the sky look like before rain?

The Cognitive Connection

  • Sit-spot is the embodied version of the Thinking Journal. Both ask the student to observe, record, and notice change over time
  • A student who watches the same patch of ground for a semester and sees what changes has practiced sustained attention and inference — the same capacities the cognitive curriculum builds with argument structure. (Young, Haas & McGown, 'Coyote's Guide to Connecting with Nature,' 2010)

Assessment

  • Can the student make a claim about something they observed outside and give evidence from direct observation? ('I think it rained last night because the soil is wet and the worms are on the surface.')
  • Can they identify one animal by its tracks or sign?

The sit-spot is not a quiet time activity. It is a structured observation practice. Students should have a designated spot they return to repeatedly — novelty is not the goal, attention to change is.
