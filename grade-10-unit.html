Tier 10
Should AI Be Allowed to Assist in Medical Diagnosis?

Tier 10 is the last year of discovery before deep specialization locks in. The medical AI question is chosen because it combines empirical uncertainty (how accurate is the AI?), ethical complexity (who is responsible when the AI is wrong?), technical implementation (how does a diagnostic algorithm actually work?), and regulatory governance (who should decide?). Each track goes meaningfully deeper than Tier 9 — apprenticeships and research projects begin this year.

Unit Design · Tier 10

What This Unit Builds

Five weeks at the intersection of technology, ethics, medicine, and governance — with each track going one level deeper than Tier 9.

Tier 10's defining move is track depth. Where Tier 9 required breadth across four perspectives, Tier 10 requires genuine depth in the student's primary track — while maintaining enough cross-track awareness to understand where tensions with other tracks arise. The medical AI question is designed to produce genuine cross-track tension: the systems/engineering track may find the AI's diagnostic accuracy compelling; the civic track may find the liability and consent questions unresolved; the academic track may find the clinical evidence insufficient; the vocational track may find the implementation in real medical settings impractical.

The ethical frameworks introduced this year — utilitarianism and deontological ethics — are not abstract philosophy exercises. They are applied to the specific decision of whether and how to deploy diagnostic AI, and the genuine tension between them is the unit's most important intellectual content.

What you will see by Week 5: Students who can apply both a utilitarian AND a deontological analysis to the same policy question and explain why they reach different conclusions. Students who can read a simplified clinical study abstract and evaluate: sensitivity, specificity, and the base rate problem in diagnostic accuracy. Students who produce a track-depth deliverable — a bioethics argument, a device audit, an algorithm evaluation, or a regulatory brief — at near-professional quality.

Week 1 · Tier 10 Unit · All Tracks

What Does AI Actually Do in Medicine?

Technical baseline — all tracks establish shared understanding of how diagnostic AI systems work before ethical and policy analysis begins.

LensDaily FocusWhat All Students DoReasoning Connection
Grammar & WritingHow diagnostic AI worksStudents read a simplified technical overview of how a diagnostic AI system works: training data, pattern recognition, output (probability score, not a diagnosis), integration into clinical workflow. Write: "What is the AI actually doing when it 'diagnoses'? What is it not doing? What does the clinician still decide?"Most public discourse about diagnostic AI conflates pattern recognition (what AI does) with clinical judgment (what physicians do). Students who understand the actual function of AI in the diagnostic workflow can evaluate claims about AI replacing vs. assisting doctors with precision rather than generality.
MathematicsSensitivity, specificity, and base rateStudents apply the Tier 8 Bayes' theorem framework to diagnostic AI: a cancer detection AI has 95% sensitivity (true positive rate) and 90% specificity (true negative rate). In a population where 1% have the cancer: P(cancer | positive test) = (0.95 × 0.01) / ((0.95 × 0.01) + (0.10 × 0.99)) ≈ 8.7%. Students verify: even a very accurate test produces mostly false positives in a low-prevalence population. "What does this mean for AI deployment in screening vs. high-risk populations?"The base rate calculation shows that diagnostic accuracy is population-dependent — the same AI that performs well in a high-risk population performs poorly as a general screening tool because false positives dominate in low-prevalence settings. This is the mathematical core of the responsible deployment question.
VisualizationDiagnostic AI decision treeStudents draw the clinical decision pathway with AI: patient presents → AI analyzes image/data → AI outputs probability score → physician reviews score + clinical picture → physician makes diagnosis → treatment recommended. Mark at each step: what is automated? What requires human judgment? Where can errors occur? What feedback loops exist?Mapping the full decision pathway before evaluating the technology is a systems thinking practice: understanding the system before proposing changes to it. Students who have drawn this diagram understand that AI deployment is not a binary "AI decides vs. physician decides" but a complex integration question.
Project WorkThree real deploymentsStudents research three actual diagnostic AI deployments: FDA-approved AI for diabetic retinopathy screening, AI for radiology image analysis, AI for sepsis early warning. For each: what clinical problem does it address? What does the published evidence show about accuracy? What is the deployment context (screening vs. diagnosis, high vs. low prevalence)?Real deployments differ from theoretical cases in important ways: the evidence is messier, the implementation challenges are real, and the regulatory and liability landscape is specific. Students who research actual deployments are reasoning about real technology, not thought experiments.
Argument/ReasoningInitial ethical intuitionStudents write their initial ethical intuition: "I think AI should / should not be allowed to assist in medical diagnosis because ___." Then: "Is my reason primarily about consequences (what outcomes would this produce?) or principles (what rights or duties are at stake regardless of outcomes?)?" This distinction sets up the Week 2 ethical framework analysis.Distinguishing consequentialist from deontological reasoning in one's own initial intuitions is the first step in applying formal ethical frameworks. Students who notice that their intuition is consequence-based or principle-based can then see which framework their argument naturally uses — and can check whether it survives the challenge from the other framework.

By end of Week 1

  • Can explain what a diagnostic AI system actually does — and what it does not do
  • Has calculated sensitivity/specificity/base rate for a diagnostic AI scenario
  • Has drawn a complete clinical decision pathway with AI integration
  • Has identified whether their initial intuition is consequence-based or principle-based
Week 2 · Tier 10 Unit · All Tracks

Ethical Frameworks — Utilitarianism and Deontology Applied

Both frameworks applied to the same question — and why they reach genuinely different conclusions.

LensDaily FocusWhat All Students DoReasoning Connection
Grammar & WritingTwo frameworks, one questionStudents write two structured analyses of the same AI deployment question: (1) Utilitarian: What are the aggregate consequences? If AI deployment saves X lives per year and causes Y adverse outcomes, what does the utilitarian calculation say? (2) Deontological: Does the patient have a right to know they are being diagnosed with AI assistance? Does the physician have a duty to inform? Can these duties be waived by aggregate benefit?The frameworks genuinely diverge on this question. Utilitarianism may support deployment if the net lives saved is positive; deontology may oppose it if it violates informed consent duties regardless of outcomes. Students who see this divergence understand why ethical disagreements persist among serious, intelligent people — they are using different frameworks that prioritize different values.
MathematicsUtilitarian calculation — aggregate outcomesStudents build a simplified utilitarian calculation: AI deployment in 1,000 hospitals, diagnosing 500 patients per year each = 500,000 diagnoses/year. If AI improves accuracy by 5% (25,000 more correct diagnoses), and if each correct diagnosis improves outcomes by 0.2 QALYs (quality-adjusted life years), total benefit = 5,000 QALYs. Compare to costs and risks. "Is this calculation sufficient to justify deployment? What does it miss?"The QALY calculation is a real tool in health economics and utilitarian policy analysis. Students who build this calculation understand both its power (it makes tradeoffs explicit and comparable) and its limits (it treats all QALYs as equivalent regardless of whose they are, which raises distributive justice concerns).
VisualizationFramework comparison matrixStudents build a matrix: rows = key ethical questions (Is deployment justified? Is informed consent required? Who bears responsibility for errors? Should access be universal?), columns = utilitarian answer / deontological answer / student's own answer. "Where do the frameworks agree? Where do they genuinely diverge?"The matrix makes the frameworks' genuine agreements and disagreements visible in a single view. Students who complete this matrix cannot claim that "ethics" gives a single answer to the deployment question — it gives multiple answers that depend on which framework you apply and why.
Project WorkTrack-specific ethical angle beginsStudents begin track-specific work: Academic track reads a bioethics journal article on AI and informed consent. Vocational track researches medical device liability standards. Systems track investigates algorithm audit requirements. Civic track analyzes FDA regulatory framework for AI/ML-based Software as a Medical Device (SaMD). Each produces a preliminary track deliverable outline.Track-specific ethical engagement begins in Week 2 so that tracks have three weeks to develop depth before the Week 5 synthesis. The ethical frameworks from this week are the shared foundation; the track-specific application is the depth each student develops in their own discipline.
Argument/ReasoningFramework stress testStudents identify the strongest objection to each framework in this context: "The best objection to the utilitarian approach is ___. The best objection to the deontological approach is ___." Then: "Does either framework, when taken alone, give a satisfactory answer? What would a more complete ethical analysis look like?"Stress-testing frameworks — finding their strongest objections — is the meta-ethical skill that prevents framework worship. No single ethical framework answers all questions satisfactorily. Students who understand this are equipped to use frameworks as tools rather than as authorities.

By end of Week 2

  • Has written two structured ethical analyses — one utilitarian, one deontological — of the same question
  • Has built a simplified QALY calculation and identified its limits
  • Has completed the framework comparison matrix
  • Has begun track-specific work with a preliminary deliverable outline
Week 3 · Tier 10 Unit · Track Depth Begins

Reading a Clinical Study — and Going Deep by Track

All tracks read the same clinical study this week. Then each track analyzes it through their distinctive lens.

TrackStudy Analysis FocusTrack-Specific Deep WorkDeliverable Progress
All Tracks (shared)Clinical study evaluationStudents read a simplified version of a real clinical study on diagnostic AI performance (e.g., Esteva et al. 2017 on dermatology AI, or Google Health's diabetic retinopathy AI). Apply the Tier 8 study evaluation rubric: sample size, study design, effect size, limitations stated by authors, replication status. Write: "What does this study prove? What does it not prove? Should it be sufficient to justify clinical deployment?"The shared study evaluation ensures all tracks are working from the same evidentiary base before diverging into track-specific analysis — the same structure as Tier 9's Week 1.
Academic TrackBioethics deep-diveStudents research the informed consent doctrine in medicine, the Belmont Report's three principles (respect for persons, beneficence, justice), and how each applies to AI-assisted diagnosis. Draft a bioethics argument paper: "Does AI-assisted diagnosis require a new informed consent framework?" Minimum 800 words, with three academic citations.Bioethics argument paper — 800+ words with academic citations. First time in the curriculum that this level of academic writing is required.
Vocational TrackMedical device standardsStudents research FDA Class II medical device requirements and how AI/ML systems are regulated under Software as a Medical Device (SaMD) guidance. Analyze: what testing is required before deployment? What post-market surveillance is required? What happens when the AI is updated and the training data changes? Produce a compliance checklist for a hypothetical diagnostic AI device.Medical device compliance checklist — a practical document that demonstrates understanding of regulatory requirements for real AI deployment.
Systems TrackAlgorithm auditStudents investigate algorithmic auditing: what does it mean to audit an AI system for bias? Students analyze a simplified case where a diagnostic AI trained primarily on data from one demographic performs worse on another demographic. Build an audit framework: what tests would reveal performance disparities? What would remediation require? Produce an algorithm audit protocol outline.Algorithm audit protocol — a technical document that demonstrates understanding of how to evaluate AI systems for performance disparities across populations.
Civic TrackRegulatory briefStudents analyze the FDA's 2021 AI/ML Action Plan and the EU AI Act's provisions for high-risk AI in healthcare. Compare: what does each regulatory framework require? What do patients have a right to know under each? Which framework better protects patient interests? Produce a regulatory comparison brief.Regulatory comparison brief — a policy document comparing two real regulatory frameworks for medical AI.

By end of Week 3

  • Has evaluated the shared clinical study using the Tier 8 rubric
  • Has produced a substantial track-specific deliverable draft (800+ words for academic; equivalent depth for other tracks)
  • Has identified the specific cross-track tension their track's analysis creates with at least one other track
Week 4 · Tier 10 Unit · Track Deliverables

Completing Track Deliverables — Peer Review Across Tracks

Students complete their track deliverables and receive peer review from a student in a different track.

LensDaily FocusWhat Students DoReasoning Connection
Grammar & WritingFinal track deliverableStudents complete and polish their track deliverable: bioethics paper (academic), compliance checklist with analysis (vocational), algorithm audit protocol (systems), regulatory comparison brief (civic). Each deliverable is evaluated against track-appropriate standards — not a single common rubric.Track-appropriate evaluation standards are a Tier 10 innovation: students are now evaluated against the standards of their chosen discipline, not a generic school assignment rubric. A bioethics paper is evaluated differently from an algorithm audit — as they would be in professional contexts.
MathematicsQuantitative component of deliverableEvery track's deliverable must include a quantitative component: academic (effect size from the clinical study cited in the argument), vocational (cost estimate for compliance requirements), systems (demographic performance disparity calculation), civic (comparison of regulatory compliance burden in $ or hours). Numbers must be specific and sourced.Every track at Tier 10 must engage quantitative evidence — this is not just the mathematics track's responsibility. A bioethics paper without engagement with the clinical evidence it evaluates, and a regulatory brief without comparison of compliance costs, are professionally incomplete.
VisualizationCross-track peer reviewStudents exchange drafts with a student from a different track. Review task: (1) What does this track's perspective reveal that mine misses? (2) What does my track's perspective reveal that this one misses? (3) Where would these two perspectives conflict in the real world of AI deployment policy? Write a 200-word cross-track review.Cross-track peer review surfaces the genuine professional tensions that emerge when different disciplines engage the same problem. A regulatory brief that assumes clinical accuracy data the algorithm audit reveals is biased; a bioethics argument that assumes consent procedures the vocational track knows are impractical in clinical workflows. The tensions are real and productive.
Project WorkCross-track tension statementStudents write a "cross-track tension statement": "From my track's perspective, the most important unresolved tension with [another specific track]'s perspective is ___. We would need to resolve this tension before any deployment policy could be complete because ___."Naming the specific unresolved tension between two tracks is more intellectually productive than claiming a synthesis that doesn't exist. Some tensions between ethical frameworks and technical constraints are genuine and cannot be resolved — acknowledging this is intellectual honesty at Band III level.
Argument/ReasoningWhat changes your mind?Students revisit Week 1's initial intuition statement. Write: "My initial intuition was ___. After five weeks of research, my position is ___. The evidence or argument that most changed my thinking was ___. If I am honest, is there anything I still believe based on intuition rather than evidence — and what would it take to test that intuition?"The belief revision question at Tier 10 is more demanding than earlier grades: students are asked to identify intuitions that may not be fully evidence-based and specify what would test them. This is the highest-level intellectual honesty exercise in Band III — and appropriate for students who are approaching genuine expertise in their chosen track.

By end of Week 4

  • Has completed and polished the track deliverable including a quantitative component
  • Has reviewed a peer from a different track and written a 200-word cross-track review
  • Has written a cross-track tension statement identifying an unresolved conflict between two tracks
  • Has updated their initial intuition statement with honest acknowledgment of remaining intuition-based beliefs
Week 5 · Tier 10 Unit · All Tracks

Cross-Track Synthesis and Tension

Track deliverables presented. Tensions named. A policy recommendation built at the intersection of all four perspectives — with unresolved tensions acknowledged honestly.

LensDaily FocusWhat All Students DoReasoning Connection
Grammar & WritingTrack presentationEach track presents a 7-minute summary of their deliverable. Audience from other tracks asks: "What does your analysis require that you assumed my track would provide? What have you learned that changes how you think my track's approach needs to work?" This is a professional consultation structure — tracks must inform each other.A professional consultation structure — where each discipline presents its findings and explicitly asks what it needs from other disciplines — is the real-world model of interdisciplinary work. Tier 10 introduces this structure before students specialize deeply in Tier 11.
MathematicsIntegrated deployment recommendationAll tracks work together to produce a single quantitative deployment recommendation: given the clinical evidence (academic), the regulatory requirements (civic), the compliance costs (vocational), and the algorithmic bias findings (systems), what specific deployment context is supported by the combined evidence? "Deploy in high-risk populations, with demographic audit required before population expansion, at an estimated per-facility compliance cost of $___."A specific, quantified deployment recommendation that integrates four disciplinary perspectives is more actionable and more honestly uncertain than a generic "deploy with caution" recommendation. Specificity forces integration — vagueness allows each track to ignore the others' constraints.
VisualizationTension mapStudents build a tension map: four tracks at corners, arrows between any pair that has a genuine unresolved tension identified in Week 4. Label each arrow with the specific tension. Center of the map: what would need to be true for all tensions to be resolved? Is that state achievable?A tension map that acknowledges genuine unresolved conflicts between disciplinary perspectives is more intellectually honest than a synthesis diagram that implies all tensions were resolved. Some tensions in real interdisciplinary work are permanent — they reflect genuine value differences, not analytical errors.
Project WorkTier 10 track declarationStudents write a formal track declaration: "For Tier 11 and 12, my primary track is ___ because ___. The cross-track fluency I most need to maintain is ___. The question I most want to answer through deep work in my track is ___." This is the student's statement of academic direction — not binding, but serious.The track declaration formalizes the self-knowledge component of Tier 10's "deepening focus" theme. Students who can articulate why they are choosing a track, what they still need from other tracks, and what question drives them have done the metacognitive work that makes specialization purposeful rather than arbitrary.
Argument/ReasoningThinking Journal — Band III synthesis to dateThinking Journal: "The most important thing I understand about ethical reasoning that I did not before Tier 10 is ___. The track I feel most drawn to and why I think that is honest rather than just comfortable ___. The question I could not answer about the medical AI problem — and why it could not be answered with the evidence we have — is ___."Identifying what cannot be answered with current evidence is the Band III synthesis of the "appropriate confidence" habit built since Tier 3. Students who know what the evidence cannot settle — and why — have achieved a level of epistemological sophistication appropriate to deep specialization.

By end of Week 5

  • Has presented track deliverable in a professional consultation format
  • Has contributed to an integrated, specific deployment recommendation
  • Has built a tension map with labeled unresolved cross-track conflicts
  • Has written a formal track declaration for Tiers 11–12
Unit Assessment · Tier 10

What Mastery Looks Like

Track Deliverable — Standard

  • Academic track paper: Clear thesis, three supporting claims with academic citations, engagement with the strongest counterargument, appropriate epistemic hedging, and an honest limitations section. Must engage the clinical study evidence quantitatively.
  • Vocational compliance checklist: Complete coverage of FDA SaMD requirements, realistic cost estimates with sources, and identification of at least two points where the regulatory framework is ambiguous or silent.
  • Systems algorithm audit protocol: Systematic framework covering performance metrics, demographic stratification, continuous monitoring, and remediation triggers — with a worked example showing how the protocol applies to a specific AI system.
  • Civic regulatory brief: Accurate summary of both frameworks, specific comparison on at least four dimensions, patient rights analysis, and a recommendation with honest acknowledgment of the limits of regulatory solutions.
Gating Connection · Tier 10

How This Unit Connects to Gating Criteria

Gating Criteria Addressed

  • Applies both utilitarian and deontological frameworks to a real policy question and explains why they diverge — Week 2's structured analysis and stress test.
  • Evaluates a clinical study for sensitivity, specificity, and base rate implications — Week 1's diagnostic AI calculation and Week 3's shared study evaluation.
  • Produces a track-depth deliverable at near-professional standard — Week 4's polished track deliverable, evaluated against track-appropriate standards.
  • Identifies genuine unresolved tensions between disciplinary perspectives — Week 4's cross-track tension statement and Week 5's tension map.

The Tier 10 gating question: Has this student produced work in their chosen track that reflects genuine depth — not just broad engagement — and can they articulate the specific question they want to pursue through deep specialization in Tiers 11 and 12?
