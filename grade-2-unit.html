Does This Cereal Box Tell the Truth About Being Healthy?

Every child has held a cereal box and seen the claims on it. This unit uses that familiarity to build the most important distinction in critical thinking: the difference between what can be verified and what is merely an opinion. The cereal box is a laboratory for spotting weasel words, misleading numbers, and authority appeals — all before the children know those terms by name.

Unit Design · Grade 2

What This Unit Builds

Five weeks of examining one real artifact — the cereal box — through increasingly critical eyes.

Two cereal boxes are chosen at the start of the unit — one marketed to children with athletic or sports imagery, one marketed as a family product. The boxes are the central artifact for all five weeks. Students do not read about advertising; they read the actual boxes. The nutrition label, the health claims, the imagery, the ingredient list — all of it is real evidence that students can handle and examine.

The unit's three fallacies — appeal to authority (a sports star says it's good), misleading statistics (30% of your daily value of one vitamin), and weasel words ("part of a healthy breakfast") — are not named as fallacies in Week 1. They are first encountered as puzzles. "This says it's healthy. Does that mean it's healthy? How would you check?" The names come later, after the children have already understood the concept.

What you will see by Week 5: Students who look at any advertising claim and ask "can that be verified or is that just an opinion?" Students who notice the phrase "part of a healthy breakfast" and ask what the other parts are. Students who point out that an athlete saying something is healthy does not make it healthy. These habits are more important than knowing the word "fallacy."

Week 1 · Grade 2 Unit

What Does "Healthy" Actually Mean?

Before evaluating the cereal, students must be able to define the standard — what would make something healthy?

LensDaily FocusWhat Students DoReasoning Connection
Literacy & WritingFact or opinion?Teacher reads pairs of sentences: "Apples are fruit." / "Apples are the best fruit." "This cereal has 12g of sugar per serving." / "This cereal is a great breakfast choice." Students sort each sentence: can it be checked (fact) or is it a preference (opinion)? Write one new example of each type.The fact/opinion distinction is Grade 2's foundational move. It is the first formal tool for evaluating whether a claim can be verified — the prerequisite for all source evaluation work in Grade 3 and beyond.
MathematicsWhat numbers meanLook at the nutrition label. Students read: serving size, calories, grams of sugar, % daily value of vitamins. Teacher asks: "What does 12g of sugar mean? Is that a lot or a little? How would you know?" Students compare to a known quantity: one teaspoon of sugar = 4g. So 12g = 3 teaspoons.Numbers without context are meaningless. Teaching students to ask "compared to what?" before accepting a numerical claim as evidence is one of the most durable critical thinking habits in this curriculum.
VisualizationBox anatomy mapStudents receive a printed image of the cereal box. They label different sections: the health claim area, the nutrition label, the ingredient list, the imagery (sports star, cartoon character). Color code: green = verifiable information, yellow = claim, red = opinion or image.Mapping the box as an artifact makes visible the different rhetorical zones on the package — some designed to inform, some designed to persuade. Students learn to read the box as a designed object, not just a container.
Project WorkDefine "healthy" togetherClass constructs a definition of "healthy food": what properties would a genuinely healthy food have? Students contribute ideas. Teacher steers toward verifiable criteria: low added sugar, contains vitamins and minerals, provides sustained energy. Post the definition on the wall — it will be the standard used to evaluate the cereal all unit.Establishing a standard before evaluating against it is a critical logical move. Without an agreed definition of "healthy," the question "is this cereal healthy?" has no answer. This is the earliest version of defining terms before making claims.
Argument/ReasoningThe cereal's claimRead the cereal box's health claim aloud: "Part of a smart, healthy breakfast!" Ask: "Is that a fact or an opinion? Can it be checked? What would you need to know to check it?" Students respond — most will notice the word "part" and ask what the other parts are. Surface: the sentence is carefully constructed to avoid being checkable.Weasel words are words that make a claim appear to say something specific while actually saying nothing verifiable. "Part of" is a classic example. First encounter: students notice it independently, before the teacher names it.

By end of Week 1

  • Can sort a list of sentences into "fact" and "opinion" with at least 80% accuracy
  • Can convert grams of sugar into teaspoons and explain what that comparison means
  • Has labeled the box anatomy map with color codes
  • Has noticed and questioned at least one weasel phrase on the cereal box
Week 2 · Grade 2 Unit

What Does the Label Say vs. What the Box Says?

The nutrition label is verifiable. The front-of-box claims are not. Students learn to use the label as a check on the claims.

LensDaily FocusWhat Students DoReasoning Connection
Literacy & WritingCompare two textsStudents read the nutrition label (back of box) and one health claim (front of box). Write: "The box says ___ but the label says ___." Where they contradict: highlight in red. Where the label supports the claim: highlight in green. Most claims will have no label support at all.Text comparison — reading two documents about the same thing and looking for agreement and contradiction — is a foundational source evaluation skill. Students practice it here on two parts of the same physical object.
MathematicsPercent daily valueThe label says "25% daily value of Vitamin C." Students ask: what is 25% of a whole? If 100% is everything I need, what is 25%? Teacher uses a simple bar model: a rectangle divided into four parts — one part is 25%. Is 25% of one vitamin enough? What about the other 75%? What about all the other vitamins not listed?Percentages as a persuasion tool: 25% sounds like a lot, but it means the cereal provides one-quarter of one nutrient. Students learn to evaluate what a number actually claims before being impressed by it.
VisualizationTwo-box comparison chartSide-by-side comparison: Cereal A vs. Cereal B. Columns: sugar (g), fiber (g), vitamins (% DV), price. Students fill in the chart from the actual labels. "Which cereal is healthier by your definition? Use the chart as your evidence."A comparison chart forces students to use specific evidence rather than impressions. "This one seems healthier" is not an argument. "This one has 6g less sugar and 3g more fiber" is.
Project WorkIngredient list analysisStudents read the ingredient lists. Ingredients are listed in order of weight — the first ingredient is the most plentiful. "What is the first ingredient in each cereal? What does that tell you?" Students circle the first three ingredients and evaluate them against the class definition of "healthy."Understanding that ingredient order = quantity order is a piece of label literacy that transforms how students read food packaging for the rest of their lives. It is also a lesson in how to read any ranked or ordered list.
Argument/ReasoningMisleading statisticsTeacher provides a made-up example: "SparkCereal now has 50% MORE iron than before!" Students ask: "50% more than what? If it had 2mg before, now it has 3mg — is that meaningful?" The point: percent change without a baseline is uninformative. Students find an example of a misleading statistic on the real cereal box.Misleading statistics are facts that are technically true but designed to create a false impression. The 50% more example is the first explicit introduction to this rhetorical technique — Grade 4 will formalize it as correlation vs. causation misuse.

By end of Week 2

  • Has completed a two-cereal comparison chart using label data
  • Can explain what "25% daily value" means and why it might be misleading
  • Can identify the first ingredient in a product and explain what that means
  • Has found and explained one misleading statistic on the cereal box
Week 3 · Grade 2 Unit

Who Is Talking and Why Should We Trust Them?

The sports star endorsement. Why does having a famous person say something make us believe it?

LensDaily FocusWhat Students DoReasoning Connection
Literacy & WritingWho said it?Students examine the athlete or cartoon character on the cereal box. Write: "The box uses ___ to make you think ___. But is ___ an expert on nutrition? What are they actually an expert in?" Students draw a T-chart: "What they're famous for" vs. "Why they would know about nutrition."Appeal to authority is using someone's fame or status in one domain as evidence for a claim in a different domain. An athlete is an expert on athleticism. They are not necessarily an expert on nutrition science. This distinction — domain expertise — is one of the most practically useful critical thinking concepts.
MathematicsCounting persuasion techniquesStudents count: how many health claims are on the front of box? How many images of energetic/happy people? How many colors that suggest health (green, blue)? How many verifiable facts? Students create a tally. Typical result: 8 persuasion elements, 1 verifiable fact.Quantifying the ratio of persuasion to information is a powerful exercise. Students who can say "there are eight things trying to make me feel good about this cereal and only one thing I can check" have a more sophisticated understanding of advertising than most adults.
VisualizationRedesign the box — honest versionStudents redesign the front of one cereal box using only verifiable facts. No opinions, no weasel words, no celebrity endorsements — only things that can be checked from the label. Compare the honest version to the original. "What did you have to remove? Why?"Redesigning forces students to identify and remove every non-factual element. What remains is the verifiable core. The exercise is also creative — students discover that honest packaging looks very different from persuasive packaging.
Project WorkExpert vs. famous personTeacher presents two "endorsements" of the cereal: one from a registered dietitian who has studied the product, one from a celebrity athlete. Students discuss: "Which endorsement is stronger evidence? Why?" Students write one sentence explaining their choice.This is the explicit introduction to domain expertise as a criterion for authority. An expert's endorsement is better evidence than a celebrity's — not because the expert is more famous, but because their expertise is relevant to the claim.
Argument/ReasoningAuthority fallacy namedTeacher names the appeal to authority fallacy: "Using a famous person's opinion as if it were evidence, when their fame is in a different area." Students generate their own examples: "A famous singer says this medicine is good." "A professional athlete says this school is the best." Students test each: is the person an expert in that area?Naming the fallacy after students have already understood the concept (from the week's activities) makes the name a useful shorthand, not an abstract definition to memorize. The order matters: concept first, label second.

By end of Week 3

  • Can explain what the appeal to authority fallacy is in their own words
  • Has redesigned a cereal box using only verifiable facts
  • Can distinguish between expert testimony and celebrity endorsement
  • Has generated an original example of the appeal to authority fallacy
Week 4 · Grade 2 Unit

What Tricks Are Being Used to Change Our Mind?

Weasel words named. Students can now identify three persuasion techniques by name and find them independently.

LensDaily FocusWhat Students DoReasoning Connection
Literacy & WritingWeasel word huntStudents receive a list of common weasel words: "part of," "may help," "up to," "some people say," "as part of a healthy lifestyle." They search the cereal boxes AND two printed advertisements for these phrases. Tally: how many weasel words found per page of text?A weasel word is one that appears to make a specific claim while being technically non-verifiable. "May help reduce cholesterol" means "sometimes in some people this might have some effect" — legally defensible but practically meaningless. Students who can spot weasel words read all advertising differently.
MathematicsServing size manipulationBoth cereals list nutritional data per serving. But their serving sizes differ: one is 28g, one is 40g. Students recalculate: "How does cereal A's sugar compare to cereal B's if we use the same serving size?" Recalculate all numbers to a common 40g serving. The comparison looks different after adjustment.Incomparable denominators are a form of misleading presentation. Serving sizes are deliberately set to make products appear comparable or favorable. Students who recalculate to a common basis have applied the mathematical version of "control for variables."
VisualizationPersuasion technique mapStudents create a reference card: three columns listing the three techniques studied (weasel words, misleading statistics, appeal to authority), with a definition, an example from the cereal box, and a test question: "How would you check whether this is evidence or persuasion?"The reference card builds a personal taxonomy of persuasion techniques — a tool students will carry into Grade 3 and beyond. Taxonomy building is the precursor to formal fallacy recognition in Grades 6–8.
Project WorkAdvertising auditStudents bring in (or teacher provides) three other advertisements not related to cereal. Students apply their reference card: identify which persuasion techniques appear. Report: "This ad uses ___ technique because ___."Transfer across domains: students move from analyzing cereal boxes to analyzing other advertisements. The techniques are the same; the domain changes. This is the same transfer test as Grade 1's unfamiliar plant photograph.
Argument/ReasoningCan this claim be checked?Final checklist practice: students ask three questions about any claim: (1) Is this a fact or an opinion? (2) Who is saying it and are they an expert in this area? (3) Is there verifiable evidence, or just weasel language? Apply to five new claims on a teacher-prepared worksheet.The three-question checklist is the students' first portable evaluation tool — a framework they can apply independently. It is the Grade 2 version of what becomes the Internal Heatmap in Grade 6 and the full source evaluation protocol in Grade 3.

By end of Week 4

  • Can identify all three persuasion techniques by name and with examples
  • Has recalculated nutrition data to a common serving size
  • Has applied the three-question checklist to at least five new claims
  • Has completed an advertising audit on non-cereal material
Week 5 · Grade 2 Unit

Is This Cereal Actually Healthy? — The Final Argument

Students build and present a complete argument using all the tools from the unit. The answer matters less than the quality of the reasoning.

LensDaily FocusWhat Students DoReasoning Connection
Literacy & WritingArgument paragraphStudents write a paragraph-length argument answering: "Is [Cereal A] healthy by our class definition?" Must include: claim (yes or no or partially), at least two pieces of label-based evidence, identification of at least one misleading element on the box. No opinion sentences allowed — teacher marks any opinion sentence with a question: "Can this be checked?"A paragraph argument is a significant step from the Grade 1 three-box map. Students must sequence their reasoning in prose, which requires understanding not just the components of an argument but their logical order.
MathematicsFinal data summaryStudents complete a final comparison chart: both cereals, all key nutritional metrics, adjusted to common serving size. Write one sentence about which is healthier "by the numbers" and one sentence about where the numbers don't tell the whole story.Acknowledging where data is insufficient is a sophisticated epistemological move — students who can say "the data shows X but doesn't tell us Y" understand that evidence has limits, not just conclusions.
VisualizationPoster presentationIn pairs, students create a poster: "The Truth About [Cereal]." Sections: What the box claims, What the label actually shows, Persuasion techniques we found, Our verdict and why. Post around the classroom for a gallery walk.The poster is the unit's project deliverable. It externalizes the full argument in a format that others can evaluate. Presenting an argument publicly — with peers who can question it — is a social accountability structure that strengthens reasoning.
Project WorkGallery walk questioningStudents walk the gallery and ask at least one question at two different posters: "How do you know?" or "Is that a fact or an opinion?" or "Is that expert an expert in nutrition?" Students write down the most interesting answer they received.Peer questioning of peer arguments is the highest-level activity in this unit. Students who can ask a good evaluative question have internalized the framework — not just applied it to their own work.
Argument/ReasoningThinking Journal — transferThinking Journal entry: "I will use what I learned this week when I see ___." Students predict a real-world situation where these skills will matter. Share with a partner. Teacher asks: "Why will those skills matter in that situation?"Connecting classroom learning to life contexts is the prerequisite for genuine transfer. A student who can describe when and why they would use fact/opinion evaluation has moved from skill to understanding.

By end of Week 5

  • Has written a paragraph argument about cereal healthiness with label-based evidence
  • Has completed a final data comparison chart with serving-size adjustment
  • Has created and presented a "Truth About Cereal" poster
  • Has asked at least two evaluative questions at classmates' posters
Unit Assessment · Grade 2

What Mastery Looks Like

Transfer Task — New Product

  • Setup: Give the student a printed image of an advertisement for a product they have not seen in class (juice, vitamins, sports drink). Ask: "Is this advertisement honest? Find one claim that can be checked and one that cannot."
  • Pass: Student correctly identifies a verifiable claim (a specific number from a label) and a non-verifiable claim (a weasel phrase or authority endorsement). Can explain why the distinction matters.
  • Fail: Student says everything on a label must be true because labels are "official." This is the most common failure mode — students who have learned to read cereal box labels may still believe that institutional formats guarantee truth.

Fallacy Identification

  • Setup: Read three short claim sentences. Ask the student to identify which persuasion technique each uses and why.
  • Pass: Student correctly identifies technique by name and explains the specific flaw in the reasoning — not just the label.
  • Key failure mode: Students who can name fallacies but cannot explain what is wrong with the reasoning have memorized categories without understanding them. The name is not the lesson.
Gating Connection · Grade 2

How This Unit Connects to Gating Criteria

Gating Criteria Addressed

  • Distinguishes between verifiable facts and opinions — practiced daily through the fact/opinion sort and the three-question checklist.
  • Identifies at least two fallacies by name and can explain what makes them fallacious — appeal to authority and weasel words are both introduced with concept first, name second.
  • Reads data critically — asks "compared to what?" — the serving size recalculation and the "50% more" exercise both build this habit.
  • Writes a paragraph argument with verifiable evidence — the Week 5 argument paragraph is the first multi-sentence argument in the curriculum.

The Grade 2 gating question: Can this student look at a confident claim and ask whether it can be verified — even when it comes from an authority figure? The cereal box unit establishes that institutions, celebrities, and confident-sounding packaging are all subject to the same question: where is the checkable evidence?
