Core Skills · Grade 2

Literacy — Word Study, Fluency & Paragraph Writing

28 minutes daily. Decoding becomes automatic; composition becomes the new frontier.

Grade 2 is where decoding begins to become automatic for most students — what Chall calls the "confirmation and fluency" stage. Students who decoded effortfully in Grade 1 now read the same words with speed and ease. When this happens, cognitive resources that were consumed by decoding are freed for comprehension. The literacy block shifts its emphasis accordingly: less explicit phonics, more fluency practice, and the first real paragraph-length writing. (Chall, J.S., 1983. Stages of Reading Development. McGraw-Hill. Stage 2 — Confirmation and Fluency — is characterized by consolidating word recognition into automatic, effortless decoding.)

Fluency practice means reading the same short text multiple times with a focus on expression and accuracy — not speed. Repeated reading with a partner or the teacher produces significant gains in both fluency and comprehension. (Therrien, W.J., 2004. "Fluency and comprehension gains as a result of repeated reading." Remedial and Special Education, 25(4), 252–261. Meta-analysis: repeated reading produces significant gains in both fluency and reading comprehension — the two most important Grade 2 literacy targets.)

Spelling as Linguistic Investigation

By Grade 2, students are ready to ask not just "how do I spell this?" but "why is it spelled this way?" Words Their Way positions spelling as word study — understanding the patterns that govern English orthography. This connects naturally to the curriculum's source evaluation work: where did this word come from? (Many English spelling patterns are explained by the word's French, Latin, or Anglo-Saxon origin.) Moats argues this framing — spelling as linguistic archaeology — is both more accurate and more intellectually engaging than word list memorization.

Literacy Gating — End of Grade 2

  • Reads a grade-appropriate text (100–150 words) with accuracy and expression — decoding is not effortful
  • Sorts words by vowel pattern (e.g., long-a: a_e vs. ai vs. ay) and states the rule
  • Writes a 3-sentence opinion paragraph independently: claim, evidence, closing
  • Thinking Journal entries are written (not just drawn) — at least one complete sentence per entry
Core Skills · Grade 2

Mathematics — Place Value, Addition & Multiplication Foundations

28 minutes daily. Place value to 1000. Addition and subtraction fluent. Multiplication introduced as repeated addition.

Grade 2 mathematics extends place value understanding from tens and ones to hundreds, and builds toward fluency in addition and subtraction within 100. The critical conceptual move this year is understanding why the standard addition algorithm works — not just the steps, but the place value logic underneath. Students who understand this can reconstruct a forgotten procedure; students who only memorized steps cannot. (Rittle-Johnson, B. & Star, J.R., 2007. "Does comparing solution methods facilitate conceptual and procedural knowledge?" Journal of Educational Psychology, 99(3), 561–574. Conceptual understanding enables reconstruction of forgotten procedures and supports transfer to novel problems.)

Introducing multiplication conceptually in Grade 2 means Grade 3 fact fluency practice is building on genuine understanding, not rote memorization of isolated facts.

Mathematics Gating — End of Grade 2

  • Builds a 3-digit number with base-ten blocks and explains what each digit represents
  • Adds two 2-digit numbers using at least two different strategies and explains why both work
  • Represents a multiplication fact (e.g., 3×4) as an array, as repeated addition, and as a multiplication sentence
  • Adds and subtracts within 100 accurately — not by counting on fingers
Phase One · Grade 2

The First Line in the Sand

Some things can be checked. Some things are preferences. And some people try to make preferences look like facts.

The fact/opinion distinction is the first epistemic line this curriculum draws explicitly. It arrives not as a grammar lesson but as a power tool — students learn it because it helps them catch adults (and themselves) doing something slippery.

Fallacies enter this year as named patterns in stories and conversations, not as abstract definitions. 'Everyone says so' is the first one — because it is everywhere, and students already know it feels wrong before they have a name for it.

Core Domain · Reasoning

What Can Be Checked

Facts have evidence. Opinions have preferences. Some claims disguise one as the other.

What Students Practice

  • Sorting statements: 'Dogs are better than cats' vs 'Dogs are mammals' — which can be checked?
  • The question: 'How would we find out if that's true?' applied to every claim
  • Loaded language: words that sneak an opinion into a description ('obviously,' 'everyone knows,' 'clearly')
  • Spotting when an opinion is stated as if it were a fact — and naming it
  • Writing both: an opinion sentence and a fact sentence about the same topic
Core Domain · First Fallacies

Naming the Tricks

Two fallacies introduced this year — as story patterns, not Latin terms.

Appeal to Popularity — 'Everyone Says So'

  • Introduced through a story: the whole class says the moon is made of cheese — does that make it true?
  • The question: 'Does it matter how many people believe something if there's no evidence?'
  • Students find examples in their own experience: 'Everyone has this toy so I need it'
  • Written practice: finding the hidden claim and asking 'but how do we know?'

Ad Hominem — Attacking the Person, Not the Idea

  • Introduced through a story: 'Don't listen to him — he's weird'
  • The key question: 'Is the argument bad, or is the person who made it bad?'
  • Practice: separating the speaker from the claim — evaluating the argument on its own
  • Connection to the Heatmap: 'Does your feeling about a person change how you hear their argument?'

Fallacies are introduced as patterns to recognize, not vocabulary to memorize. A student who can spot 'everyone says so' in a conversation has learned something — a student who can spell 'ad populum' has not necessarily learned anything.

Core Domain · Pattern & Logic

AND, OR, NOT — The First Logic Operators

Introduced through sorting games before they are named as logic.

What Students Practice

  • AND: Sorting blocks that are big AND red — both conditions must be true
  • OR: Sorting blocks that are big OR red — at least one condition must be true
  • NOT: 'All animals that are NOT dogs' — exclusion as a logical operation
  • Combining operators: 'Find something that is big AND red AND NOT round'
  • Verbal puzzles: 'I'm thinking of a number that is greater than 5 AND less than 10 AND NOT 7'
  • Introduction to the truth table — a simple two-column chart showing when a rule is satisfied
Core Domain · Visualization

Venn Diagrams & Evidence Charts

The first formal visual logic tools.

What Students Practice

  • Venn diagrams with two circles: things that are true of A, things true of B, things true of both
  • Evidence charts: the claim in the middle, evidence items around it, lines connecting them
  • Simple yes/no decision tree: 'Is this a fact? → Can we check it? → Where would we look?'
  • Fact vs opinion sorting visual: two labeled columns, statements sorted into each
Operational Layer · Grade 2

Evaluation Primitives

Each capability is only real when it can be demonstrated on an unfamiliar problem under no-hint conditions.

Task 1 — Fact vs Opinion Sort on New Statements

Setup: Present 8–10 statements on a new topic the class has not discussed (e.g., a made-up animal, a fictional town). Ask the student to sort them into "fact" and "opinion" and explain each sorting decision.

Pass: Student correctly sorts at least 7 of 10 statements AND provides a checkability-based explanation for each: "this is a fact because we could look it up / measure it / test it" — not just "it sounds true." Catches at least one opinion that uses loaded language ("obviously," "everyone knows") and names it as opinion-disguised-as-fact.

Fail: Student sorts by whether the statement "sounds true" or "sounds like a feeling" rather than by checkability. Cannot explain why an opinion is an opinion — only that it "seems like one."

Failure mode to watch: Sorting by tone or certainty rather than verifiability — students learn that opinions "sound uncertain" and facts "sound confident," so they misclassify loaded language as fact because it sounds definitive.

Task 2 — Fallacy Spotting in a New Conversation

Setup: Read aloud a short invented dialogue (characters and scenario not from class) where a character uses either "everyone does/thinks X" or "don't listen to him, he's [dismissive label]." Ask: "What's wrong with that argument?"

Pass: Student identifies that the argument doesn't address whether the claim is actually true — explains in their own words why the number of believers doesn't settle the question (for Appeal to Popularity), or why the person's character doesn't settle whether their argument is right (for Ad Hominem). Does not need to use the fallacy names.

Fail: Student says the argument is wrong because they disagree with the conclusion, or says "that's mean" (for Ad Hominem) without identifying the logical problem.

Failure mode to watch: Identifying the feeling of unfairness without the logical reason — "that's not fair to say" is not the same as "that doesn't tell us whether the claim is true."

Task 3 — Boolean Sorting on a New Problem

Setup: Present a new set of objects or picture cards (not blocks, not anything used in class practice). Give a multi-condition rule: "Find all the things that are [X] AND [Y] but NOT [Z]." Then give a second rule using OR.

Pass: Student correctly identifies the qualifying items for both the AND and OR rule sets, and can explain the difference: "AND means both have to be true, OR means at least one." Handles NOT correctly — does not accidentally include items that satisfy NOT as a positive category.

Fail: Student treats AND and OR interchangeably, or treats NOT as meaning "find the things that are Z" rather than "exclude Z."

Failure mode to watch: Correct answers on the AND task through elimination by visual inspection — students who get to the right pile by narrowing down rather than understanding the rule, revealed when they cannot explain the OR result.

Task 4 — Venn Diagram on New Content

Setup: Name two things the class has never compared (e.g., a rainstorm and a snowstorm; a library and a playground). Ask the student to build a Venn diagram that correctly places shared and non-shared properties.

Pass: Student places at least 3 items in each outer region and at least 2 in the overlap — all correctly categorized. Can explain why each item in the middle belongs to both. Does not confuse "things I know about A" with "things that are true of A only."

Fail: Student treats one circle as the "important" one and puts most items there, leaving the overlap sparse — or uses the overlap for items that are actually only true of one category.

Failure mode to watch: Venn diagrams filled by association rather than by logical category membership — placing "cold" in the snowstorm circle alone when it belongs to both.

Operational Layer · Grade 2

Gating Criteria

What a student must demonstrate — not just complete — before advancing to Grade 3.

Required Demonstrations

  • Sorts new fact/opinion statements by checkability — not by how true they sound — and catches at least one loaded-language opinion-as-fact disguise
  • Identifies the logical flaw in an Appeal to Popularity or Ad Hominem argument in new content: explains why it doesn't settle the question, not just that it "seems wrong"
  • Applies AND, OR, and NOT rules correctly to unfamiliar sorting material — can explain the rule, not just produce the right pile
  • Builds a Venn diagram on new content with correctly placed items in both outer regions and the overlap — can defend each placement
  • Asks "how would we check that?" spontaneously when a claim is made — not only when prompted by the teacher
  • Has recorded at least three real-world fallacy sightings in the Thinking Journal, with a written explanation of what was wrong

Literacy & Mathematics — Required Demonstrations

  • Reads a grade-appropriate passage with fluency and expression — decoding is not effortful
  • Sorts words by vowel pattern and states the rule that governs the group
  • Writes a 3-sentence opinion paragraph independently: claim, evidence, closing
  • Builds a 3-digit number with base-ten blocks and explains what each digit represents
  • Adds two 2-digit numbers using at least two strategies and explains why both work
  • Represents a multiplication fact as an array, as repeated addition, and as a multiplication sentence

What Does Not Count as Mastery

  • Correctly sorting the same fact/opinion examples used in class practice — familiar examples test memory
  • Naming the two fallacies by label without being able to explain the logical flaw in a new example
  • Completing Boolean sorting games on the same block set used during instruction
  • A Venn diagram completed with a partner or with teacher guidance on what belongs where
  • Journal entries that copy a class example rather than recording an independently noticed instance

The gating question: When this student hears a new claim — from a peer, a book, or an adult — do they automatically ask whether it is a fact or an opinion, and whether the argument actually addresses the question? That reflex, applied to unfamiliar content without prompting, is the bar. Worksheet completion is not.

Operational Layer · Grade 2

Adversarial Exercises

Sovereignty requires training against the actual conditions that undermine independent thinking.

Exercise 1 — The "Everyone Is Doing It" Game

Setup: The teacher sets up a low-stakes class vote: "I think we should [pick a clearly minor thing — have apple juice instead of orange juice at snack / use red paper instead of white for today's drawing]. Who agrees?" Almost everyone will raise their hand. Then the teacher points to a student who didn't and asks: "Why didn't you raise your hand?" — making the lone hold-out visible.

The question posed to the class: "Does the fact that everyone voted for it make it the right answer? What would make it the right answer?"

What students practice: Noticing the social pressure to agree with the majority — and separating that pressure from the question of whether the majority is correct. The exercise is repeated with a question that actually has a checkable right answer ("how many legs does a spider have?") so students can compare: popularity settles preferences, not facts.

The skill being trained: Resistance to Appeal to Popularity in real time — not just pattern-recognition on a worksheet. Students should be able to say out loud: "A lot of people think it, but that doesn't mean it's true."

Exercise 2 — The "But He's Annoying" Trap

Setup: Present a short puppet show or story where a character says something that is actually correct ("The snack is on the top shelf") but is a character that other characters don't like or find irritating. The other characters say "Don't listen to him — he's always annoying."

The question posed to the class: "Is the snack on the top shelf? Does it matter whether the character is annoying? How do we find out if the claim is true?"

What students practice: Separating how they feel about the speaker from whether the speaker's claim is correct. Students are then given new mini-scenarios and asked: "Is this about the person or about the idea?"

The skill being trained: Ad Hominem resistance — the capacity to evaluate a claim independently of feelings about the claimant. Age-appropriate version: "Even someone you don't like can be right about a fact."

Why adversarial training at age 7? Both fallacies taught this year — Appeal to Popularity and Ad Hominem — are social weapons. They work by recruiting feelings (belonging, dislike) to replace logic. The only way to build resistance is to practice noticing the feeling and holding the question open anyway. Worksheets teach recognition; these exercises train the actual response under social pressure — which is when it matters.

Embodied Sovereignty · Grade 2

Patterns in the Land

Water cycles, seasonal rhythms, edible versus not — the first layer of practical literacy.

What Students Do

  • Individual weather journal (building from K's collective one)
  • Water cycle traced as a pattern: rain → soil → plant → evaporation → rain
  • Plant identification expanded: 5 local plants, edible vs. non-edible distinction introduced — not foraging, just identification literacy
  • Simple food preservation: drying herbs from the class garden
  • Soil study: what is in healthy soil? (worms, organisms, structure)

The Pattern Connection

  • Cognitive Grade 2 teaches Boolean basics and pattern recognition. In embodied sovereignty, the same capacity is applied to the natural world: what repeats? What is the rule? What breaks the pattern and what does that mean?
  • A student who can read a food web as a pattern structure has extended the same skill into a different domain. (Sobel, 'Mapmaking with Children,' 1998)

Assessment

  • Can the student correctly identify 5 local plants and distinguish edible from non-edible with a stated reason?
  • Can they explain the water cycle in their own words and trace it in their local environment?

The edible/non-edible distinction is introduced conceptually here, not practically. Students learn that the distinction exists and how to approach it — confirmation by a knowledgeable adult is always required before anything is consumed. This is source evaluation applied to the land.
