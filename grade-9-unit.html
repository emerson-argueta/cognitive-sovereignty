Tier 9
How Should Cities Respond to Homelessness?

Band III begins with breadth — every student encounters the same Core Problem through the lens of their emerging track, while remaining fluent in all four. Homelessness is chosen because it is morally serious, empirically contested, and genuinely multi-disciplinary: it requires data analysis (systems track), sociological research (academic track), policy thinking (civic track), and practical construction knowledge (vocational track). All four lenses are legitimate. None is complete alone.

Unit Design · Tier 9

What This Unit Builds

Five weeks exploring the same complex social problem through four disciplinary lenses — then synthesizing across them.

Tier 9 is discovery territory. Students are beginning to know which track calls to them, but deep specialization does not begin until Tier 11. The homelessness unit is designed to show what each track brings to a real-world problem — and what each track misses by itself. An engineer who can model housing supply without understanding the social determinants of chronic homelessness will build the wrong solution. A social researcher who understands the causes but cannot engage with the construction and logistics of housing policy will produce research with no implementation pathway.

Week 1 is identical for all students — establishing the common factual base. Weeks 2–4 diverge by track, with each track going deeper into its distinctive tools and perspective. Week 5 brings all four tracks back together for a cross-track synthesis where students must explain their track's contribution to peers from other tracks.

What you will see by Week 5: Students who can say "from a systems perspective, the problem looks like ___; from a civic perspective, it looks like ___; and the tension between them is ___." Students who understand that disciplinary depth is not the same as disciplinary exclusivity — that the best practitioners know when to reach across disciplines.

Week 1 · Tier 9 Unit · All Tracks

What Are the Facts? — Cross-Track Common Ground

All four tracks engage the same material this week. The factual base is established before perspectives diverge.

LensDaily FocusWhat All Students DoReasoning Connection
Grammar & WritingDefining the problem preciselyStudents read three definitions of homelessness used by different agencies (HUD's sheltered/unsheltered definition, the broader "housing instability" definition, the European ETHOS typology). Write: "Which definition would produce the largest count? The smallest? What are the policy implications of choosing one definition over another?"Definition choice in policy is not neutral — it determines who gets counted, who gets services, and how the problem's scale is presented. Students who have mapped the definitional choices understand that the "homeless population" is not a fixed fact but a construct that depends on the definitions used to count it.
MathematicsScale and composition of the populationUsing national point-in-time count data: students analyze the composition of the unhoused population — families with children, chronically homeless adults, veterans, youth aging out of foster care, people with mental illness or substance use disorders. Build a breakdown chart. "What percentage of the homeless population has been unhoused for more than a year? What does that imply for intervention strategies?"Population composition determines intervention strategy. A policy designed for chronically homeless single adults will not address family homelessness — and a policy designed for transitional homelessness will not address chronic homelessness. Understanding composition before proposing solutions is the analytical prerequisite for effective policy design.
VisualizationCausal diagram — shared baselineStudents build a causal diagram of homelessness as a class: housing cost, income insufficiency, job loss, medical crisis, domestic violence, mental illness, substance use disorders, criminal record, lack of social support. Arrows showing which factors cause which outcomes. "Which factors appear in multiple causal pathways? Which are most central?"A shared causal diagram built collectively establishes the factual baseline all four tracks will work from. The centrality of different nodes in the causal diagram will be weighted differently by different tracks — which is the source of legitimate disciplinary disagreement.
Project WorkWhat has been tried — outcomes dataStudents survey five major intervention types: emergency shelters, transitional housing, permanent supportive housing (Housing First), criminalization/sweeps, mental health street outreach. For each: what outcomes data exists? What populations does it serve? What is the cost per person housed? Students compile a comparative table.Surveying what has been tried before proposing new solutions prevents the most common policy error: proposing interventions that have already been tried and failed, or ignoring evidence of what works. The outcomes data table is the evidentiary foundation for every track's subsequent analysis.
Argument/ReasoningInitial track framingStudents write their first track-specific response to the common problem. Academic track: "The research question I would ask." Vocational track: "The practical constraint I notice immediately." Systems track: "The feedback loop I want to model." Civic track: "The stakeholder whose interests are most neglected in current policy." All responses shared across tracks.The initial track framing reveals the genuine diversity of expert perspectives on the same problem — each is legitimate, each is partial. Students who read all four framings before going deep in their own have a broader understanding than students who go straight to their own track's perspective.

By end of Week 1

  • Can explain how definitional choice affects homeless population counts and policy
  • Has built a population composition chart and identified implications for intervention strategy
  • Has contributed to the class causal diagram
  • Has written an initial track-specific framing and read all four framings
Week 2 · Tier 9 Unit · Tracks Diverge

Academic Track: What Does the Research Show About Causes?

Academic track deep-dive — research methodology, causal inference, and the Housing First evidence base. All tracks do equivalent deep-dives in parallel.

Note on parallel tracks: While this page shows the Academic track week in detail, the Vocational track examines affordable housing construction economics and building code constraints; the Systems track models housing supply and demand as a feedback system; the Civic track analyzes municipal policy failures and tenant protection legislation. All four tracks present their findings in Week 5.

LensDaily FocusAcademic Track Students DoReasoning Connection
Grammar & WritingLiterature review skillsStudents read five research abstracts on homelessness causes and interventions — including the landmark Pathways to Housing study on Housing First (Tsemberis, 1999) and subsequent replications. Annotate for: study design, population, outcome measured, effect size, whether findings have been replicated. Produce a structured literature summary table.A structured literature summary table is the academic equivalent of the Tier 6 evidence quality matrix — applied now to actual peer-reviewed research in a complex social domain. Students who can synthesize five studies into a summary are doing the foundational work of academic research.
MathematicsCost-effectiveness analysisStudents calculate cost per person housed for three intervention types using provided data: emergency shelter ($35/night × 365 = $12,775/year), transitional housing ($20,000/year), permanent supportive housing ($15,000/year + health cost savings of $10,000+/year). "Which is most cost-effective over five years? What does this calculation not capture?"Cost-effectiveness analysis reveals that permanent supportive housing — which appears most expensive — is often least expensive over five years because it dramatically reduces emergency room use, jail costs, and crisis service costs. Students who can do this calculation understand why the "most expensive" intervention can be the most economical investment.
VisualizationCausal inference diagramStudents draw a directed acyclic graph (DAG) for one proposed cause of homelessness: does mental illness cause homelessness, or do the stresses of housing instability cause mental illness, or both? Draw both causal models and identify what data would distinguish them.A DAG (simplified) is the visualization tool for causal inference in observational data. Drawing alternative causal models and then identifying distinguishing evidence is the most sophisticated form of the "correlation vs. causation" reasoning established in Tier 4.
Project WorkEvidence synthesis — what works?Students produce a one-page evidence synthesis: "Based on the best available research, what interventions have the strongest evidence for reducing chronic homelessness? What interventions are widely used but have weak evidence? What research is missing?" This becomes the academic track's contribution to the Week 5 cross-track synthesis.A policy-relevant evidence synthesis — translating research into actionable findings for decision-makers — is the academic track's distinctive contribution. Other tracks produce practical, systems, and civic analyses; the academic track produces the evidentiary foundation for evaluating all of them.
Argument/ReasoningWhat the other tracks are missingAcademic track students write: "Here is what the vocational/systems/civic perspectives on this problem don't see that the research reveals." And: "Here is what the research perspective misses that each other track would see." Honest engagement with the limits of one's own track perspective is required.Identifying both what your track uniquely contributes AND what it uniquely misses is the intellectual humility practice at Band III. The goal is disciplinary fluency, not disciplinary supremacy — understanding when to reach across perspectives.

By end of Week 2 (Academic Track)

  • Has produced a structured literature summary table for five studies
  • Has calculated cost-effectiveness of three intervention types over five years
  • Has drawn a DAG for one proposed causal relationship and identified distinguishing evidence
  • Has written a one-page evidence synthesis as the track's Week 5 contribution
Week 3 · Tier 9 Unit · Tracks Diverge

Vocational Track: What Are the Construction Constraints?

Vocational track deep-dive — affordable housing economics, building codes, modular construction, and the trades shortage. All tracks run parallel deep-dives this week.

LensDaily FocusVocational Track Students DoSystems / Civic / Academic Parallel
Grammar & WritingReading specifications and codesStudents read simplified excerpts from residential building codes and zoning ordinances (setback requirements, minimum unit sizes, parking minimums, single-family zoning). Write: "Which of these requirements increases housing costs? Which ones might be relaxed to enable more affordable units? What are the arguments for keeping each requirement?"Systems track: models zoning as a constraint on housing supply feedback loops. Civic track: analyzes who benefits politically from restrictive zoning. Academic track: reviews research on zoning reform and housing cost outcomes.
MathematicsConstruction cost breakdownStudents analyze a simplified construction cost breakdown for a 50-unit affordable housing project: land (30%), hard construction costs (40%), soft costs — permits, design, legal (15%), financing costs (15%). Calculate: if land costs double, how does total project cost change? If permit timelines triple soft costs, what is the new total? "Which cost categories does policy most directly control?"Understanding construction economics is the vocational track's analytical tool. Students who know that land and permits are the most policy-sensitive cost categories can evaluate housing proposals that only address construction costs with appropriate skepticism.
VisualizationModular and prefab housing comparisonStudents compare conventional stick-built construction vs. modular/prefabricated construction: cost per square foot, build time, quality variance, scalability. Draw a comparison matrix. "Could modular construction significantly reduce affordable housing costs? What are the barriers to wider adoption?"Technical alternatives to conventional construction are the vocational track's distinctive contribution to housing policy. A policy that ignores construction method alternatives is leaving significant cost-reduction potential on the table — which the vocational track is best positioned to identify.
Project WorkFeasibility analysisStudents conduct a simplified feasibility analysis for a hypothetical 30-unit affordable housing development: land cost (given), construction cost per unit (given), financing assumptions, required rent to service debt. "At what rent level does this project break even? What subsidy would be needed to achieve 30% AMI rents?" This becomes the vocational track's Week 5 contribution.A feasibility analysis is the practical reality check on housing policy: it tells you whether a proposed development is financially viable, and at what subsidy level it becomes viable. Policy proposals that ignore feasibility produce plans that cannot be built.
Argument/ReasoningWhat the other tracks are missingVocational track students write: "Here is what none of the other tracks understands about building housing that the trades perspective reveals." And: "Here is what the vocational perspective alone cannot answer."Same intellectual humility structure as the Academic track in Week 2: genuine contribution AND genuine limits acknowledged explicitly.

By end of Week 3 (Vocational Track)

  • Has identified which building code and zoning requirements most affect housing costs
  • Has analyzed a construction cost breakdown and identified policy-sensitive categories
  • Has compared conventional and modular construction and assessed barrier to adoption
  • Has produced a simplified feasibility analysis as the track's Week 5 contribution
Week 4 · Tier 9 Unit · Systems & Civic Tracks

Systems Track: Feedback Loops · Civic Track: Policy and Rights

The two remaining tracks complete their deep-dives. All four prepare Week 5 contributions.

TrackDeep-Dive FocusWeek 4 ActivitiesWeek 5 Contribution
Systems TrackHousing as a complex adaptive systemStudents model housing supply and demand as a causal feedback loop: rising rents → more construction → more supply → lower rents (balancing loop). But also: rising rents → displacement → less community stability → businesses leave → neighborhood decline (reinforcing loop). Students draw both loops and identify leverage points — where small interventions can produce large system changes. Apply systems archetypes: "fixes that fail" (shelters that keep people cycling without addressing root causes), "tragedy of the commons" (no single actor has incentive to build affordable housing).Systems map of homelessness as a feedback system, with identified leverage points for intervention and labeled system archetypes. Presented to full class in Week 5.
Civic TrackPolicy history and rights frameworksStudents research the history of US housing policy: public housing (1937–1970s), Section 8 vouchers (1974), HOPE VI (1990s), Housing First (2000s–present). For each: who benefited? Who was excluded or harmed? What political coalitions supported or opposed it? Students also examine: does the US recognize a right to housing? Do other countries? What are the legal implications of rights-based vs. needs-based approaches?Policy history brief: what has been tried, who it served, who it failed, and what a rights-based approach would require differently. Presented to full class in Week 5.

By end of Week 4 (Systems and Civic Tracks)

  • Systems track: causal loop diagram with two feedback loops and three identified leverage points
  • Civic track: policy history brief covering four major policy eras with beneficiary and harm analysis
  • Both tracks: written acknowledgment of contribution and limits of their perspective
  • All tracks: prepared 5-minute Week 5 presentation of their track's contribution
Week 5 · Tier 9 Unit · All Tracks

Cross-Track Synthesis

All four tracks present. Students integrate. A policy proposal that could only exist at the intersection of all four perspectives is built together.

LensDaily FocusWhat All Students DoReasoning Connection
Grammar & WritingTrack presentations — 5 minutes eachOne representative from each track presents their track's contribution: Academic (evidence synthesis), Vocational (feasibility analysis), Systems (causal loops and leverage points), Civic (policy history and rights framework). Audience from other tracks takes notes: "What does this track see that mine doesn't? What question would I ask?"Presenting to an audience from other tracks requires translation — explaining the logic of your track's tools in terms accessible to people who use different tools. This is a high-level communication skill that most professional contexts require but rarely teach explicitly.
MathematicsCross-track cost modelingAll students work together: combine the vocational feasibility numbers, the systems leverage point analysis, and the academic cost-effectiveness data to build an integrated cost model for a proposed intervention. "What would it cost to house every chronically homeless person in our city using Housing First? Where do the vocational, academic, and systems numbers agree? Where do they conflict?"Cross-track integration of quantitative analyses from different disciplinary perspectives is the hardest mathematical task in Band III — it requires translating between different cost categories, time horizons, and units of analysis. The disagreements that emerge reveal where disciplinary frameworks make different assumptions.
VisualizationIntegrated solution diagramStudents build a class-wide solution diagram: a proposed policy response to homelessness that integrates all four track perspectives. The diagram shows: what the policy does (civic), why it works (academic evidence), how it gets built (vocational), and what system dynamics it leverages (systems). Identify: what would this policy need from all four tracks to be successfully implemented?A solution that genuinely integrates all four perspectives is more robust than a single-track solution — because it has been tested against the constraints and insights that each disciplinary perspective uniquely provides. This is the argument for disciplinary breadth in Tier 9.
Project WorkIndividual cross-track reflectionStudents write individually: "The perspective that most changed how I think about this problem was ___. The most important thing I learned from a track that is not mine is ___. If I could only implement one policy from the evidence we reviewed, it would be ___ because ___."The individual reflection after the group synthesis ensures students have done their own integration, not just absorbed the group's output. Each student's synthesis will be different — which is appropriate for a genuinely complex problem with no single correct answer.
Argument/ReasoningWhat single-track thinking missesFinal discussion: "What would have been wrong with a homelessness policy designed only by academic researchers? Only by construction professionals? Only by systems modelers? Only by civic advocates?" Students identify the specific failure mode of each single-track approach — and articulate why integration is not just additive but qualitatively different.The argument for integration is not just that more perspectives are better — it is that each track has specific blind spots that another track can correct. Understanding the specific failure mode of single-track thinking is the strongest argument for disciplinary breadth as a Band III value.

By end of Week 5

  • Has attended all four track presentations and taken comparative notes
  • Has contributed to the cross-track cost model integration
  • Has contributed to the integrated solution diagram
  • Has written an individual cross-track reflection naming the most important insight from outside their track
Unit Assessment · Tier 9

What Mastery Looks Like

Cross-Track Transfer — New Problem

  • Setup: Present a new complex social problem (e.g., rural hospital closures). Ask: "How would each of the four tracks approach this problem? What would each see that the others miss?"
  • Pass: Student describes all four track perspectives with specific tools (not just general impressions), identifies at least one genuine tension between two tracks' approaches, and explains why integration would produce a better solution than any single track alone.
  • Fail: Student describes their own track's approach in depth and gives thin, generic descriptions of other tracks. Track fluency — not just track depth — is the Tier 9 standard.
Gating Connection · Tier 9

How This Unit Connects to Gating Criteria

Gating Criteria Addressed

  • Engages a complex problem from all four track perspectives with equal seriousness — the unit structure requires deep engagement with both the student's own track and all others.
  • Identifies the specific contribution and blind spot of their own track — explicitly required in each track's Week 2–4 "what the other tracks are missing" reflection.
  • Produces a track-specific deliverable at research or professional level — the evidence synthesis, feasibility analysis, systems map, or policy brief is the track's contribution to Week 5.
  • Integrates cross-track insights into a more robust solution than any single track would produce — the integrated solution diagram and individual reflection in Week 5.

The Tier 9 gating question: Can this student understand the same problem from four different disciplinary perspectives — and articulate what each perspective uniquely contributes and uniquely misses? Band III breadth means understanding the landscape before choosing where to go deep.
