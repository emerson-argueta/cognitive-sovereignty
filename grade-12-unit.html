Grade 12 · Sample Unit · Phase 3

The Capstone Defense

Five weeks. One thesis. A public defense of everything you've learned to think.

Unit Overview

There is no shared Core Problem this unit. Each student selects their own thesis — a defensible claim that cuts across disciplines, tracks, and the Five Lenses they have practiced since Grade 1. The Capstone Defense is the culminating demonstration that a student can not only hold a position under pressure, but earned it through rigorous evidence, honest engagement with opposition, and disciplined reasoning.

The Five Lenses, which have always been analytical tools, now become the five pillars of the defense document itself: written argumentation, quantitative support, visual evidence, a completed deliverable, and a public argument defended in real time.

Who this unit is for: All Grade 12 students, regardless of track. Academic, Vocational, Systems, and Civic track students all complete the same five-week arc and defend on the same day. The content of each thesis will differ; the standard of rigor will not.

The Five Defense Pillars (= Five Lenses Applied)

Lens Defense Pillar What the Panel Looks For
Grammar & Writing Written Thesis Document Precise claim, coherent structure, proper citation, no hedge-by-vagueness
Math / Pattern Logic Quantitative Support Section At least one data set analyzed; no misleading statistics; uncertainty acknowledged
Visualization Visual Evidence Package Chart, diagram, or model that adds information not already in the prose
Project Work Deliverable Artifact A concrete output: prototype, policy memo, experiment log, annotated bibliography, or equivalent
Argument / Reasoning Live Oral Defense 15-minute presentation + 10-minute panel Q&A; steelmanned opposition addressed; qualifiers used appropriately

Week 1 · Thesis Selection & Scope Audit

A good thesis is not a topic. "Climate change" is a topic. "Carbon pricing is a more effective emissions reduction tool than vehicle electrification mandates when measured over a 20-year horizon" is a thesis — a defensible, falsifiable claim that implies evidence, acknowledges alternatives, and can be wrong. Week 1's entire work is generating and stress-testing that claim before any research begins.

Week 1 · Five Lenses

Lens This Week's Work
Writing Draft three candidate thesis statements. Apply the "Falsifiability Test": could a reasonable person disagree with evidence? Apply the "Scope Test": can this be defended in 15 minutes with available sources?
Math / Logic Formalize the thesis as a conditional or biconditional claim. Identify what would have to be true for the claim to be false. List the 2–3 key variables the defense will need to measure or cite.
Visualization Draw a "thesis map" — claim at center, evidence branches radiating out, opposition branches in a different color. This is a planning diagram, not a deliverable; it surfaces scope problems early.
Project Submit a 1-page Scope Memo: chosen thesis, rationale for choice, track connection (how does your track lens inform the claim?), anticipated hardest counterargument.
Argument Peer workshop: trade thesis statements. Each reviewer writes one genuine objection in Toulmin format (Claim / Grounds / Warrant). Author revises or prepares a rebuttal. Instructor signs off on final thesis before Week 2 begins.
Instructor approval gate: No student advances to Week 2 without an approved thesis. Common rejection reasons: topic without a claim, claim without a testable component, scope too broad to defend in 25 minutes, or claim that is definitionally true and therefore undefendable.

Thesis Quality Spectrum

Level Example Problem
Topic (not a thesis) "Artificial intelligence in healthcare" Makes no claim; cannot be defended or refuted
Weak thesis "AI has both benefits and drawbacks in healthcare" Trivially true; no reasonable person disagrees
Acceptable thesis "AI diagnostic tools reduce misdiagnosis rates in radiology" Defensible but broad; needs scope qualifier
Strong thesis "AI-assisted radiology reduces false-negative mammography rates more than radiologist training programs at equivalent cost per prevented missed diagnosis" None — specific, falsifiable, implies evidence standard

Week 2 · Evidence Audit

Evidence is not decoration — it is the load-bearing structure of the defense. Week 2 is a systematic audit of what sources exist, what they actually show, and how strong the inferential chain is from source to claim. Students who skip this step often discover in Week 4 that their evidence supports a different claim than the one they are making.

Week 2 · Five Lenses

Lens This Week's Work
Writing Annotated source inventory: for each of the 8+ sources, write (a) a 2-sentence summary of what the source shows, (b) a 1-sentence statement of how it connects to your thesis, (c) a 1-sentence honest assessment of the source's limitations.
Math / Logic Rate each source on two dimensions: strength of evidence (anecdote / case study / observational study / RCT / meta-analysis) and proximity to your specific claim (direct / indirect / analogous). Plot these on a 2×2 grid to see where your evidence is weak.
Visualization Build an evidence chain diagram: thesis claim at top, each piece of evidence as a node below it, arrows showing inferential steps. Identify any "missing links" — inferences with no source support.
Project Gap analysis memo: after the audit, identify the 1–2 weakest points in your evidence chain. Describe how you will address them in Weeks 3–4 (find a better source, narrow the claim, or acknowledge the limitation explicitly in the defense).
Argument Source reliability workshop: each student presents their two weakest sources to a small group. Group applies the Grade 3 Source Checklist (Author / Date / Method / Replication) and the Grade 8 p-hacking screen. Does the source survive scrutiny? If not, the student must either replace it or quarantine it to "acknowledged limitations."
Continuity moment: The 4-criterion source checklist (Author credibility, Publication date, Methodology transparency, Independent replication) was introduced in Grade 3. Students should now be applying it fluently, without a rubric, as a habit of mind.

Evidence Strength Hierarchy

Evidence Type Inferential Strength Appropriate Use in Defense
Anecdote / single case Low Illustration only; never load-bearing
Expert opinion Low–Moderate Supporting context; note credentials and conflicts of interest
Observational study Moderate Acceptable if methodology is disclosed; note confounders
Randomized controlled trial High Core evidence; still note sample, setting, effect size
Systematic review / meta-analysis Highest Preferred; note heterogeneity and publication bias risk

Week 3 · Steel-Manning the Opposition

The weakest defenses argue against a strawman — a distorted, weakened version of the opposing view that collapses easily. The strongest defenses engage the best possible version of the counterargument and survive it anyway. Week 3 is entirely devoted to building the strongest possible case against your own thesis, then determining which objections you can defeat and which you must acknowledge as legitimate limitations.

Week 3 · Five Lenses

Lens This Week's Work
Writing Write a 400-word steelman: argue against your thesis as persuasively as you can, using the best evidence available to the opposition. Then write a 200-word rebuttal for each objection you can defeat. Objections you cannot defeat become acknowledged limitations in your defense document.
Math / Logic For each counterargument, map its logical structure: what claim does it make, what evidence does it cite, what warrant connects them? Identify whether it attacks your evidence, your warrant, or your qualifier — different attack types require different responses.
Visualization Argument map update: add counterargument nodes to your evidence chain diagram (different shape/color). Draw arrows from your rebuttal nodes to the counterargument nodes they defeat. Any counterargument with no rebuttal arrow = unresolved; address in the defense document's "Limitations" section.
Project Adversarial interview: pair with a student whose thesis is in a different domain. They read your steelman and spend 10 minutes questioning you as if they are a skeptical panelist. You respond in real time. Write a 1-page debrief: what surprised you, what you need to strengthen before Week 4.
Argument Qualifier calibration: review your thesis statement. Does it still hold after engaging the opposition? If the steelman revealed genuine limits to your claim, revise the qualifier (e.g., "in most cases" → "in high-income urban contexts"). A narrower, honest thesis is stronger than a broad, brittle one.
The Steelman Rule: Your steelman is adequate when your opponent — the person who actually holds the opposing view — reads it and says "yes, that's what I believe." If they say "that's not quite right," you haven't steelmanned; you've strawmanned with extra steps.

Counterargument Attack Types

Attack Type What It Challenges How to Respond
Evidence attack Your source is wrong, biased, or cherry-picked Provide additional corroborating sources; acknowledge if source is weak
Warrant attack Even if the evidence is true, it doesn't support your claim Make the inferential step explicit; provide bridging evidence for the warrant
Qualifier attack Your claim is true in some cases but not the cases that matter Narrow the qualifier honestly; show your cases are the relevant ones
Value attack Even if true, the conclusion requires accepting values the opponent rejects Make value commitments explicit; show they are widely shared or defensible

Week 4 · Draft Defense + Peer Critique

The full defense document exists for the first time this week. All five pillars — written thesis document, quantitative support section, visual evidence package, deliverable artifact, and live argument structure — are assembled into a coherent whole. A peer critique panel reviews each draft before the student finalizes anything for the public defense.

Week 4 · Five Lenses

Lens This Week's Work
Writing Complete the written thesis document (1,500–2,500 words): Introduction and thesis, Evidence section (citing annotated sources), Counterarguments and rebuttals, Acknowledged limitations, Conclusion. Precision over length — the panel will notice padding.
Math / Logic Finalize the quantitative support section: present at minimum one data set in full, show your calculation or analysis, state the uncertainty range, and explicitly avoid at least one misleading framing (cherry-picked time window, suppressed baseline, percent vs. absolute). Label the section "Quantitative Evidence" clearly.
Visualization Finalize the visual evidence package: at minimum 2 visuals (chart, diagram, map, model). Each visual must have a title, labeled axes or legend, source citation, and a 1-sentence caption that tells the reader what to notice — not just what it shows.
Project Submit the deliverable artifact. This is track-specific but must be a concrete output the panel can evaluate. Academic: annotated literature review. Vocational: feasibility analysis or prototype documentation. Systems: causal loop diagram with leverage point analysis. Civic: policy brief or legislative draft.
Argument Peer critique session: present a 10-minute version of your defense to a 3-person peer panel. Peers complete a structured critique form (thesis clarity, evidence strength, steelman quality, visual utility, delivery). Author takes written notes. Revise before final defense based on at least 2 specific critique points — document which critiques were incorporated and how.
Peer critique protocol: Critique panels must give at least one specific strength and two specific, actionable improvements. "Good job" and "could be better" are not acceptable feedback. Panels are assessed on the quality of their critique, not just authors on the quality of their defense.

Track Deliverable Options

Track Deliverable Artifact Minimum Standard
Academic Annotated literature review 8+ sources, each with methodology note and connection to thesis
Vocational Feasibility analysis or prototype documentation Cost estimate with sources, implementation timeline, at least one constraint identified
Systems Causal loop diagram + leverage point analysis At least 6 variables, 2 feedback loops identified, 1 high-leverage intervention proposed with rationale
Civic Policy brief or legislative draft Problem statement, proposed intervention, implementation mechanism, anticipated opposition addressed

Week 5 · Final Rehearsal + Public Defense

The public defense is not a performance — it is a demonstration of thinking under pressure. The panel asks genuine questions. The student has not seen them in advance. The goal is not a flawless recitation but a coherent, honest, responsive mind in action. The student who says "that's a genuine limitation of my argument — here is how I would address it with more time" earns more credibility than one who deflects or bluffs.

Week 5 · Five Lenses

Lens This Week's Work
Writing Final thesis document submitted Monday (no edits after submission). Self-assessment submitted same day: 3 things you are confident in, 2 things you would strengthen with more time, 1 thing you learned about how you argue that you did not know when you started.
Math / Logic Rehearsal with timed sections: 15 minutes for presentation, 10 minutes for Q&A. Practice concise quantitative explanations — state the finding, state the uncertainty, stop. Panelists will probe any statistic you mention; know your numbers.
Visualization Final visual rehearsal: walk through each visual aloud without reading the caption. Can you explain what it shows and why it matters without looking at the text? If not, either simplify the visual or practice more.
Project Deliverable artifact is submitted with the final document. It is presented during the defense as evidence of track-depth engagement. Panelists may ask about any element of it.
Argument Live public defense: 15-minute presentation, 10-minute Q&A with a panel of 3 (instructor + 1 peer reviewer from outside your class + 1 community member or subject expert). Panel evaluates across the five defense pillars rubric. Student earns a "Defense Certified" designation upon passing.
Panel composition note: The third panelist — a community member or subject expert — is not there to be adversarial. They are there to model what authentic intellectual engagement with ideas looks like outside the classroom. Their questions are the ones that matter most.

Defense Day Timeline

Time Activity
0:00–0:15 Presentation: thesis, evidence, counterargument response, limitations, conclusion
0:15–0:25 Panel Q&A: genuine questions, no advance notice
0:25–0:30 Panel deliberation (student waits outside)

Capstone Assessment Rubric

The rubric maps directly to the five defense pillars. Each pillar is scored 1–4. A score of 3 or higher on all five pillars earns the "Defense Certified" designation. A score of 2 on any pillar requires a targeted revision and re-defense of that section within two weeks.

Pillar 4 · Exemplary 3 · Proficient 2 · Developing 1 · Beginning
Written Document Precise claim, airtight structure, sources cited with methodology notes, limitations honest and specific Clear claim, logical structure, sources cited, limitations acknowledged Claim present but vague; structure has gaps; some sources uncited Claim unclear; structure absent; sources missing
Quantitative Support Data analyzed with uncertainty stated; misleading framings actively avoided and named Data cited correctly; uncertainty acknowledged; no obvious misrepresentation Data cited but uncertainty not addressed; one misleading framing present No quantitative evidence; or data misrepresented without acknowledgment
Visual Evidence Visuals add information not in prose; fully labeled; captions direct attention precisely Visuals support argument; labeled; captions present Visuals present but redundant or incomplete labels No visuals; or visuals actively mislead
Deliverable Artifact Artifact exceeds track minimum; demonstrates depth of engagement; panel can probe any element Artifact meets track minimum; demonstrates competent engagement Artifact present but below track minimum standard Artifact missing or does not connect to thesis
Live Oral Defense Thesis stated precisely; opposition steelmanned; panel questions engaged honestly; qualifiers used; "I don't know" used appropriately Thesis clear; main opposition addressed; most panel questions answered; qualifiers present Thesis stated but drifts; strawman used for opposition; some panel questions deflected Thesis unclear; opposition ignored or strawmanned; panel questions deflected or bluffed
On "I don't know": A student who says "I don't know, but here is how I would find out" is demonstrating exactly the epistemic honesty the framework has been building toward since Pre-K. It is not a weakness. Bluffing is. Panelists are instructed to mark deflection down and honest uncertainty up.
The curriculum's promise, kept: Every tool the student uses in the Capstone Defense — the source checklist, the Toulmin structure, the steelman, the fallacy screen, the statistical skepticism, the causal diagram — was introduced in an earlier grade as a small, specific skill. The defense is not a new challenge. It is the integration of thirteen years of practice in a single, high-stakes demonstration.
