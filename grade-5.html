Phase One · Grade 5

The Bridge Year

Everything built in Grades 1–4 is assembled into one coherent toolkit.

Grade 5 is where the pieces come together. The claim-reason-evidence structure from Grade 1, the source evaluation from Grade 3, the counterargument from Grade 4, the Boolean logic from Grades 2 and 3 — all of these are unified into the beginning of the Toulmin Model.

This is also the year the Two-Keys Portfolio architecture is introduced in age-appropriate terms. Students learn that their thinking process is private (Development Key) and that what they can demonstrate is what gets shared (Credential Key). The right to fail and grow in private is named explicitly for the first time.

Core Domain · Logic

The Toulmin Model — Introduction

The argument structure that Grade 7 will formalize — introduced here in plain language.

What Students Practice

  • Claim: The position you are defending
  • Grounds: The evidence that supports the claim
  • Warrant: The reasoning that connects the evidence to the claim — the 'because this matters' layer
  • Rebuttal: The strongest objection — and your response to it
  • Drawing a simple 4-box Toulmin diagram for a familiar argument
  • Identifying which box is missing from a given argument — the weakest link

The warrant is the hardest part. Students often give a claim and evidence but skip the warrant — the explanation of why this evidence supports this claim. Finding the missing warrant is the most important analytical skill Grade 5 develops.

Core Domain · Research

Basic Research Protocol

Finding sources, evaluating them, and citing them — the first formal research process.

What Students Practice

  • Finding three sources on a topic and evaluating each using the three-question method from Grade 3
  • Identifying primary vs secondary sources and knowing when each is appropriate
  • Paraphrasing vs quoting — using someone else's idea without stealing their words
  • Simple citation: author, title, where it appeared, when
  • Triangulation: if two independent sources say the same thing, the claim is stronger
Core Domain · Media Literacy

How Presentation Creates Meaning

The same facts, presented differently, produce different beliefs.

What Students Practice

  • Headline framing: comparing headlines about the same event from different outlets
  • Photo selection: which image was chosen to accompany a story, and what impression does it create?
  • What is omitted: the information that isn't in the story — and why it might matter
  • The question: 'What do they want me to feel?' asked about every media artifact encountered
Core Domain · Fallacies

Six Fallacies Mastered

Appeal to Popularity, Ad Hominem, Strawman, False Dichotomy, Slippery Slope, Anecdotal Evidence.

All six fallacies from the Phase One sequence are now practiced in combination. Grade 5 introduces fallacy stacking — arguments that use multiple fallacies simultaneously. Students learn to identify all the problems in a single flawed argument.

New Practice This Year

  • Fallacy stacking: finding multiple fallacies in a single argument
  • Fallacy Log: students document real-world examples they encounter during the year
  • Distinguishing legitimate uses of emotion in argument (pathos) from manipulative ones
The Two-Keys System

Portfolio Foundation

The right to fail and grow in private — introduced in plain language.

What Students Learn

  • Development Key (private): 'Your thinking process — your drafts, your failed attempts, your journal — belongs to you. It is private.'
  • Credential Key (public): 'What you can demonstrate is what gets shared. Not how long it took.'
  • Students begin curating a portfolio: not just correct work, but work that shows their thinking process
  • The capstone defense: presenting and explaining their best argument from the year to an audience
  • 'Changing your mind is a sign of thinking.' The Thinking Journal is reviewed and the student reflects on what changed across the year.

This is the last year without tracks. From Grade 6 onward, students begin discovering which kinds of thinking feel most alive to them. Grade 5 ensures every student arrives at that exploration with the same foundational toolkit.

Operational Layer · Grade 5

Evaluation Primitives

Each capability is only real when it can be demonstrated on an unfamiliar problem under no-hint conditions.

Task 1 · Toulmin Construction

Setup: Student receives an unfamiliar, non-rehearsed topic and 20 minutes to write a complete 4-part Toulmin argument.

  • Pass: All four components present. Warrant explicitly bridges evidence to claim — not a restatement. Rebuttal addresses a genuine objection, not a strawman.
  • Fail: Warrant restates the claim in different words. Rebuttal is "but I still think I'm right." Evidence is purely anecdotal with no attempt at verification.
  • Failure mode to watch: Students who have memorized the four-box template but fill it with circular reasoning — the warrant says the same thing as the claim.

Task 2 · Fallacy Stack Detection

Setup: Student reads a 200-word argument containing exactly 3 unlabeled fallacies. Task: identify each, name it, and explain the structural flaw.

  • Pass: Identifies ≥2 fallacies with correct names and a structural explanation of why the reasoning fails — not just "that's wrong."
  • Fail: Names fallacies without explaining the structural flaw. Marks emotional language as a fallacy without explaining why it is manipulative in this context.
  • Failure mode to watch: Students who recognize the word "everyone" and label it Appeal to Popularity without checking whether the argument actually depends on that move.

Task 3 · Source Triangulation

Setup: Three sources on the same topic — one credible, one biased, one unreliable. Student ranks them and justifies the ranking using the 3-question protocol (who said it, why they said it, when).

  • Pass: Correctly identifies the weakest source. Justification references source criteria, not surface features (length, familiarity of name, professional-looking layout).
  • Fail: Ranks by website appearance, article length, or author prestige without checking for conflicts of interest or verifiability of claims.
  • Failure mode to watch: Students who defer to whichever source sounds most authoritative — authority signal without evaluation.

Task 4 · Media Framing Analysis

Setup: Two headlines about the same verified event from different outlets. Student explains the intended emotional effect of each and identifies what information is likely omitted.

  • Pass: Identifies the emotional target of each headline. Names at least one piece of omitted context. Does not conclude one headline is "true" and the other "false" — correctly treats this as a framing difference, not a factual contradiction.
  • Fail: Says one headline is correct and the other is lying. Cannot name what might be left out. Confuses tone with accuracy.
  • Failure mode to watch: Students who identify framing in headlines they already disagree with, but not in headlines that confirm their existing view.
Operational Layer · Grade 5

Gating Criteria

What a student must demonstrate — not just complete — before advancing to Grade 6.

Required Demonstrations

  • Complete Evaluation Tasks 1–4 at passing level on unfamiliar material (not material covered in class that year)
  • Produce a Thinking Journal with at least one entry per week — content private, existence and volume verifiable
  • Submit a Fallacy Log with ≥10 real-world examples documented from outside the classroom (news, conversation, advertising)
  • Deliver the year-end portfolio review distinguishing Development Key (private process) from Credential Key (demonstrated mastery)

What Does Not Count as Mastery

  • Completing a worksheet correctly — worksheets rehearse, they do not test transfer
  • Defining a fallacy from memory — recognition on a novel argument is the standard
  • Scoring well on a multiple-choice test — multiple choice rewards pattern matching, not structural understanding
  • Group work where individual contribution cannot be separated

The gating question: Can this student do these things on a problem they have never seen before? If yes: advance. If no: more repetition on the gap, not the whole year.

Operational Layer · Grade 5

Adversarial Exercises

Sovereignty requires training against the actual conditions that undermine independent thinking.

Exercise 1 · The Authority Signal

Setup: Present a claim attributed to "a leading professor at a major university" — no other credentials given. Ask: do you believe this?

Correct response: Apply the source protocol. Who specifically? Expertise in what field, exactly? Does their professional role create a conflict of interest? A title is not evidence — it is a signal that triggers deference. The goal is to notice the deference reflex and override it with evaluation.

What this trains: Separating the claim from the authority signal. Recognizing that an authoritative tone is a presentation choice, not a quality of the underlying reasoning.

Exercise 2 · The Emotional Trap

Setup: Present a fundraising appeal or news story that uses a real problem but omits statistics that would change the reader's response (e.g., describes individual suffering without noting prevalence or trend data).

Student identifies: what emotion is being targeted, what numerical or contextual information is missing, and whether the core claim would still hold with the full context. The point is not that emotion is invalid — it is that emotion can be used to bypass the evaluation step.

What this trains: Asking "what am I not being shown?" as a reflex, not just a technique.

Exercise 3 · Source Conflict

Setup: Two credible-seeming sources that directly contradict each other on a factual claim. Student must determine which is more reliable — or conclude that they cannot determine from available information — and explain the reasoning.

No hedging accepted: "both are probably a little right" is not an answer. The student must engage the structural question: what would need to be true for each source to be correct? Which is more falsifiable? Which has the stronger incentive to be accurate?

What this trains: Tolerating genuine uncertainty without defaulting to false balance or deference to whichever source sounds more confident.

Why adversarial training matters: A student who can construct a perfect Toulmin argument in a safe classroom may still defer to social pressure, authority signals, or emotionally resonant framing in real conditions. Sovereignty is not just knowing the tools — it is using them when they are inconvenient.

What Grade 5 Builds

Ready for Phase Two

  • 1
    Can construct and explain a 4-part Toulmin argumentClaim, Grounds, Warrant, Rebuttal — each component identified and defended.
  • 2
    Can conduct basic research using three sourcesFind, evaluate, paraphrase, cite — a complete research micro-cycle.
  • 3
    Recognizes six fallacies and can find multiple in one argumentIncluding fallacy stacking — arguments with multiple simultaneous flaws.
  • 4
    Can analyze a media artifact for framing and omission'What do they want me to feel?' applied consistently to headlines, photos, and stories.
  • 5
    Understands and accepts the Two-Keys Portfolio modelThe distinction between private thinking process and public demonstrated mastery.
