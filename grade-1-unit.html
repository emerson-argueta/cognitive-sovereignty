Tier 1
Why Do Plants Grow Toward the Light?

Plants growing toward the light — phototropism — is the perfect Tier 1 Core Problem. It is visible, reproducible, observable over days, and generates genuine questions that only evidence can answer. Most importantly, it demands the three-part argument structure: a claim about what the plant is doing, a reason why, and evidence the child can point to directly.

Unit Design · Tier 1

What This Unit Builds

Five weeks of growing a plant, watching it move, and building an argument about what is happening and why.

Each child or pair plants fast-germinating seeds (radish or bean) in a small cup on Day 1 of Week 1. The plant grows throughout the unit — it is live evidence. By Week 3, plants placed near a window are visibly leaning toward the light. By Week 4, plants in covered boxes with a side opening have grown toward the single opening. The argument the children build is not about abstract facts — it is about something they grew and watched and can point to.

The Claim → Reason → Evidence structure is introduced in Week 1 using a non-threatening example (a character from a story), practiced in Weeks 2–3 with direct plant observations, deepened in Week 4 with an if/then experiment, and demonstrated independently in Week 5 on a new unfamiliar plant scenario.

What you will see by Week 5: Children who spontaneously produce three-part argument sentences when they notice something — not just during structured activities. Children whose Thinking Journal entries include a claim and a reason. Children who notice when a classmate's explanation is missing its "because." And plants that have bent measurably toward a light source.

Week 1 · Tier 1 Unit

What Do Plants Need? — Setting Up the Claim

Plant the seeds. Begin observing. Introduce Claim → Reason → Evidence with a story before applying it to the plants.

LensDaily FocusWhat Students DoReasoning Connection
Literacy & WritingClaim sentences from a storyTeacher reads a short picture book about a plant or garden. Students identify: "What did the main character think? Why did they think it? How did they know?" Teacher models: "The character claims ___ because ___, and they know this because ___." Students write one claim sentence about the story using "because."Using fiction to introduce argument structure removes the cognitive load of thinking about evidence while also trying to understand a new structure. Once the structure is familiar, apply it to the real plants.
MathematicsMeasuring and countingStudents measure the depth they plant their seed (2 cm) using a ruler. They count days on a calendar and predict: "I think the plant will sprout in ___ days because ___." Teacher writes predictions on chart paper.Measurement introduces precision — the opposite of vague claims. A plant that is 4 cm tall is different from one that is "big." Precise observation is a prerequisite for precise argument.
VisualizationArgument map — first useTeacher draws a three-box argument map on the board: CLAIM → REASON → EVIDENCE. Uses the story claim to fill it in. Students copy the map structure into their Thinking Journals with the story argument filled in.The visual map makes the structure external and inspectable. Students can point to it. They can check: is every box filled in? Does the arrow go in the right direction?
Project WorkPlant setupEach student plants seeds in a labeled cup, records: date planted, seed type, depth, location (window vs. no window — two experimental conditions set up by teacher). Students draw their cup in the Observation Log section of their Thinking Journal.Setting up an experiment with two conditions (light vs. no light) is the physical enactment of an if/then question: "If a plant gets light, then it will grow toward it." The experiment exists before the children know that is what they are doing.
Argument/ReasoningWhat do plants need?Class brainstorm: "What does a plant need to grow?" Students share. Teacher writes answers. For each answer, teacher asks: "How do you know? Is that a guess or did you see it somewhere?" Students distinguish between things they observed and things they heard.Distinguishing personal observation from received information is the first step in evidence evaluation. "My mom told me" and "I watched it happen" are different kinds of knowing.

By end of Week 1

  • Seeds are planted in two conditions: window and no-window
  • Each student has written one "I think X because Y" sentence about the story
  • Has drawn the three-box argument map in their Thinking Journal
  • Has made a sprouting prediction with a because sentence — recorded on the class chart
Week 2 · Tier 1 Unit

What Did We Observe? — First Plant Arguments

The plants have sprouted (or are sprouting). Students make their first claims from direct observation.

LensDaily FocusWhat Students DoReasoning Connection
Literacy & WritingObservation sentencesStudents write daily observations in their Thinking Journals: "I observe that ___ because I can see ___." Teacher distinguishes: "I observe" means you saw it directly, not that you heard it or guessed. Students practice distinguishing observation from inference.Observation language ("I see," "I notice," "I measured") vs. inference language ("I think," "I believe") is the foundation of evidence-based argument. A claim supported only by "I think" is weaker than one supported by "I measured."
MathematicsDaily height measurementStudents measure plant height in cm every morning and record in a simple table: Day | Height (cm) | Drawing. By end of week, they have five data points. "Which plant is growing faster — window or no-window?"A table of measurements is evidence. Students are building the evidentiary foundation for the argument they will construct in Week 3.
VisualizationGrowth chartStudents plot their five measurements on a simple bar chart. Two bars side by side: window plant, no-window plant. "What does the chart show? What does it not show?"A chart is a visual argument. It shows the difference at a glance. But students must also articulate what the chart does not show — it shows height, not direction of growth. Teaching students to name the limits of evidence is as important as teaching them to use it.
Project WorkFirst plant argumentStudents fill in a three-box argument map about their plant: CLAIM ("My plant is growing toward the window") → REASON ("because plants need light") → EVIDENCE ("I can see it is leaning — I measured 3 cm on the window side and 1 cm on the dark side"). Teacher circulates and asks: "How do you know your reason is right?"The "how do you know your reason is right?" question is the critical move that distinguishes a reason from a guess. A reason that cannot be supported by evidence is an assumption. This is the Tier 1 version of source evaluation.
Argument/ReasoningGap detection practiceTeacher reads two student-level arguments aloud (anonymized). One is complete: claim + reason + evidence. One is missing its evidence. Class identifies: "Which one is complete? What is missing from the second one?" Focus: the missing evidence makes the argument weaker, not wrong — but we can't tell if it's right without it.Gap detection at Tier 1: "they said what they think but didn't show how they know." This is the same skill as the Pre-K "what's missing?" prompt, now formalized and applied to argument structure.

By end of Week 2

  • Has five days of plant height measurements in a table
  • Has plotted measurements on a simple bar chart
  • Has completed a three-box argument map about the plant
  • Has identified a missing piece of evidence in at least one argument
Week 3 · Tier 1 Unit

How Do We Know Our Evidence Is Good?

The plants are visibly leaning. Now: is "I can see it leaning" good enough evidence? What would make it better?

LensDaily FocusWhat Students DoReasoning Connection
Literacy & WritingStrong vs. weak evidenceTeacher provides two versions of the same argument: one with "I think the plant leans because I feel like it does," one with "I measured the plant leaning 15 degrees from vertical using a protractor drawing." Class votes: which evidence is stronger? Students write a sentence explaining why one is stronger.Evidence quality — not just evidence presence — is the focus this week. The distinction between feeling-based evidence and measurement-based evidence is the earliest version of empirical standards.
MathematicsMeasuring lean angleStudents hold a ruler against the base of their plant and estimate the angle of lean using a simple angle diagram (teacher-drawn: straight = 0°, slightly tilted = 15°, much tilted = 45°). Record in table. "Has the lean increased since Week 2?"Quantifying a qualitative observation ("it's leaning") makes the evidence more precise and harder to argue with. This is why scientists measure — not because numbers are magic but because numbers are harder to dispute than impressions.
VisualizationBefore-and-after argument mapStudents draw two argument maps side by side: Week 1 argument about the plant (prediction) vs. Week 3 argument (now based on measurements). Arrow between them labeled "now I have evidence." Students discuss: "How did your argument get stronger?"Arguments improve when evidence improves. Students who see this visually understand that building an argument is a process, not a single act.
Project WorkPhoto documentationStudents photograph or sketch their plants from the same angle on Days 1, 8, and 15 of the unit. Pin the three images in sequence. "What changed? What stayed the same? What would a person who had never seen the plant conclude from these three images?"Asking "what would someone else conclude?" introduces perspective — the idea that evidence must be readable by someone who wasn't there. This is the seed of communicable argument.
Argument/ReasoningThe confident claim exerciseTeacher (or a puppet) states confidently: "Plants don't really grow toward the light. They just grow upward because of gravity." Students respond: "That's a claim. Do they have a reason? Evidence?" Class evaluates using the three-box map. Students check their own evidence against the alternative claim.The confident claim exercise from Tier 1's adversarial section is embedded here: confidence is not evidence. A competing claim forces students to evaluate whether their own evidence is strong enough to prefer their conclusion over the alternative.

By end of Week 3

  • Can explain why measurement-based evidence is stronger than impression-based evidence
  • Has measured plant lean angle and recorded the change over time
  • Has drawn a before-and-after argument map showing argument improvement
  • Has evaluated a competing claim against their own evidence
Week 4 · Tier 1 Unit

What If We Changed the Light Direction? — If/Then

A new condition. Students predict using if/then, then observe the result.

LensDaily FocusWhat Students DoReasoning Connection
Literacy & WritingIf/then writingStudents write: "If we move the light to the other side, then the plant will ___ because ___." Predictions posted on the board. At end of week, students write: "I predicted ___ and the plant ___. I was right/wrong because ___."Writing a prediction before the experiment and then evaluating it afterward is the simplest form of the scientific method. The post-experiment sentence is also a reflection on reasoning quality — an early metacognitive move.
MathematicsDirection trackingStudents use a compass card (N/S/E/W) to record which direction their plant is leaning each day this week. When the light source is moved, they record when the lean begins to change direction. "How many days did it take the plant to respond?"Time as a variable — how long before the plant responds — adds a quantitative dimension to the if/then experiment. Students learn that causes take time to produce effects, and that the delay itself is data.
VisualizationExperiment diagramStudents draw a top-down diagram of their cup showing: cup center, light source position (before and after), plant lean direction (before and after). Arrows show the change over time.The diagram makes the causal relationship spatial and visible. Students who struggle to write the argument often succeed with the diagram first — then the words follow.
Project WorkBox experimentFor students who finish early or work in pairs: a cardboard box with a single opening cut in one side. Plant placed inside. "I predict the plant will grow toward the opening because ___." Observe over two days.The box experiment is a more controlled version of the window observation — it removes competing variables (air, heat from the window) and isolates light direction as the single variable. Students do not need to know the word "variable" — they need to notice that only the light changed.
Argument/ReasoningRevised argumentStudents revise their Week 2 three-box argument map using the new evidence from Week 4. "Did the if/then experiment make your argument stronger or weaker? Why?" Students write a one-sentence reflection in their Thinking Journal.Revising an argument in response to new evidence is the core scientific and logical practice. Students who change their map when the evidence changes have understood the relationship between evidence and conclusion.

By end of Week 4

  • Has written and evaluated at least one if/then prediction about the plant
  • Has tracked plant lean direction change after light source was moved
  • Has drawn a diagram showing the experimental setup and result
  • Has revised the three-box argument map using new evidence
Week 5 · Tier 1 Unit

The Complete Argument — Transfer to a New Scenario

Independent demonstration. New plant. Same structure. Can students apply everything without the teacher scaffolding each step?

LensDaily FocusWhat Students DoReasoning Connection
Literacy & WritingIndependent argumentStudents receive a photograph of a different plant (not the one they grew) in an unfamiliar setting — a plant growing sideways out of a wall crack, reaching toward an opening. Task: "Write a complete three-part argument explaining what the plant is doing and why. Use everything you know." No teacher prompts during writing.Transfer to an unfamiliar case is the only valid test of understanding. If students can apply Claim → Reason → Evidence to a plant they have never seen, they understand the structure. If they can only apply it to the class experiment, they have memorized a specific case.
MathematicsGrowth summaryStudents compile their five weeks of measurement data into a single summary table. Calculate: total growth (cm), maximum lean angle, direction of lean. Write one number sentence showing the relationship between light exposure and growth.Summarizing data across a multi-week observation period is the first experience of longitudinal evidence — evidence built up over time, which is more convincing than a single measurement.
VisualizationComplete argument mapStudents draw their most complete argument map of the unit — the one that includes all the evidence from all five weeks. Title it: "Why plants grow toward the light." Post in the classroom for a gallery walk.The gallery walk lets students see each other's argument maps. Students look for: is every box filled? Is the evidence specific (measurements, dates) or vague ("I saw it")? This is peer evaluation of argument quality — a social form of argument critique.
Project WorkTeach backIn pairs: one student explains the whole unit's conclusion — why plants grow toward light — using only their own plants, journal, and argument map. The other student asks: "How do you know? Did you see that or did someone tell you?"The partner's questioning role directly enacts the Gap Detection skill. Being asked "how do you know?" by a peer is more memorable than being asked by a teacher — it is the social version of adversarial questioning.
Argument/ReasoningThinking Journal reflectionFinal Thinking Journal entry: "I used to think ___ about plants, but now I think ___ because ___." Students who have genuinely changed their understanding will write something different from their Week 1 prediction. Students who have not will need a follow-up conversation.The "I used to think / now I think" structure is the Tier 1 version of intellectual humility and belief revision. Changing your mind when evidence changes is the most important habit this curriculum builds.

By end of Week 5

  • Has written an independent three-part argument about an unfamiliar plant photograph
  • Has compiled five weeks of measurement data into a summary table
  • Has participated in a gallery walk evaluating classmates' argument maps
  • Has written a "I used to think / now I think" Thinking Journal entry
Unit Assessment · Tier 1

What Mastery Looks Like

Three tasks. All on unfamiliar material. Spontaneous application counts more than prompted performance.

Task 1 — Transfer Argument (Written)

  • Setup: Give the student a photograph of a new plant in an unusual situation (growing toward a window, growing sideways, etc.). Ask: "Write everything you can argue about why this plant looks the way it does."
  • Pass: Student produces a written argument with all three parts — a claim about what the plant is doing, a reason, and evidence they can point to in the photograph. Does not need to be scientifically precise, but must be structurally complete.
  • Fail: Student describes the plant ("it is green and tall") without making a claim. Makes a claim without a reason. Makes a claim and reason but does not point to evidence. These are the three failure modes — each indicates a specific gap in the structure.

Task 2 — Gap Detection (Oral)

  • Setup: Read the student a simple two-sentence argument: "The plant grew tall. It must have had lots of light." Ask: "Is anything missing from that argument?"
  • Pass: Student identifies that the connection between tall growth and light is not established — "they said the plant grew tall and then said it had light, but they didn't say how they know those things go together."
  • Fail: Student says "that sounds right" because the conclusion is plausible. Plausibility is not evidence. The structure is the problem, not the content.

Task 3 — If/Then on a New Scenario (Oral)

  • Setup: "If we put a plant in a completely dark room with no windows or lights, what would happen? How do you know?"
  • Pass: Student makes a prediction and connects it to the unit's findings — "because we saw that plants lean toward the light, so with no light at all..." Correct reasoning from evidence, even if the specific answer is imprecise.
  • Fail: Correct prediction without reasoning. Student says "it would die" but cannot connect the answer to what was observed in the experiment.
Gating Connection · Tier 1

How This Unit Connects to Gating Criteria

Gating Criteria Addressed

  • Produces a complete Claim → Reason → Evidence structure spontaneously — practiced every week, with Week 5's transfer task as the test of spontaneous application.
  • Identifies a missing reason in a confident-but-incomplete argument — the Week 3 confident claim exercise and Week 5 gap detection task directly target this.
  • Completes and explains an unfamiliar if/then rule — Week 4's if/then experiment and Week 5's dark-room question both test this in unfamiliar territory.
  • Draws a three-box argument map with correct labels and directed arrows — drawn and revised in Weeks 1, 2, 3, and 4; used in the Week 5 gallery walk.
  • Maintains the Thinking Journal through the year — daily journal entries throughout the unit, culminating in the "I used to think / now I think" final entry.

The Tier 1 gating question: Can this student produce a complete argument on a topic they have never studied before? The plant unit provides the practice; the transfer task provides the test.
