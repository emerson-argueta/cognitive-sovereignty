Grade 11
Does Technology Make Us More or Less Free?

Grade 11 is where tracks diverge sharply in content while remaining connected through a shared Core Problem. The freedom and technology question is chosen because it is genuinely different in each track's disciplinary vocabulary: a philosophical question of autonomy (academic), a practical question of tool dependency (vocational), a technical question of surveillance and algorithmic control (systems), and a legal question of rights architecture (civic). Each week focuses on one track's lens — but all students engage with all four.

Unit Design · Grade 11

What This Unit Builds

Five weeks at the intersection of freedom, technology, and four disciplinary traditions — each week led by one track, attended by all.

The unit structure is different from Grades 9 and 10. Rather than having all tracks engage the Core Problem simultaneously, each week is led by one track's disciplinary tools and perspective — with all students participating and engaging. Week 1 is the academic/philosophical track's territory: political philosophy from Mill to Foucault. Week 2 is the vocational track's territory: tool dependency in skilled trades. Week 3 is the systems track's territory: surveillance architectures and algorithmic control. Week 4 is the civic track's territory: digital rights legislation and constitutional frameworks.

Week 5 is the synthesis — where all four perspectives are brought to bear simultaneously on a specific technology policy question that requires insight from all four to answer well. The cross-track fluency requirement means students from all tracks must be able to engage at a substantive level in each week's discussion, not just as observers.

Cross-track fluency standard at Grade 11: Students from non-featured tracks are expected to engage substantively in each week's discussion — not at the depth of the featured track, but at the level of an intelligent, well-prepared non-specialist. The featured track goes deep; all other tracks must maintain fluency.

Week 1 · Academic Track Leads · All Tracks Participate

Philosophical Argument: Mill, Foucault, and the Concept of Freedom

Academic track drives the philosophical analysis. Other tracks engage as informed participants.

LensAcademic Track (Deep)Other Tracks (Fluent)Reasoning Connection
Grammar & WritingAcademic track reads primary sources: excerpts from Mill's On Liberty (harm principle as the limit of legitimate interference with freedom), Foucault's Discipline and Punish (panopticon as a technology of power). Write a 600-word analysis: "In what specific ways does algorithmic recommendation technology resemble the panopticon Foucault describes? Where does the analogy break down?"Other tracks read a 500-word summary of both texts and write a 200-word response: "From my track's perspective, the most important insight in these philosophical texts is ___. The question they raise that my track is best equipped to address is ___."Primary source engagement at Grade 11 is the academic track's distinctive standard. Other tracks engage with the ideas through synthesis — the difference is reading speed and textual depth, not the quality of engagement with the ideas.
MathematicsAcademic track: Quantify the surveillance state. Using available data: number of CCTV cameras per capita in three countries, percentage of internet traffic monitored, algorithmic decision-making in credit, criminal justice, and employment. Build an argument that combines the philosophical framework with the quantitative scale.All tracks: Review the same data and answer — does the scale change the moral analysis? Does it matter whether 10% or 90% of public space is surveilled?Connecting philosophical argument to empirical scale is a hybrid skill — it requires both the conceptual framework (what is freedom?) and the empirical precision (how much of it is constrained?). Scale can change the ethical valence of a practice.
VisualizationAcademic track: Draw a concept map of the philosophical tradition: negative liberty (freedom from interference) vs. positive liberty (freedom to achieve one's goals). Connect each concept to a specific technology scenario. Where does the negative/positive distinction produce different policy conclusions about the same technology?All tracks: Place three technology examples on the negative/positive liberty spectrum using the concept map. Discuss: which conception of freedom does your track most naturally use?The negative/positive liberty distinction produces different policy conclusions from the same premises — surveillance may threaten negative liberty while universal broadband access promotes positive liberty. Making this distinction visible allows students to see why people with the same values can reach different conclusions.
Project WorkAcademic track: Draft thesis for the unit's final argument — a sustained 1,200-word philosophical argument on whether a specific technology (social media algorithmic feeds, predictive policing, or GPS location tracking) is compatible with Mill's harm principle and Foucauldian analysis.All tracks: Prepare two substantive discussion questions for next week's vocational track session. Questions must engage the philosophical framework from this week in a way that connects to the vocational domain.Preparing cross-track questions is a fluency exercise: to ask a good question in another track's domain, you must understand both your own track's framework and the other track's concerns well enough to identify where they connect.
Argument/ReasoningAcademic track: Apply the Foucauldian framework to one specific technology company's behavioral design practices. Where specifically does the argument work? Where does it fail? Write an honest self-assessment of the framework's limits.All tracks: Read the academic track's framework summary and write: "The philosophical argument that most changes how I think about technology in my own track's context is ___. The philosophical question I cannot answer from my track's perspective alone is ___."Cross-track philosophical engagement at Grade 11: other tracks do not need to read Foucault in depth, but they need to understand the Panopticon argument well enough to assess whether it applies to surveillance systems they encounter in their own track's work.
Week 2 · Vocational Track Leads · All Tracks Participate

Tool Dependency in Skilled Trades: When Technology Constrains the Practitioner

Vocational track examines freedom through the lens of practitioner autonomy and tool dependency in skilled work.

LensVocational Track (Deep)Other Tracks (Fluent)Reasoning Connection
Grammar & WritingVocational track reads and discusses: Matthew Crawford's Shop Class as Soulcraft and Richard Sennett's The Craftsman. Core argument: skilled work requires a direct, unmediated engagement with physical reality that produces a specific kind of epistemic and personal freedom. Write: "In what ways does increasing computerization of skilled trades (CNC machining, GPS-guided construction, automated HVAC diagnostics) reduce or increase the freedom of the practitioner?"Other tracks read a 400-word summary. Write: "From my track's perspective, is practitioner autonomy a value that should be preserved as automation increases? What would my track sacrifice to preserve it, and what would it not sacrifice?"The vocational track's contribution to the freedom question is irreplaceable: only practitioners with experience of skilled physical work can assess whether digital mediation of that work genuinely constrains freedom or changes its character. Theory about this from the outside is structurally incomplete.
MathematicsVocational track: Analyze data on trade automation: which tasks in construction, electrical, and plumbing work are most vulnerable to automation? Least vulnerable? Build a risk matrix: task type × automation probability × estimated timeline × skill-level required for remaining work. "Does automation increase or decrease the cognitive demands on skilled tradespeople?"All tracks: Review the risk matrix. Discuss: does the data suggest that automation is reducing or increasing the freedom of tradespeople? What does your track predict about the long-term trajectory?Automation risk data makes the philosophical question about practitioner autonomy concrete and time-bound. The freedom question is not abstract — it is about specific tasks in specific trades over specific timelines, and the data shows a genuinely mixed picture.
VisualizationVocational track: Map the skill tree of a specific trade (e.g., electrical work) before and after computerization. Where does automation simplify work? Where does it create new skills requirements? Where does it remove the practitioner's ability to make independent judgments? Mark the "freedom nodes" — points where the practitioner's judgment is either preserved or replaced.All tracks: Use the skill tree to identify: in my track's context, where are the "freedom nodes" — the decision points where practitioner (or researcher, or engineer, or policy maker) autonomy is most at stake in relation to technology?The "freedom node" concept — decision points where human judgment is or could be replaced by automated decision-making — is a practical analytical tool that applies across all tracks. Identifying freedom nodes is the first step in designing automation systems that preserve appropriate human autonomy.
Project WorkVocational track: Apprenticeship reflection. Students in apprenticeship contexts write a 500-word reflection: "In my apprenticeship, where has technology increased my freedom as a practitioner? Where has it constrained it? What is at stake in those constraints?"All tracks: Research one example of technology that changed practitioner autonomy in a professional field other than skilled trades (medicine, law, journalism, teaching). Present a 3-minute summary.The apprenticeship reflection is available only to students already in apprenticeship contexts — a Grade 11 possibility for vocational track students. For other tracks, the parallel exercise maintains the same conceptual focus on practitioner autonomy in a different domain.
Argument/ReasoningVocational track: Write the strongest argument for preserving practitioner autonomy as automation increases — and then write the strongest counterargument. Which side is stronger given the evidence?All tracks: Prepare two discussion questions for next week's systems track session, connecting the practitioner autonomy question to surveillance and algorithmic control.The vocational track's argument for practitioner autonomy is not just a statement of preference — it is a claim about what skilled work requires for its own integrity, and about the epistemic losses that occur when direct engagement with physical reality is mediated by algorithmic interpretation.
Week 3 · Systems Track Leads · All Tracks Participate

Surveillance and Algorithmic Control: The Technical Architecture of Unfreedom

Systems track maps the technical mechanisms of surveillance and behavioral modification. Freedom becomes a systems design question.

LensSystems Track (Deep)Other Tracks (Fluent)Reasoning Connection
Grammar & WritingSystems track reads: Shoshana Zuboff's The Age of Surveillance Capitalism (excerpts) and technical documentation on recommendation algorithm design. Write: "Describe the specific technical mechanism by which a recommendation algorithm modifies user behavior without the user's awareness. At what point in this mechanism does freedom become constrained, and what specifically is constraining it?"Other tracks read a 500-word summary. Write: "From my track's perspective, which technical mechanism described poses the greatest threat to the freedom my track most values? What intervention would my track recommend?"The systems track's contribution is unique: it can describe the technical mechanism of behavioral modification with precision that philosophical analysis cannot provide. A philosophical critique of algorithmic control that does not understand how recommendation algorithms actually work is attacking a caricature, not the real system.
MathematicsSystems track: Analyze engagement optimization mathematically. An algorithm maximizes a metric (watch time, clicks, shares). Model: if engagement is maximized by showing progressively more extreme content, at what point does engagement optimization produce a filter bubble? Build a simplified model showing how an engagement-maximizing algorithm and a truth-seeking user reach different equilibria.All tracks: Review the model. Does engagement optimization constitute a constraint on freedom? Apply the Week 1 philosophical framework: which concept of freedom (negative/positive) is most threatened by this mechanism?The mathematical model makes the engagement optimization problem precise — it is not an accident or a side effect, it is the logical outcome of optimizing a specific metric. Students who understand the mathematical structure cannot be satisfied with "they should just try harder to be responsible."
VisualizationSystems track: Map the complete data flow in a modern social media platform: data collection → user modeling → content ranking → behavioral modification → more data collection (feedback loop). Label each stage: what data is collected? What model is updated? What behavior change is induced? Where in this loop could a freedom-preserving intervention be inserted?All tracks: Identify one point in the data flow diagram where their track's tools or perspective could most usefully intervene. Academic: where does the philosophical framework suggest the deepest intervention? Vocational: where is the data generated by skilled practitioners, and what would it mean to own it? Civic: where would regulatory intervention be most effective?The cross-track intervention question makes the data flow diagram a shared analytical tool — each track uses it to identify where their distinctive capability is most useful. This is the systems track's contribution to cross-track synthesis.
Project WorkSystems track: Design a privacy-preserving alternative architecture for one feature of a major platform (news feed ranking, search suggestion, or content recommendation). What technical constraints would need to be relaxed? What user experience tradeoffs would result?All tracks: Evaluate the alternative architecture from your track's perspective. Academic: is it more compatible with Mill's harm principle? Vocational: does it preserve practitioner/creator autonomy? Civic: would it satisfy current regulatory standards?Cross-track evaluation of a technical design proposal is the synthesis move: the systems track produces a design; other tracks evaluate it against their own standards. A design that satisfies the systems track's technical constraints but fails the civic track's regulatory standards is not a complete solution.
Argument/ReasoningSystems track: What does the technical architecture reveal about the philosophical claim? Write: "The technical facts about how recommendation algorithms work either confirm or undermine the philosophical arguments made in Week 1. Here is specifically where they confirm: ___. Here is where the technology does not fit the philosophical framework: ___."All tracks: Prepare discussion questions for Week 4's civic track session connecting surveillance architecture to constitutional and statutory rights frameworks.The "confirm or undermine" exercise is the systems track's contribution to cross-track argument evaluation — using technical facts to test philosophical claims. This is the reverse of Week 1, where philosophical frameworks were used to interpret technical facts.
Week 4 · Civic Track Leads · All Tracks Participate

Digital Rights Legislation: Freedom as a Legal Architecture

Civic track analyzes what existing law does and does not protect — and what a comprehensive digital rights framework would require.

LensCivic Track (Deep)Other Tracks (Fluent)Reasoning Connection
Grammar & WritingCivic track reads: EU GDPR (key provisions), California Consumer Privacy Act, and the proposed American Data Privacy Protection Act. Write: "What specific freedoms does each framework protect? What gap in protection does each leave? Draft three provisions that a comprehensive digital rights framework would include that current law does not — and justify each provision using the philosophical framework from Week 1."Other tracks read a 400-word summary of the three frameworks. Write: "From my track's perspective, which gap in current law is most consequential? Which provision would my track find most problematic to implement?"Drafting legal provisions — rather than just analyzing existing ones — is the civic track's high-level creative output. Students who can draft a provision and justify it using a philosophical framework are performing the actual work of legislative drafting, not just critiquing it.
MathematicsCivic track: Analyze the economic impact of GDPR compliance: average compliance cost for small, medium, and large companies; change in data-driven advertising revenue; effect on market concentration (did GDPR increase or decrease big tech dominance?). "Do the economic effects of privacy regulation support or undermine the freedom goals of the regulation?"All tracks: Review the economic analysis. Academic: does economic concentration undermine the political freedom the regulation was designed to protect? Systems: did GDPR create technical compliance requirements that favor large players over small ones? Vocational: what compliance burden did GDPR impose on small business owners?Unintended consequences of privacy regulation — including potential market concentration — are a genuine policy challenge. Students who can see how a well-designed freedom-protecting regulation can produce anti-competitive outcomes have understood that policy analysis requires more than identifying the intended benefit.
VisualizationCivic track: Draw the rights architecture for digital freedom: constitutional rights (4th Amendment search and seizure, 1st Amendment expression), statutory rights (GDPR, CCPA), common law rights (privacy torts), and the gaps between them. Map: where does the current architecture leave citizens unprotected? Where does protection overlap redundantly?All tracks: Use the rights architecture map. Identify: where in the architecture would your track's proposed intervention most effectively improve freedom protection? What type of protection (constitutional, statutory, regulatory, technical) would your track most naturally produce?The rights architecture map is the civic track's analytical contribution to the cross-track synthesis — it shows where legal protection is present, where it is absent, and where different types of protection (constitutional vs. technical vs. market) might substitute for each other.
Project WorkCivic track: Write a 600-word legislative brief advocating for one specific digital rights provision. Must include: the right being protected, the specific legal mechanism, the constitutional basis, three potential objections with responses, and the implementation cost estimate.All tracks: Review two civic track legislative briefs from different students. Write 150 words evaluating: is the proposed provision technically feasible (systems track)? Does it protect practitioner autonomy (vocational track)? Is it philosophically justified (academic track)?Cross-track peer evaluation of a legislative proposal is the Grade 11 version of the Grade 7 warrant attack — examining not whether the evidence is correct but whether the principle connecting evidence to conclusion is sound. "Is this philosophically justified?" is a warrant question applied to law.
Argument/ReasoningCivic track: What does the legal framework reveal about what freedom actually requires from institutions? Write: "The most important thing law can do to protect digital freedom is ___. The most important thing law cannot do — and what other mechanisms must substitute — is ___."All tracks: Final cross-track preparation for Week 5. Each student writes: "The specific synthesis question I want to address next week, at the intersection of all four tracks, is ___. The evidence from my track that is most important for answering it is ___."The preparation for Week 5 is structured: each student arrives with a specific synthesis question and their track's most important evidence. Week 5 is not a free-form discussion — it is a structured synthesis of four prepared tracks engaging a common question with their specific tools.
Week 5 · All Tracks · Cross-Track Synthesis

Does Technology Make Us More or Less Free? — The Integrated Answer

All four weeks brought to bear on a specific contemporary technology policy question. The answer requires all four tracks.

LensDaily FocusWhat All Students DoReasoning Connection
Grammar & WritingSynthesis questionClass selects one specific technology policy question for synthesis: "Should the EU AI Act's requirements for transparency in algorithmic decision-making be adopted in the US?" Each student writes a 400-word response using all four track perspectives. Must include: the philosophical basis (Week 1), the practitioner impact (Week 2), the technical feasibility (Week 3), and the legal mechanism (Week 4).A 400-word synthesis that genuinely integrates four disciplinary perspectives is harder than a 1,000-word single-track argument. The constraint forces prioritization: what is the one most important insight from each track? What can be omitted without losing the essential argument?
MathematicsIntegrated evidenceStudents compile the quantitative evidence from all four weeks: surveillance scale data (Week 1), automation risk matrix (Week 2), engagement optimization model (Week 3), GDPR compliance cost data (Week 4). Build a single integrated evidence table that addresses the synthesis question. "What does the combined quantitative picture say about whether the AI Act's transparency requirement would improve freedom?"An integrated quantitative evidence table from four disciplinary sources requires translation between different units, time horizons, and populations. The translation work reveals where disciplinary assumptions differ and where they conflict — which is the most important intellectual output of the synthesis.
VisualizationFinal synthesis mapStudents build a final synthesis map: the Core Problem at the center, four tracks arranged around it, with bidirectional arrows showing: what each track provides to the synthesis, what each track requires from others, and where genuine unresolved tensions remain. Label the tensions honestly — do not manufacture a false consensus.A synthesis map with labeled unresolved tensions is more intellectually honest than one that implies complete integration. Some questions cannot be answered from within any single track; some tensions between tracks are permanent rather than resolvable. Acknowledging this is the mature epistemic position of deep specialization.
Project WorkIndividual synthesis argumentFinal individual deliverable: a 600-word synthesis argument answering "Does technology make us more or less free?" The argument must acknowledge that the answer depends on: which technology, which conception of freedom, whose freedom, and what time horizon. Students take a specific position while explicitly acknowledging these dependencies.The dependencies clause — "the answer depends on which technology, which freedom, whose, and when" — is not a hedge; it is a philosophically mature statement about the structure of the question. A student who can specify the conditions under which their answer holds and the conditions under which it does not has answered the question more completely than one who gives an unqualified answer.
Argument/ReasoningGrade 12 preparationFinal Thinking Journal: "The question I want to answer in my Grade 12 Capstone that has been sharpened by this unit is ___. The track I am deepest in, and why I am confident it is the right one for me, is ___. The cross-track fluency I will most need to maintain — because my Capstone will require it — is ___."The Grade 12 preparation reflection makes the connection between Grade 11 synthesis and Grade 12 specialization explicit. Students who have sharpened their Capstone question through a unit that required them to engage the question from four perspectives have a more precise question — and a more honest awareness of its disciplinary dependencies — than they would have had from single-track specialization alone.

By end of Week 5

  • Has written a 400-word synthesis response integrating all four track perspectives
  • Has compiled an integrated evidence table from four disciplinary sources
  • Has built a synthesis map with labeled unresolved tensions
  • Has submitted a 600-word individual synthesis argument with explicit dependency conditions
  • Has written a Grade 12 preparation Thinking Journal entry sharpening the Capstone question
Unit Assessment · Grade 11

What Mastery Looks Like

Primary Track Depth

  • The student's track-leading week work — their primary contribution — is evaluated at near-professional standard: an academic track student's philosophical analysis is evaluated against the standards of undergraduate philosophy; a systems track student's technical architecture is evaluated against the standards of a junior software engineer's technical writing.
  • Cross-track fluency is evaluated at "intelligent non-specialist" standard: the student should be able to engage substantively with each other track's leading week — asking good questions, identifying genuine cross-track connections, and applying the other track's framework with reasonable accuracy.

Synthesis Argument Evaluation

  • Pass: The 600-word synthesis argument specifies which technology, which conception of freedom, whose freedom, and what time horizon — and takes a position that is defensible given these specifications. Does not overclaim a general answer to an inherently context-dependent question.
  • Fail: An eloquent general statement ("technology can both increase and decrease freedom depending on how it is used") that does not commit to a specific position or specify the conditions under which the argument holds. Generality is not synthesis.
Gating Connection · Grade 11

How This Unit Connects to Gating Criteria

Gating Criteria Addressed

  • Produces primary track work at near-professional standard — evaluated against track-appropriate criteria in the track-leading week.
  • Engages other tracks at intelligent non-specialist level — assessed through cross-track reflections and synthesis question contributions in each week.
  • Produces a synthesis argument that specifies conditions rather than making unqualified general claims — the 600-word synthesis deliverable in Week 5.
  • Has sharpened the Capstone question and identified its disciplinary dependencies — the Grade 12 preparation Thinking Journal entry.

The Grade 11 gating question — and the question that defines readiness for Grade 12: Can this student produce work in their chosen track at a standard that a professional in that field would recognize as serious — and articulate the specific question they will pursue in their Capstone with enough precision to begin the evidence audit?
