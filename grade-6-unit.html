Tier 6
Is Social Media Making Teenagers More Anxious?

Band II begins with the question that defines many of these students' lives. Social media and anxiety is not a hypothetical — it is their actual experience, mediated by claims they encounter daily in headlines, adult conversations, and their own internal states. This unit applies the Internal Heatmap protocol, a formal fallacy taxonomy, and study evaluation methodology to a question that is both personally charged and genuinely contested in the research literature.

Unit Design · Tier 6

What This Unit Builds

Five weeks formalizing what Band I introduced — with a domain where students have genuine personal stakes and strong emotional reactions.

The social media and anxiety question is chosen deliberately because students are not neutral about it. They have feelings about their own social media use. They have heard this claim from parents, teachers, and the media. Their emotional reaction to the question is itself data — and the first thing the Internal Heatmap protocol helps them process. Band II's defining move is applying analytical tools to emotionally charged material without either suppressing the emotion or being controlled by it.

The four fallacies formalized this week — post hoc ergo propter hoc, hasty generalization, appeal to emotion, and false dichotomy — were encountered informally in Band I. They are now named, defined, and practiced as a taxonomy. Naming them is not the goal; being able to identify the specific logical error each represents — and explain it in plain language — is the goal.

What you will see by Week 5: Students who pause before reacting to an alarming headline and apply the Heatmap protocol — noticing their emotional response, naming it, and separating it from their analytical evaluation. Students who can name the specific fallacy in a claim about social media without needing a list in front of them. Students who read a study abstract and immediately ask: what was the sample? Was it correlational or experimental?

Week 1 · Tier 6 Unit

What Does Your Gut Say — and How Do You Know?

The Internal Heatmap protocol introduced. Feelings as the first data point — not to be ignored, not to be obeyed.

LensDaily FocusWhat Students DoReasoning Connection
Literacy & WritingReading a sensational headlineTeacher displays: "STUDY: Social Media Is Destroying Teenagers' Mental Health." Students record their immediate reaction in writing — before any analysis. Then: "Is your reaction based on what the headline says, what you already believe, or something else? What is your reaction made of?"Separating the initial emotional response from the analytical response is the Heatmap's core move. Most people skip this separation — they respond to a headline based on how it makes them feel, then rationalize. Making the sequence visible and explicit is the first step to breaking it.
MathematicsWhat the headline's study actually saysStudents receive the (simplified) abstract of the study the headline claims to summarize. Key data: a survey of 5,000 teens; self-reported social media use; self-reported anxiety levels; correlation coefficient r = 0.17. Students calculate: what does r = 0.17 mean? (Using the Tier 4 correlation framework.) Does the headline match the data?r = 0.17 is a weak positive correlation — it explains about 3% of the variance in anxiety. The headline says "destroying." The gap between r = 0.17 and "destroying" is a measurable distortion. Students who can quantify that gap are immune to most science headlines.
VisualizationHeatmap protocol diagramStudents draw the Heatmap protocol as a four-step flowchart: (1) Notice your emotional reaction and name it. (2) Ask: what is causing this reaction — the evidence or your prior beliefs? (3) Separate the evidence from the feeling. (4) Evaluate the evidence as if you had no prior view. Post in Thinking Journal for reference throughout the unit.Making the protocol visual and personal — as a diagram in their own journal — makes it a tool, not a rule. Students who draw the protocol are more likely to remember it than students who read about it.
Project WorkPersonal experience inventoryStudents complete a private, non-shared inventory: "How many hours per day do I use social media? Do I feel anxious after using it? Do I feel better or worse about myself after scrolling? Have I ever compared myself to someone online and felt worse?" Students are not required to share — only to be honest with themselves.Personal data is data. Students who have reflected honestly on their own experience come to the research question with genuine curiosity rather than detached analysis. Their personal experience is one data point — not a representative sample, but a real one.
Argument/ReasoningPrior belief auditStudents write: "Before looking at any research, I believe that social media [does / does not] cause anxiety, with confidence level [1–5]. My belief comes from: ___." Record and date. This is the starting point for tracking belief change across the unit.Auditing prior beliefs before examining evidence is the cognitive equivalent of the Pre-Study Registration that scientists use to prevent post-hoc rationalization. Students who know what they believed before the evidence is presented cannot pretend they always believed what the evidence shows.

By end of Week 1

  • Has applied the Heatmap protocol to at least one sensational headline
  • Has calculated that the study's r = 0.17 explains approximately 3% of variance in anxiety
  • Has drawn the Heatmap protocol as a four-step diagram in their Thinking Journal
  • Has recorded a prior belief with confidence level and source
Week 2 · Tier 6 Unit

What Fallacies Are in This Headline?

Four fallacies formally named and practiced — applied to real social media discourse about teen anxiety.

LensDaily FocusWhat Students DoReasoning Connection
Literacy & WritingFallacy taxonomyStudents build a reference card for four fallacies with: name, definition in plain language, example from social media discourse, and the specific logical error: (1) Post hoc ergo propter hoc — "after this, therefore because of this" (correlation treated as causation); (2) Hasty generalization — "one study/one case proves everyone"; (3) Appeal to emotion — "this will make you feel afraid/outraged, so you should believe it"; (4) False dichotomy — "either ban social media or accept the consequences." Apply each to three social media headlines.Fallacy names are useful only insofar as they point to a specific logical error. Students who can name a fallacy but not explain the error are using a label as a substitute for analysis — the opposite of what this curriculum intends. The definition in plain language is as important as the name.
MathematicsHasty generalization — sample problemsStudents evaluate three claims about social media and anxiety based on their sample descriptions: (a) a study of 50 college students; (b) a study of 50,000 teens across 12 countries; (c) a parent's report about their own child. Questions: how large is the sample? Is it representative of the population the claim addresses? Can we generalize?Sample size and representativeness are the mathematical heart of the hasty generalization fallacy. A study of 50 college students says nothing about "teenagers." Students who can identify the mismatch between sample and claimed population are protected against the most common form of research overclaiming.
VisualizationFalse dichotomy diagramStudents draw the false dichotomy as a diagram: two extreme options presented as if they are the only choices, with the space of intermediate options erased. Then: "What are three options between 'ban social media' and 'do nothing'?" Students map the actual option space.False dichotomies are persuasive precisely because they erase the middle ground. Making the middle ground visible — diagrammatically, before arguing — breaks the rhetorical force of the false dichotomy and reveals where the real policy discussion lives.
Project WorkFallacy huntStudents collect five social media-related headlines or social media posts (teacher-provided examples to avoid sharing personal accounts). For each: identify which fallacy is present (if any) and explain the specific logical error in two sentences. Some headlines may have no fallacy — students must also identify clean claims.Not every claim contains a fallacy — students who see a fallacy everywhere are over-applying the taxonomy. Identifying "this claim is not fallacious because ___" is as important as identifying "this is fallacious because ___."
Argument/ReasoningAppeal to emotion — recognitionTeacher reads two descriptions of social media's effect on teens: one uses neutral, data-referenced language; one uses emotionally charged language ("our children are suffering," "epidemic of loneliness," "silent crisis"). Students evaluate: does the emotional language make the argument stronger or weaker? What is the difference between evidence that happens to be emotionally affecting and emotional language used as a substitute for evidence?The distinction between evidence that is emotionally affecting (legitimate) and emotional language used as evidence (fallacious) is one of the most important and subtle distinctions in this curriculum. Strong evidence can make you feel things; that is not the same as rhetoric that makes you feel things to substitute for evidence.

By end of Week 2

  • Has completed a fallacy reference card for all four fallacies with plain-language definitions
  • Has evaluated sample sizes from three studies and explained whether generalization is warranted
  • Has identified fallacies in five headlines — including correctly identifying at least one clean claim
  • Can explain the difference between emotionally affecting evidence and emotional rhetoric
Week 3 · Tier 6 Unit

How Do We Evaluate the Study Behind the Claim?

Moving from headlines to methods. Students read a simplified research abstract and evaluate its methodology.

LensDaily FocusWhat Students DoReasoning Connection
Literacy & WritingReading a study abstractStudents receive a simplified abstract of a real study on social media and anxiety (Amy Orben and Andrew Przybylski's 2019 specification curve analysis, simplified for Tier 6). Students annotate: methodology (survey), sample (large but correlational), key finding (small effect size, r ≈ 0.05–0.15 depending on specification), and the authors' own conclusion (effects are small and context-dependent).Reading the actual research — not a news article about the research — is the foundational media literacy skill of Band II. Most public discourse about science is about research, not the research itself. Students who can read a study abstract are two steps ahead of most news consumers.
MathematicsEffect size interpretationThe study reports effect sizes (r values). Students compare: social media's effect on anxiety (r ≈ 0.05–0.15) to other factors: sleep deprivation (r ≈ 0.4), exercise (r ≈ −0.3), poverty (r ≈ 0.35). "If social media has r = 0.10 and sleep deprivation has r = 0.40, what does that tell us about relative importance? Why might media coverage focus on social media rather than sleep?"Comparing effect sizes reveals relative importance. Social media's association with anxiety is real but small compared to other factors. Students who can make this comparison can evaluate whether a real effect is practically important — a distinction that matters enormously in public health and policy.
VisualizationStudy evaluation rubricStudents build a study evaluation rubric with five criteria: (1) Sample size and representativeness, (2) Correlational or experimental design, (3) Effect size (small/medium/large), (4) Replication by other researchers, (5) Authors' own stated limitations. Apply to two studies. Which study is stronger evidence? Why?A study evaluation rubric gives students a systematic way to compare research quality. Without a rubric, students default to accepting whatever study supports their prior belief — the confirmation bias version of source evaluation. The rubric makes quality assessment independent of conclusion preference.
Project WorkConfirmation bias auditStudents receive eight study summaries on social media and anxiety — four showing significant effects, four showing minimal effects. They read all eight and write: "Which four would a person who already believed social media is harmful tend to cite? Which four would a person who believed it is harmless cite? How would you cite all eight honestly?"Confirmation bias at the research level — citing only studies that support your position — is called cherry-picking, and it is one of the most common forms of dishonest argument in public discourse. Students who can identify the cherry-picked version of an argument can also construct the honest version.
Argument/ReasoningWhat does the research actually support?Students write a one-paragraph summary of what the research on social media and anxiety actually shows — citing the full range of studies, using accurate effect size language, and including a qualifier: "The research suggests a small correlation between heavy social media use and increased anxiety in some populations, with effect sizes typically ranging from r = 0.05 to r = 0.15 — smaller than the effects of sleep deprivation and poverty."This summary paragraph is the honest version of the Week 1 headline. The gap between the two — "destroying mental health" vs. "small correlation in some populations" — is the measure of what students have learned to resist.

By end of Week 3

  • Has annotated a research abstract and identified methodology, sample, and effect size
  • Can compare effect sizes across different factors and explain what the comparison means
  • Has applied the five-criterion study evaluation rubric to two studies
  • Has written an honest research summary that cites the full range of findings
Week 4 · Tier 6 Unit

What Does a Well-Constructed Argument About This Look Like?

Building the full argument — Toulmin structure, honest effect sizes, fallacy-free language, appropriate qualifiers.

LensDaily FocusWhat Students DoReasoning Connection
Literacy & WritingArgument constructionStudents write a full Toulmin argument on one of three positions: (1) The evidence justifies concern about social media and teen anxiety; (2) The evidence does not justify major policy action; (3) The evidence is insufficient to draw conclusions and more research is needed. All three positions are defensible given the research — students must choose based on their honest reading of the evidence and argue accordingly.Offering three defensible positions rather than a binary removes the pressure to pick a side and instead asks students to calibrate their position to the evidence. Position 3 — "we don't know enough" — is often the most honest and the hardest to defend, because it feels like avoiding the question. It is not; it is proportionate epistemic humility.
MathematicsQuantifying the claimStudents include at least two specific effect sizes and one sample size in their argument. These must be accurate and contextualized — not "the correlation was significant" but "the correlation was r = 0.10, which explains approximately 1% of variance in anxiety levels, comparable to the effect of wearing glasses on academic performance."Specific numbers with context are the mathematical backbone of a strong argument about research findings. Vague quantitative language ("the effect was significant," "the sample was large") is accurate but uninformative. Precision is a virtue in argument as in measurement.
VisualizationArgument map with fallacy checkStudents draw their argument as a Toulmin diagram, then run a fallacy check: Does any part of my argument use post hoc reasoning? Am I making hasty generalizations from limited samples? Am I using emotional language as a substitute for evidence? Am I presenting a false dichotomy? Mark any problematic sections and revise.Self-auditing an argument for fallacies before presenting it is Band II's version of the Band I "argument map revision." The skill is the same — checking the argument's structure — but now applied to a complex research-based argument with specific named failure modes.
Project WorkGroup argument preparationStudents form groups of three, each having chosen a different position. Each student presents their argument to the group; the other two ask: "Where is the fallacy?" "What is the sample size?" "Does the evidence support the qualifier?" Groups prepare a joint presentation showing all three positions and where the evidence is genuinely uncertain.Presenting to peers who know the argument structure and the fallacy taxonomy is a high-stakes evaluation. Unlike presenting to a teacher, peers are equally equipped to find errors — which creates the social dynamic of genuine intellectual accountability.
Argument/ReasoningThe verification standardStudents answer: "What genuine evidence about social media and anxiety would look like, and where would I find it?" Criteria: longitudinal design (not one-time survey), experimental or quasi-experimental (not purely correlational), large and representative sample, pre-registered methodology, independently replicated. "Does such evidence currently exist? What does that tell us about how certain we should be?"The verification standard — asking what strong evidence would look like and whether it exists — is the Tier 6 version of the Tier 1 "what would prove this?" question, now applied to professional scientific research. Students who can describe the ideal evidence and compare it to what is available have a sophisticated understanding of how knowledge works.

By end of Week 4

  • Has written a full Toulmin argument with specific effect sizes and sample sizes cited
  • Has run a fallacy check on their own argument and revised any problematic sections
  • Has participated in group argument evaluation as both presenter and questioner
  • Has described what ideal evidence would look like and compared it to available evidence
Week 5 · Tier 6 Unit

Group Defense Presentation

Public presentation of all three positions. Class evaluates using the Heatmap protocol and the fallacy taxonomy.

LensDaily FocusWhat Students DoReasoning Connection
Literacy & WritingFinal written positionStudents write a final individual position statement: which of the three positions do they personally hold, and why? Must include: honest acknowledgment of what the evidence shows, the specific reason they chose this position, and at least one thing the evidence does not resolve. Cannot be a copy of their group's presentation — must be their own synthesis.The individual position statement after the group work ensures that students have internalized the argument, not just delivered a collectively assembled product. Group arguments can obscure individual understanding; this task makes individual understanding visible.
MathematicsConfidence rating updateStudents update their Week 1 belief confidence rating. Compare: Week 1 confidence level vs. Week 5 confidence level. Most students will find their confidence has decreased — they now know the question is more uncertain than they initially believed. "Is lower confidence a sign of learning or a sign of confusion?"Appropriate confidence decrease in response to genuine uncertainty is a sign of good epistemics, not of confusion. Students who become less certain because they understand the complexity better than they did at the start have learned something more important than a new conclusion.
VisualizationGroup defense presentationEach group of three presents: 10 minutes presenting all three positions (2–3 min each), 5 minutes of Q&A from the class. Class uses the Heatmap protocol during the presentation: What is my emotional reaction to this argument? Is it evidence-based or prior-belief-based? Class members write one Heatmap note during each presentation.The audience applying the Heatmap protocol while watching a presentation is the unit's highest-level integration. Students are simultaneously evaluating the argument's content (fallacies, evidence quality) and their own emotional responses — two layers of analysis running in parallel.
Project WorkPeer evaluationAfter each presentation, class members complete a brief evaluation form: (1) Did the presenters accurately represent the research? (2) Did they identify and avoid the four fallacies? (3) Did they use appropriate qualifiers? (4) What was the most convincing part of each position? (5) What was the weakest part?Peer evaluation at this level of specificity — tied to named criteria rather than vague impressions — builds both the ability to evaluate others' arguments and the ability to anticipate how one's own argument will be evaluated.
Argument/ReasoningBand II opening reflectionThinking Journal: "The Internal Heatmap protocol changes how I respond to alarming claims by ___. The fallacy I am most likely to commit myself is ___ because ___. The question I will always ask when reading about a new study is ___."Band II opening reflection grounds the unit in habits, not conclusions. The three prompts point to behavioral changes — using the Heatmap, monitoring for self-committed fallacies, automatically asking about study methodology — that will persist long after the specific content about social media is forgotten.

By end of Week 5

  • Has written an individual final position statement with honest acknowledgment of uncertainty
  • Has updated belief confidence and explained why confidence decreased (or changed)
  • Has participated in group defense presentation and peer evaluation
  • Has written a Band II opening Thinking Journal reflection on Heatmap and fallacy habits
Unit Assessment · Tier 6

What Mastery Looks Like

Heatmap Application — New Alarming Headline

  • Setup: Show an unfamiliar alarming headline on a different topic (e.g., "Smartphones linked to declining attention spans in children"). Ask: "Walk me through applying the Heatmap protocol to this headline."
  • Pass: Student names their emotional reaction, identifies what it is based on (prior belief vs. the headline's content), separates the feeling from the analysis, and then evaluates the claim analytically — asking for the study, the effect size, the sample. Does this as a sequence, not as a simultaneous blur.
  • Fail: Student goes directly to analysis without acknowledging the emotional layer, or goes directly to emotional reaction without any analytical evaluation. Both extremes miss the Heatmap's structure.

Fallacy Identification — Transfer Domain

  • Setup: Three short claims from a domain unrelated to social media. Ask: "Which fallacy does each commit?"
  • Pass: Correct identification of the fallacy AND a plain-language explanation of the specific logical error. The name alone is not sufficient.
Gating Connection · Tier 6

How This Unit Connects to Gating Criteria

Gating Criteria Addressed

  • Applies the Internal Heatmap protocol to an emotionally charged claim — practiced in Week 1 and Week 5; assessed in transfer task.
  • Identifies all four fallacies by name and plain-language explanation — built in Week 2, applied in Week 3 (cherry-picking) and Week 4 (self-audit).
  • Reads a research abstract and evaluates methodology, sample, and effect size — the central skill of Week 3, formalized in the study evaluation rubric.
  • Constructs a Toulmin argument that is fallacy-free and uses specific quantitative evidence — Week 4's argument construction task, evaluated by peers in Week 5.

The Tier 6 gating question: When confronted with an emotionally charged claim backed by research, can this student separate their emotional response from their analytical response, evaluate the research's methodology and effect size, and construct a position that is proportionate to what the evidence actually supports?
