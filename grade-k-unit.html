Why Does the Moon Look Different Every Night?

The moon is the perfect Kindergarten Core Problem: it is real, it is visible to every child, it changes in a predictable pattern, and it generates genuine wonder. This unit uses the moon's cycle to teach pattern recognition, story structure, cause-and-effect, and — for the first time — the word "because" in writing.

Unit Design · Kindergarten

What This Unit Builds

Five weeks building from observation to pattern to story to cause-and-effect to prediction.

Kindergartners are concrete thinkers who have already internalized story structure from years of picture books. Beginning · Middle · End is not an abstract concept to a five-year-old — it is the shape of every story they have ever heard. This unit exploits that prior knowledge: the moon's monthly cycle is a story with a beginning (new moon), a middle (waxing to full), and an end (waning back to dark). The same three-part structure children use to narrate a fairy tale also describes a recurring natural pattern.

By Week 5, children will be making predictions. "I think tomorrow the moon will look _____ because last week it looked _____ and it is getting bigger every night." That sentence — a claim, a reason, and a reference to observed evidence — is the Pre-K "because" sentence grown into something more complex. It is the direct precursor to Tier 1's Claim → Reason → Evidence structure.

What you will see by Week 5: Children who can sequence moon phase cards in order without help. Children who say "because" in complete sentences about what they observed. Children who predict what the moon will look like tonight and explain why. Children who connect their feeling about something ("I feel scared of the dark moon") to a reason ("because it is very dark outside").

Week 1 · Kindergarten Unit

What Have You Noticed About the Sky?

Start with what children already know and have observed.

LensDaily FocusWhat Children DoReasoning Connection
Story StructureSky storiesRead aloud a picture book involving the night sky (e.g., Papa, Please Get the Moon for Me by Eric Carle). After reading: "What happened at the beginning? The middle? The end?" Children retell using a three-part sequence card: Beginning · Middle · End drawn on paper.Sequence and story structure are the pre-literacy forms of logical order. Beginning-Middle-End is the earliest version of premise → argument → conclusion.
Pattern & SequenceMoon observation log startsEach child gets a Moon Journal — a folded paper booklet. Every morning this week, teacher shows a photograph of the moon from the previous night. Children draw what they see. By Friday, five drawings exist. "Do you notice anything?"Sustained observation over time — not a single snapshot — is the only way to discover a pattern. This is the Kindergarten version of the sit-spot practice.
DrawingMy night sky drawingChildren draw what the sky looks like at night from their own memory. Teacher asks: "Is the moon always the same shape when you see it?" Children add details from memory.Drawing from memory activates what the child already knows — and reveals gaps. A child who draws a circle moon every time has noticed the moon but not its changing shape.
Building & PlayMoon shape blocksLight table or dark paper with moon phase shape cutouts. Children arrange the shapes and notice which ones look familiar. Teacher: "Can you put them in order from the smallest to the biggest?"Physical manipulation of shapes before the concept is named lets children discover the pattern kinesthetically.
Because SentencesFeeling about the nightFeeling map check-in: "How do you feel about the dark? Show me on the map." Then: "Why do you feel that way? Try to say: I feel ___ because ___." Dictated sentences written on sticky notes and posted on the class feeling wall.Connecting feelings to reasons is the Kindergarten version of emotional logic. The feeling map grounds abstract emotion in visible, nameable states.

By end of Week 1

  • Has five moon drawings in their Moon Journal — one per school day
  • Can retell a story in beginning-middle-end order
  • Has said "I feel ___ because ___" at least once
  • Has noticed that the moon drawings are not all the same shape
Week 2 · Kindergarten Unit

What Is a Pattern?

The word "pattern" is named. Children learn to find the rule behind a repeating sequence.

LensDaily FocusWhat Children DoReasoning Connection
Story StructurePattern in storiesRead Goodnight Moon (Brown) or another book with a repeating structural pattern. "What happens every time the bunny looks at something? Does the author follow a pattern?" Children identify the repeated structure: look at thing → say goodnight → move on.Patterns in stories make the abstract concept of a pattern concrete in a familiar domain. Children can then look for the same phenomenon in the moon observations.
Pattern & SequenceMoon phase pattern namedTen days of Moon Journal drawings reviewed together. Teacher lays them out in order. "What do you notice? Does the moon get bigger and then smaller? Can you predict what it will look like in three more days?" Children draw a prediction in their journal.Making a prediction from a pattern is the bridge from observation to inference. "I think it will look like this because I can see it is getting bigger each night."
DrawingPattern stripChildren create a paper strip with eight boxes and draw the moon phase sequence from memory. They may refer to their journal. Teacher checks: is the pattern represented correctly? Is there a direction to the sequence?Reproducing a pattern from memory requires internalization — the child must hold the rule in mind, not just copy.
Building & PlayAB and ABC patterns firstBefore the moon pattern: warm up with bead stringing and block patterns (red-blue-red-blue). Teacher names: "This is a pattern. What is the rule?" Then: "Is the moon's change a pattern? What is the rule?"Connecting the moon's cycle to simpler, more obvious patterns helps children recognize that a pattern is defined by a repeating rule, not by visual simplicity.
Because SentencesPrediction sentences"I think the moon will look ___ tomorrow because ___." Children dictate or attempt to write the sentence. Teacher supports invented spelling.A prediction sentence is structurally identical to an argument. This week's oral sentence is the same move that becomes a written Claim → Reason sentence in Tier 1.

By end of Week 2

  • Can define "pattern" in their own words: "something that repeats" or "it keeps doing the same thing"
  • Has made at least one moon prediction and recorded it in the journal
  • Can sequence at least five moon phase drawings in correct order
  • Has completed a "because" prediction sentence
Week 3 · Kindergarten Unit

The Moon Has a Story

Applying story structure to the lunar cycle — beginning, middle, and end map onto new moon, full moon, and back again.

LensDaily FocusWhat Children DoReasoning Connection
Story StructureMoon as a characterChildren write (or dictate) a three-part story where the moon is the main character: Beginning (new moon — dark, small), Middle (waxing — growing bigger each night), End (full moon, then waning back). The "end" loops back to the beginning — teacher discusses: "Is this ending really an ending, or is it a new beginning?"Circular narrative structure introduces the idea of cycles — patterns that don't have a final stop. This is cognitively more complex than linear sequence and prepares children for the scientific concept of cyclical phenomena.
Pattern & SequenceCycle vs. sequenceTwo chart papers: one showing the moon sequence left to right (linear), one showing it as a circle (cycle). "Which picture is more true to how the moon actually works?" Children discuss.The distinction between a linear sequence and a cycle is a significant conceptual step. Cycles have no beginning or end — they are ongoing patterns. This is the earliest introduction to systems thinking.
DrawingMoon story bookletEach child creates a three-page mini-booklet: page 1 (new moon — beginning), page 2 (full moon — middle), page 3 (back to new — end/new beginning). Dictated text below each drawing.The booklet maps the story structure children know onto a natural phenomenon — externalizing the pattern as a narrative.
Building & PlayAct it outChildren act out the moon's cycle: start small (crouched down), slowly grow bigger (stand up, arms out), become the full moon (arms wide), then slowly shrink back. Teacher narrates: "Beginning... middle... end... and beginning again."Embodying the pattern reinforces the cycle concept kinesthetically. Movement is memory at this age.
Because SentencesStory feelingsFeeling map: "How does the new moon part of the story make you feel? How does the full moon part make you feel?" Then: "Why?" Children complete: "I feel ___ during the ___ moon because ___."Connecting story events to emotional responses and then explaining those emotions is the Kindergarten version of emotional logic — the same chain that Tier 1 formalizes in the Internal Heatmap precursor work.

By end of Week 3

  • Can describe the moon's cycle in beginning-middle-end structure
  • Can explain the difference between a sequence and a cycle in their own words
  • Has completed a three-page moon story booklet with dictated text
  • Has connected a feeling to a reason about a story event
Week 4 · Kindergarten Unit

What Causes the Moon to Change?

Introducing cause and effect — the moon doesn't change randomly. Something makes it happen.

LensDaily FocusWhat Children DoReasoning Connection
Story StructureBecause in storiesRe-read a familiar picture book. Teacher stops at key moments: "Why did that happen? What caused it?" Children identify cause-effect pairs from the story. Teacher writes on chart: "___ happened because ___."Identifying cause-effect pairs in fiction makes the logical structure visible before applying it to a scientific phenomenon.
Pattern & SequenceSimple model of moon phasesFlashlight and ball demonstration: teacher holds flashlight (sun), child holds ball (moon), class watches how the lit part of the ball changes as the child moves around. "What is causing the moon to look different? Is it the moon changing shape, or is it the light?"The model reveals that the moon's shape doesn't change — our view of the lit portion does. This is a significant conceptual distinction. Children who understand this have grasped that apparent change and actual change are different.
DrawingCause-effect diagramTwo-box diagram: CAUSE box → EFFECT box. Children draw: "The sun shines on the moon" → "We see different amounts of the lit side." Teacher scribes the because connection.The two-box diagram is the visual precursor to the three-box Claim → Reason → Evidence diagram introduced in Tier 1. Children are building the visual vocabulary of argumentation.
Building & PlayLight and shadow playDark corner with flashlights and various objects. Children explore: what makes a shadow bigger? Smaller? "What is causing the shadow to change?" Children notice that moving closer to the light makes the shadow bigger.Direct physical exploration of light behavior extends the conceptual model from the class demonstration to the child's own experiment.
Because SentencesScience because sentences"The moon looks like a crescent because ___." Children complete the sentence using what they learned from the flashlight demonstration. Multiple children share — teacher notes that different children give different but compatible answers.Multiple valid because-sentences about the same phenomenon introduce the idea that reasoning can be layered — there can be more than one "because" that is all true at once.

By end of Week 4

  • Can explain that the sun's light causes the moon to look different — in simple terms
  • Has drawn at least one cause-effect two-box diagram
  • Can distinguish between "the moon changes shape" (incorrect) and "we see different amounts of light" (correct)
  • Has completed a science "because" sentence from observation
Week 5 · Kindergarten Unit

What Will the Moon Look Like Tomorrow?

Prediction as the payoff. Pattern knowledge used to make a testable claim.

LensDaily FocusWhat Children DoReasoning Connection
Story StructureOur moon story — final draftChildren revise their three-page moon booklets from Week 3, now adding a "because" sentence to each page explaining why the moon looks as it does at that phase. Teacher confers individually.Revising writing is the earliest form of self-correction and argument improvement. Adding explanations to a narrative moves the booklet from description to reasoning.
Pattern & Sequence30-day prediction chartUsing the current moon phase, children fill in a prediction chart for the next 10 nights. They compare their predictions to actual observations (via teacher-provided daily photos). Where predictions were wrong: "Why were you wrong? What did you miss?"Testing a prediction and examining errors is the foundation of empirical reasoning. Being wrong is not a failure — it is data. This framing is established explicitly and maintained throughout all 13 grades.
DrawingMoon journal completeChildren review their full five-week journal. "What has changed in how you draw the moon from Week 1 to now?" Children write or dictate one observation about their own learning.Metacognitive observation — noticing change in one's own understanding — is introduced here for the first time. The Thinking Journal in Tier 1 formalizes this practice.
Building & PlayTeach it to someonePartner activity: one child "teaches" the moon cycle to another child using only the phase cutout shapes (no words from the teacher). The teaching child must explain the sequence and the cause.Teaching a concept consolidates understanding. If a child cannot explain the moon's cycle to a peer, they do not yet understand it — they have only memorized it.
Because SentencesFull prediction argument"I think the moon will look ___ tomorrow because I know it is ___ phase right now, and I have seen that the next phase is ___." This three-part sentence — prediction, current state, observed pattern — is the unit's culminating language goal.This sentence is structurally a complete argument: claim (prediction), reason (current state), evidence (observed pattern). It is the Kindergarten version of the Tier 1 Claim → Reason → Evidence structure.

By end of Week 5

  • Can sequence all eight moon phase cards in correct order without help
  • Has made at least three testable moon predictions and compared them to observations
  • Can explain why a prediction was wrong — pointing to what information was missing or misread
  • Has produced the full three-part prediction sentence: claim + reason + observed pattern
Unit Assessment · Kindergarten

What Mastery Looks Like

Three observable demonstrations on an unfamiliar problem.

Transfer Test — New Pattern

  • Setup: Show the child a simple unfamiliar sequence (e.g., a plant growing from seed to sprout to full plant to seed again). Ask: "Is this a pattern? How do you know? What will happen after the seed stage?"
  • Pass: Child identifies it as a cycle, explains that it repeats, and makes a prediction with a "because" sentence referencing the observed pattern.
  • Fail: Child correctly sequences the moon cards but cannot apply the pattern concept to the new example. This indicates memorization of the specific case, not transfer of the general principle.

Cause-Effect Check

  • Setup: Ask the child: "Why does the moon look different every night?" Accept a simplified but directionally correct answer. The child does not need to know the word "orbit."
  • Pass: Child references the sun's light and the moon's position — "because of where the moon is and where the sun shines." Does not need to be scientifically precise.
  • Fail: Child says "because it changes" (circular), "because it gets bigger and smaller" (describes the effect, not the cause), or "I don't know."

Prediction Sentence

  • Setup: Show the child a moon photograph from tonight. Ask: "What will the moon look like in three days? Say a sentence that explains why."
  • Pass: Child produces a sentence with at least two parts: a prediction and a reason connected by "because." Does not need to be astronomically accurate.
  • Fail: Child makes a correct prediction but cannot explain why. Correct outcomes without reasoning do not count as mastery.
Gating Connection · Kindergarten

How This Unit Connects to Gating Criteria

Gating Criteria Addressed

  • Can identify a pattern and state the rule — practiced every week through moon phase sequencing, pattern strips, and cycle vs. sequence comparison.
  • Can retell a story in beginning-middle-end order — practiced Week 1 and reinforced through the moon narrative structure in Weeks 3 and 5.
  • Connects cause to effect — the flashlight model (Week 4) and cause-effect diagram directly build this skill.
  • Uses "because" in a complete sentence — every week includes a structured "because" sentence activity; by Week 5 children produce three-part prediction arguments.

The Kindergarten gating question: Can this child make a prediction and explain why? A child who can say "I think ___ will happen because I noticed ___" has the foundational reasoning move that Tier 1 will formalize as Claim → Reason → Evidence.
