Band I · Pre-K

The Most Important Year in the System

Not because of what is taught — but because of what is built.

Every critical thinking skill this curriculum develops — Toulmin mapping, predicate logic, Red Team inoculation, policy reasoning — depends on a student who can sit with uncertainty, notice their own reactions, and ask questions without shame. None of those capacities arrive automatically. They are built, slowly, in early childhood.

Pre-K is where the Internal Heatmap is seeded. Not as a named concept — but as a lived practice. A child who learns at age four that "I feel angry right now" is information, not an emergency, is a child who will be able to keep their Logic Gate open at age fourteen when a manipulative headline triggers the same response.

There are no tracks in Pre-K. No assessments. No competency targets in the academic sense. The goal is a child who is curious, regulated, and ready to learn.

No tracks. No sorting. No specialization. Every child does everything. The only goal is a human foundation sturdy enough to build on.

Core Domain · Emotional Development

Feeling Is Information

The earliest form of the Internal Heatmap.

What Students Practice

  • Naming feelings with precision: anger, frustration, excitement, fear, sadness, confusion, pride
  • 'I notice I feel upset. That is a signal, not a verdict.' — practiced daily in morning circle
  • The body responds before the mind catches up — normalizing that experience removes shame from it
  • Conflict resolution through words: naming what happened, naming what was felt, naming what is needed
  • Recognizing that two people can feel differently about the same event — perspective as a concept introduced through play

🌡 The Heatmap Seed — Age 4

This is where the vertical thread begins. The Internal Heatmap that an 8th grader uses to evaluate a disinformation campaign starts here — with a 4-year-old learning to say 'I feel scared' instead of running away or hitting.

Teachers use a simple feelings chart and a daily circle check-in. 'How does your body feel right now?' is asked every morning — not as therapy, but as practice. The goal is automaticity: noticing before reacting.

By the end of Pre-K, a child who can say 'I feel frustrated because I couldn't build the tower' has performed the same cognitive move that will later be called the Logic Gate Protocol.

Core Domain · Physical Development

The Body Teaches the Mind

Spatial reasoning, fine motor control, and embodied logic.

What Students Practice

  • Fine motor skills through drawing, cutting, building, threading, and clay work
  • Gross motor skills through structured movement, dance, obstacle courses, and outdoor play
  • Spatial reasoning through block construction: What happens if I put the big block on top of the small one?
  • Cause-and-effect through physical action: push/pull, stack/topple, fill/empty
  • Pattern recognition through physical arrangement: sorting by color, size, shape, and sequence

Physical play is not a break from learning — it is the learning. Block construction is spatial reasoning. Sorting by color is categorical logic. A child who builds a stable tower has solved a structural engineering problem.

Core Domain · Pattern & Early Logic

Patterns Before Numbers

The same cognitive muscle that will later perform Boolean logic.

What Students Practice

  • Sorting and categorizing: 'These all belong together because...'
  • Sequencing: what comes first, next, last — the earliest argument structure
  • Simple if/then through play: 'If I water the seed, then it grows'
  • Story structure: beginning, middle, end — the first argument map
  • Noticing when a sequence is broken: 'Something is wrong here'
  • Repeating patterns: ABAB, AABB — regularity as a concept
Core Domain · Curiosity

Wonder as a Practice

Questions are never disruptive. They are the point.

Classroom Protocol

  • Teachers model 'why?' and 'how do we know?' in daily conversation — not as challenges but as genuine inquiry
  • No question is treated as silly, disruptive, or off-topic. Every question is acknowledged.
  • The phrase 'I don't know — let's find out' is used freely and deliberately
  • Children are asked 'what do you notice?' before they are told what to see
  • Disagreement between children is welcomed and guided: 'That's interesting — you think X and she thinks Y. How could we find out who's right?'

A child who arrives in Kindergarten still asking 'why?' without apology is already ahead of most adults in the most important cognitive habit this curriculum teaches.

The Vertical Thread

The Heatmap Seed in Practice

What this looks like at age 4 — and why it matters at age 14.

The Internal Heatmap is not taught as a concept in Pre-K. It is built as a habit through daily practice. Here is what that looks like concretely:

Daily Practice Examples

  • Morning Circle: 'Before we start — how does your body feel right now? Show me on the feelings chart.'
  • Conflict moments: 'I can see your body is really upset. Can you tell me what the feeling is called? Good. Now — what happened?'
  • Story discussions: 'When the wolf blew the house down, how did the pig feel? How do you know? Did your body feel anything when I read that part?'
  • Transition moments: 'We're switching activities now. I notice some of you look frustrated. That's okay. Can you name it?'

🌡 The Connection to Tier 7 Inoculation

In Tier 7, students are asked to notice their physiological response to a charged headline before engaging with it analytically. That is an advanced version of exactly what a Pre-K student does when they say 'I feel angry' before they act.

The vertical thread is literal: the neural pathway trained at age 4 — notice, name, pause — is the same pathway activated at age 13 when a Red Team exercise triggers discomfort. We are not teaching a new skill in Tier 7. We are applying a deeply practiced one.

Operational Layer · Pre-K

Evaluation Primitives

Each capacity is only real when a teacher can observe it in an unfamiliar situation — not in the routine where it was practiced.

Task 1 — Feeling Identification on a New Story

Setup: Read aloud a short picture book the child has never heard. At a moment of clear character emotion, pause and ask: "How is [character] feeling right now? How do you know?"

Pass: Child names a specific feeling word (not just "sad" for everything — distinguishes fear from sadness, frustration from anger) and points to at least one detail in the story or illustration as evidence.

Fail: Child echoes the last word the teacher said, gives a generic response ("bad"), or cannot point to any story detail.

Failure mode to watch: The child who has learned to answer "sad" as the default correct answer whenever a character looks unhappy — precision is the test, not volume of responses.

Task 2 — Body Awareness Under Mild Frustration

Setup: During a structured activity, engineer a mild obstacle (a puzzle piece that doesn't fit, a block tower that falls). Do not prompt. Observe and then ask: "What do you notice in your body right now?"

Pass: Child uses at least one body-location word without prompting ("my chest feels tight," "my face is hot," "my hands want to squeeze") or names the feeling and connects it to the situation ("I feel frustrated because the tower fell").

Fail: Child says "I don't know" and moves on without engagement, or immediately redirects to the external problem without any self-reference.

Failure mode to watch: The child who has learned to say "I feel frustrated" as a rote phrase but whose body language and behavior show no connection to the words — the label is not the skill.

Task 3 — Pattern Identification in a New Sequence

Setup: Present a physical sequence of objects the child has not sorted before (e.g., spoons and forks, or colored stickers in a new arrangement). Ask: "What's the pattern? What comes next?"

Pass: Child identifies the repeating unit (not just "it goes red, blue, red, blue" but "it keeps going red then blue, so next is red"), extends the pattern correctly at least two steps, and can say what rule they used.

Fail: Child extends the sequence by one step through imitation but cannot articulate the rule, or correctly identifies the next item but not the one after.

Failure mode to watch: Pattern completion by visual matching without rule extraction — the child who gets the right answer but cannot say why.

Task 4 — Cause and Effect in an Unfamiliar Story

Setup: Read a short story the child has never heard. Ask: "Why did [event] happen?" and separately "What happened because of [earlier event]?"

Pass: Child correctly identifies the cause of an event (not just the event before it) and can distinguish consequence from coincidence — "it happened because..." rather than "and then..."

Fail: Child retells the sequence correctly but answers "why" questions with "and then" answers — sequential without causal understanding.

Failure mode to watch: Mistaking temporal sequence for causation — "and then the wolf came and then the house fell down" is not a causal explanation.

Operational Layer · Pre-K

Gating Criteria

What a child must demonstrate — not just complete — before entering Kindergarten.

Required Demonstrations

  • Names at least 6 distinct feelings — with enough precision to distinguish fear from sadness and frustration from anger — when asked about a new story character or a real moment
  • Uses at least one body-location word when describing a feeling in the moment ("my stomach," "my chest," "my face") — not only when prompted with "where do you feel it?"
  • Identifies and extends a repeating pattern in new material, and can state the rule in their own words
  • Answers "why did that happen?" with a causal explanation ("because..."), not only a sequential one ("and then...")
  • Asks "why?" or "how does that work?" without being prompted — observed at least occasionally in free play or circle discussion
  • Can sit with mild frustration long enough to name it before acting — observed in at least one genuine frustration moment, not only in rehearsed circle practice

What Does Not Count as Mastery

  • Completing a feelings worksheet or pointing to a chart on demand — the chart is a tool, not the test
  • Correctly labeling a feeling that the teacher just modeled or named — echoing is not identification
  • Extending a pattern that was practiced repeatedly in class — the task must use novel material
  • Producing causal language only when the sentence frame "because..." is provided by the teacher
  • Regulated behavior in calm, familiar routines — the question is whether the child can name a feeling during a genuine difficult moment

The gating question: When something unexpected and mildly upsetting happens, does this child notice something in themselves before reacting — or do they only label the feeling afterward when an adult helps them reconstruct it? The goal of Pre-K is the former. Reconstruction is a consolation prize.

Embodied Sovereignty · Pre-K

The Land as First Teacher

Before skills, before knowledge — relationship. A child who finds the outside interesting is already ahead.

What Students Do

  • Daily outdoor time as curriculum not break
  • Guided sensory walks — touch soil, smell rain, watch weather
  • Growing one thing from seed
  • Supervised candle-fire observation
  • Named plants encountered by sight not just label

Teacher Framing

  • Ask 'What do you notice?' before 'What is this called?' The goal is not identification — it is attention
  • A child who looks carefully at a beetle for two minutes has practiced the same cognitive move as a child who reads carefully

What Success Looks Like

  • Child chooses to go outside
  • Child notices and names something without being prompted
  • Child asks a question about a plant, animal, or weather event

No gating criteria at this stage. The only question is whether the child finds the natural world interesting rather than something to avoid. Relationship precedes skill. (Sobel, 'Beyond Ecophobia,' 1996)
