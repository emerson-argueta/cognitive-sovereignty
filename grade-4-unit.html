Tier 4
Does More Screen Time Mean Worse Grades?

Every student has heard a version of this claim — from parents, teachers, articles. It is pervasive, plausible, and almost never examined carefully. This unit uses real-looking data about screen time and academic performance to teach students the most important statistical distinction they will ever learn: correlation is not causation. The question stays open until the data genuinely supports a conclusion.

Unit Design · Tier 4

What This Unit Builds

Five weeks of reading, creating, and critically evaluating data — applied to a question students care about.

The screen time question is chosen because it is familiar, personally relevant, and politically charged within families. Students have strong intuitions about it. The unit's job is not to settle the question but to teach students how to use data to approach it honestly — including discovering that the available data does not support the strong causal claim most adults assert.

Three types of visual data are introduced and practiced: bar charts (Week 1), line graphs (Week 2), and scatter plots (Week 3). Each type reveals different patterns and hides different things. By Week 5, students can look at any chart and ask: what does this actually show, what does it not show, and what would you need to establish a causal claim?

What you will see by Week 5: Students who look at any "X causes Y" headline and ask: "Is there data? Does the data show a correlation or a controlled experiment?" Students who can spot a lurking variable — a third factor that explains why X and Y appear related. Students who understand that "they tend to go together" is a much weaker claim than "one causes the other."

Week 1 · Tier 4 Unit

What Does the Data Look Like?

Reading bar charts and tables. Establishing what is actually measured before interpreting anything.

LensDaily FocusWhat Students DoReasoning Connection
Literacy & WritingWhat is a claim vs. what is data?Students read two paragraphs: one making a claim about screen time ("Studies show screen time hurts grades"), one presenting actual data ("In a survey of 200 students, those who reported more than 4 hours of daily screen time had an average GPA of 2.8; those reporting under 2 hours had an average GPA of 3.3"). Students underline the claim and circle the data. Write: "What does the data actually say? Does it say the same thing as the claim?"The gap between what data says and what a claim asserts is the central analytical move of this unit. A claim can say more than the data supports — and usually does. Teaching students to read back from claim to underlying data is one of the most practical critical thinking skills in this curriculum.
MathematicsReading a data tableTeacher provides a four-column data table: Student ID, Hours of screen time per day, GPA, Hours of sleep per night. 20 rows. Students calculate: average GPA for students with 0–2 hours screen time, 2–4 hours, 4+ hours. Do they find a pattern? Is the pattern what they expected?Calculating group averages from raw data gives students ownership of the analysis — they are not being told what the data shows, they are discovering it. Students who calculate the averages themselves are less likely to passively accept the conclusion that high screen time "causes" low GPA.
VisualizationBuild a bar chartStudents use their calculated averages to build a bar chart: x-axis = screen time category (low/medium/high), y-axis = average GPA. Title it, label axes, add a legend. Then ask: "What does this chart show at a glance? What information is NOT visible in this chart that was in the table?"Building a chart from data rather than reading a pre-made chart forces students to make choices about what to display — and therefore understand what is being hidden or aggregated. Chart literacy includes knowing what a chart omits.
Project WorkCollect class dataAnonymous class survey: estimated hours of screen time per day (school-related and recreational separately), self-reported sleep hours, favorite subject. Pool class data. "What patterns can we see in our own class? Is our class representative of all students everywhere?" (Discuss sample size and representativeness.)Collecting primary data builds investment in the analysis and introduces the concept of sample — not all students, just these students. Whether this sample is representative is an important caveat that professional researchers must always address.
Argument/ReasoningWhat would prove the claim?Class discussion: "If screens DO cause worse grades, what would the data look like? If they DON'T cause worse grades, what would the data look like?" Students describe what confirming vs. disconfirming data would look like before seeing the actual data — establishing the standard in advance.Specifying what evidence would confirm or disconfirm a claim before looking at evidence is the foundational move of hypothesis-driven inquiry. It prevents motivated reasoning — cherry-picking evidence after the fact that supports a predetermined conclusion.

By end of Week 1

  • Can read a data table and calculate group averages
  • Has built a bar chart from calculated data and labeled it correctly
  • Can identify what a chart shows and what information it omits
  • Has described what confirming and disconfirming data would look like
Week 2 · Tier 4 Unit

How Do We Read a Chart Without Being Fooled?

Line graphs and the tricks charts play: truncated axes, misleading scales, cherry-picked time ranges.

LensDaily FocusWhat Students DoReasoning Connection
Literacy & WritingChart headlines vs. chart realityStudents receive four charts about screen time and grades. For each: (1) read the headline, (2) read the actual chart, (3) write: "The headline says ___ but the chart actually shows ___. The difference is ___." At least one chart will have a headline that goes well beyond what the data supports.Headlines routinely overclaim relative to the underlying data. Reading the chart independently of its headline — and then comparing — is a critical media literacy skill that applies to every domain where data is presented publicly.
MathematicsAxis manipulationTeacher shows two versions of the same data plotted with different y-axis scales: one starts at 0 (modest difference visible), one starts at 2.5 GPA (dramatic difference visible). "Is the data different? Is the pattern different? Why does the graph look so different?" Students draw both versions from the same data.Truncated axes are one of the most common ways charts mislead without lying. A chart can present true data in a way that creates a false impression of the magnitude of a difference. Students who understand axis manipulation cannot be fooled by it.
VisualizationLine graph — trends over timeStudents plot a line graph showing average GPA by screen time hours, using data from a 5-year longitudinal dataset (teacher-provided, realistic but simulated). "Does the pattern change depending on which years you look at? What does the trend look like if you start from Year 1? From Year 3?"Cherry-picking time ranges is one of the most common forms of misleading data presentation. A trend that looks strong over one period may look flat or reversed over a longer period. Teaching students to ask "what happens if you extend the time range?" is a powerful protection against this.
Project WorkBuild a misleading chartStudents intentionally create a misleading version of honest data using one technique: truncated axis, cherry-picked time range, or misleading title. Then create the honest version. Present both with a caption explaining what the misleading version does and why it creates a false impression.Creating a misleading chart deliberately — with full awareness of what makes it misleading — is more educational than just spotting misleading charts. Students who can construct a deception understand the mechanics of the technique, not just the label.
Argument/ReasoningWhat the line graph provesStudents write: "Based on the line graph, what can I conclude? What can I NOT conclude?" Key limitation to surface: a line graph showing GPA and screen time together over time shows that two things changed simultaneously. It does not show which caused which — or that either caused the other.The central limitation of any correlational data presented graphically: two lines moving together does not prove causation. This limitation is introduced here informally and formalized in Week 3 as the correlation/causation distinction.

By end of Week 2

  • Can read and create a line graph with a correctly scaled y-axis
  • Has identified at least two ways the same data can be made to look different
  • Has created an intentionally misleading chart and explained why it misleads
  • Can articulate what a line graph does and does not prove
Week 3 · Tier 4 Unit

What Is the Difference Between Correlation and Causation?

The most important statistical concept in the curriculum. Introduced through scatter plots and memorable examples.

LensDaily FocusWhat Students DoReasoning Connection
Literacy & WritingSilly correlationsStudents receive absurd-but-real correlations: per capita cheese consumption correlates with deaths by bedsheet tangling. Nicolas Cage film releases correlate with pool drownings. Students write: "Do you think X causes Y? Why not? What explains why they might be correlated?" Key: some correlations are coincidence; some have a common cause; none of these have a direct causal link.Absurd correlations are pedagogically powerful because students immediately know the causal claim is ridiculous — which forces them to think about why correlation alone doesn't establish causation. Once they can articulate why cheese doesn't cause bedsheet deaths, they can apply the same reasoning to more plausible-sounding correlations.
MathematicsScatter plotsStudents plot 20 data points on a scatter plot: x = daily screen time, y = GPA. Add a best-fit line (teacher demonstrates). Read the correlation: do the points cluster around the line (strong correlation) or scatter widely (weak correlation)? Calculate: for students with 5 hours of screen time, what GPA does the line predict? How many actual students match that prediction exactly?A scatter plot makes correlation visual — and also makes variability visual. Even with a strong correlation, many individual data points deviate significantly from the predicted line. Students who see this understand that a correlation predicts on average, not for any individual.
VisualizationThe third variable diagramStudents draw a diagram with three nodes: Screen Time, GPA, and a third variable (e.g., "Hours of sleep"). Arrows: does sleep affect both screen time and GPA? If yes, sleep is a confounding variable — it could explain the correlation without any direct link between screen time and grades. Students brainstorm five possible confounding variables for the screen time/GPA relationship.The confounding variable (lurking variable) is the key concept that separates correlation from causation. Two things that both respond to a third thing will appear correlated — even with no direct relationship between them. This diagram is the visual representation of that logic.
Project WorkWhat would prove causation?Students design (but do not run) an experiment that would prove screen time causes lower grades. Key elements they must include: random assignment, control group, same screen time only (no other changes), long enough duration. Teacher asks: "Could we ethically run this experiment on real students?" Discuss.Designing a controlled experiment — even hypothetically — teaches students what causation actually requires: random assignment, control groups, isolation of variables. The ethical constraint also teaches that some causal questions cannot be answered experimentally, which is why we must be careful about causal claims in social science.
Argument/ReasoningCausal vs. correlational languageStudents rewrite five "screen time causes bad grades" headlines into accurate correlational language: "Students with more screen time tend to have lower GPAs on average" instead of "Screen time hurts grades." Then evaluate: which version is more useful? Which version is more publishable? Why is there a gap?The gap between accurate correlational language and dramatic causal language is a commercial incentive, not an epistemic one. Accurate language is less alarming and generates fewer clicks. Students who understand this gap are equipped to read media coverage of research critically for the rest of their lives.

By end of Week 3

  • Can create a scatter plot from data and describe the correlation it shows
  • Can identify at least three confounding variables for the screen time/GPA relationship
  • Can describe what a controlled experiment would require to establish causation
  • Can rewrite a causal headline into accurate correlational language
Week 4 · Tier 4 Unit

Building the Data-Backed Argument

Students construct a full argument about screen time and grades — using only what the data actually supports.

LensDaily FocusWhat Students DoReasoning Connection
Literacy & WritingWriting with dataStudents write an argument paragraph that begins: "Based on the data, here is what I can claim: ___." They must explicitly state what the data shows (correlation), what it does not show (causation), and what would be needed to establish causation. No unsupported causal language allowed.Writing with epistemic hedges — "the data suggests," "we cannot conclude from this data that" — is a form of intellectual honesty. Scientists and researchers use this language not because they are uncertain about everything, but because they are precise about what their evidence supports.
MathematicsStrength of correlationStudents calculate or estimate correlation coefficients from scatter plots using a simplified rubric: tight cluster around line = strong (r ≈ 0.8+), moderate scatter = moderate (r ≈ 0.5), wide scatter = weak (r ≈ 0.2). Apply to three different scatter plots. "A weak correlation means the relationship exists but is small — not that there is no relationship. What is the difference?"Correlation strength matters enormously for argument quality. A claim based on a weak correlation (r = 0.2) is much weaker than one based on a strong correlation (r = 0.8). Students who understand this cannot be fooled by "scientists found a link between X and Y" without knowing how strong the link is.
VisualizationArgument flowchartStudents build an argument flowchart about screen time and grades. Nodes: (1) What the data shows, (2) What confounds might explain this, (3) What the data does NOT prove, (4) What conclusion is warranted, (5) What additional evidence would strengthen the conclusion. Arrows connect each node to the next.An argument flowchart makes the logical structure of a complex argument navigable. Unlike a paragraph (linear), a flowchart shows branching — where the argument splits depending on what evidence is available. This is the Tier 4 version of the three-box argument map, now grown to handle conditional reasoning.
Project WorkFind a real studyStudents (with teacher guidance) read a one-paragraph summary of a real study on screen time and academic performance. Apply the full toolkit: is the sample representative? Is it correlational or experimental? Are confounds addressed? What does the conclusion claim vs. what the data shows? Write a one-paragraph evaluation.Evaluating a real study — even in summary form — connects the unit's skills to how scientific knowledge is actually produced and communicated. Students learn that most published research on social behavior is correlational, not experimental, and that this matters enormously for interpretation.
Argument/ReasoningCounter the overclaimTeacher states the overclaim: "The science is clear: screen time causes lower grades. Parents should limit screens." Students must respond: "The data shows a correlation. We cannot conclude causation because ___. The claim that 'science is clear' is also problematic because ___." Write the response as a paragraph.Responding to an overclaim respectfully and specifically — naming the exact logical error — is the Tier 4 version of the adversarial exercise. Students practice correcting faulty reasoning without dismissing the person making it.

By end of Week 4

  • Has written an argument paragraph using only what the data supports, with hedging language
  • Can estimate correlation strength from a scatter plot and explain what it means
  • Has built an argument flowchart with at least five nodes
  • Has evaluated a real study summary using the full toolkit
Week 5 · Tier 4 Unit

What Is Our Final, Honest Conclusion?

Transfer, presentation, and self-assessment. The answer must be calibrated to the evidence — no more, no less.

LensDaily FocusWhat Students DoReasoning Connection
Literacy & WritingFinal data argumentStudents write a full two-paragraph argument in response to the Core Problem: "Does more screen time mean worse grades?" Paragraph 1: what the data shows. Paragraph 2: what the data does not prove and what we would need to answer the question definitively. Must use correlational language throughout and identify at least two confounds.The two-paragraph structure is deliberate: the first paragraph shows the evidence; the second shows intellectual honesty about limits. Both are required for a complete argument. Students who write only paragraph 1 are overclaiming; those who write only paragraph 2 are not engaging the evidence.
MathematicsData presentationStudents select their three best charts from the unit (one bar, one line, one scatter) and prepare them for presentation: clean labels, correct scale, honest title that matches what the data shows. Present to a small group. Partners ask: "What does this chart prove? What doesn't it prove?"Presenting data to peers who ask critical questions is the social accountability version of chart literacy. A chart that holds up under peer questioning is better evidence than one that does not.
VisualizationRevised argument flowchartStudents revise their Week 4 flowchart using everything they learned in Week 5. The revision should be more precise: where Week 4 said "more evidence needed," Week 5 should specify what kind of evidence. Post on the wall.Revision in response to new understanding is the intellectual practice of updating. Students who revise their flowchart have changed their argument structure based on improved understanding — the same move as revising an argument after receiving new evidence.
Project WorkTransfer — new correlational claimStudents receive a new correlational claim from a different domain: "Students who eat breakfast have higher test scores." Task: evaluate this claim using the same framework — is there data? What type? What confounds? What would establish causation? Write a brief evaluation.Transfer to a new domain tests whether students understand the framework or just the screen time case. Students who can apply the five-question analytical framework to a new correlational claim have internalized the methodology.
Argument/ReasoningThinking Journal — what changedThinking Journal: "Before this unit, when someone told me that X causes Y, I would ___. Now I would ___. The question I will always ask first is: ___." The final question should be some version of "Is this a correlation or a controlled experiment?"The habit change — from accepting causal claims to asking whether the evidence establishes causation — is the unit's most important long-term outcome. Students who internalize this question are protected against a huge proportion of statistical misinformation for the rest of their lives.

By end of Week 5

  • Has written a two-paragraph argument that both presents the evidence and acknowledges its limits
  • Has presented three cleaned-up charts to peers and responded to critical questions
  • Has applied the correlational analysis framework to a new claim in a different domain
  • Has written a Thinking Journal entry about what changed in how they approach causal claims
Unit Assessment · Tier 4

What Mastery Looks Like

Transfer Task — New Correlational Claim

  • Setup: Present a headline: "Kids who read more are happier, study finds." Ask: "Is this good evidence that reading causes happiness? What would you need to know?"
  • Pass: Student asks whether the study was correlational or experimental, names at least one confound (e.g., happy kids have more time or inclination to read), and states that a causal conclusion requires a controlled experiment. Does not dismiss the finding — acknowledges the correlation while specifying its limits.
  • Fail: Student says "yes that proves reading makes you happy" (accepts correlation as causation) OR "that's probably wrong" without engaging the evidence. Both extremes indicate failure to reason from the data.

Chart Reading

  • Setup: Show an unfamiliar scatter plot with a truncated y-axis. Ask: "What does this chart show? Is anything misleading about how it is displayed?"
  • Pass: Student correctly reads the correlation, identifies the truncated axis, and explains how the truncation exaggerates the apparent strength of the relationship.
Gating Connection · Tier 4

How This Unit Connects to Gating Criteria

Gating Criteria Addressed

  • Reads bar charts, line graphs, and scatter plots correctly — built, read, and critically evaluated across Weeks 1–3.
  • Explains the correlation/causation distinction and names at least two confounding variables — the central concept of Week 3, applied in every subsequent activity.
  • Writes a data-backed argument with explicit claims about what the evidence does and does not prove — Weeks 4 and 5 require this in paragraph form.
  • Builds an argument flowchart with branching conditional structure — Weeks 4 and 5 require this as the primary visualization format.

The Tier 4 gating question: When shown a correlational finding, can this student articulate what it proves, what it does not prove, and what additional evidence would be needed? This question applies to every quantitative claim in journalism, science communication, and public debate.
