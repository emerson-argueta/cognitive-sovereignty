Phase Three · Grade 10

The Last Year to Change Course

Deep specialization begins in Grade 11. Grade 10 is the final wide-angle year.

Track movement in Grade 10 remains as frictionless as in Grade 9 — a conversation, a willingness to try, done. But students feel the clock ticking. By the end of this year, they will have identified their primary track. The Grade 11 transition is real.

All four tracks begin producing things with real stakes this year. No more simulations for their own sake — real mentors, real materials, real audiences, real consequences when something doesn't work.

Academic Track · Grade 10

Research Methodology at Depth

Study design, peer review, the replication crisis — the machinery of knowledge production.

What Students Practice

  • Study design: control groups, double-blinding, confounding variables, statistical power
  • Peer review: what it is, what it catches, what it misses, why it remains the best system we have
  • The replication crisis: why many famous studies failed to replicate and what this means for scientific knowledge
  • Original research project: a genuine question, a genuine methodology, genuine data collection and analysis
  • Formal paper: structured as an academic article — abstract, introduction, methods, results, discussion
Vocational Track · Grade 10

First Real Apprenticeship

Real mentors, real materials, real consequences.

What Students Practice

  • Active apprenticeship placement begins — minimum 2 days per week in a real trade environment
  • Advanced contract and regulatory literacy: reading real employment agreements, safety regulations, union contracts
  • Financial reasoning: job costing, material estimation, quote preparation — real numbers for real work
  • Documenting decisions: every non-trivial choice in an apprenticeship task is recorded and explained
  • Evaluating a real product or service claim using the full Phase Two analytical toolkit
Systems & Engineering Track · Grade 10

Design Under Real Constraint

The first build that has to work — with real materials, real failure, real accountability.

What Students Practice

  • A semester-long design project with defined real-world constraints: budget, materials, performance requirements
  • Failure analysis: when the first design fails (it will), the failure is documented, analyzed, and fed back into the redesign
  • Studying real engineering failures: the Tacoma Narrows Bridge, the Challenger O-rings, the Boeing 737 MAX MCAS — what reasoning errors caused them?
  • Computational thinking deepened: working with real data, real algorithms, real debugging
  • Formal constraint documentation: every design decision recorded against the constraints it satisfies or violates
Civic Track · Grade 10

Policy Design — First Attempt

A real policy proposal. A real critic. A real revision.

What Students Practice

  • Identifying a real local or regional policy problem with genuine stakes
  • Designing a policy proposal: the problem statement, the proposed intervention, the evidence for it, the likely objections
  • Presenting the proposal to a real external critic — someone who has a reason to disagree
  • Revising under pressure: incorporating legitimate criticism into a stronger version of the proposal
  • Legal reasoning introduced: how does law work? What is a precedent? How are statutes interpreted?
Operational Layer · Grade 10

Evaluation Primitives

Each capability is only real when it can be demonstrated on an unfamiliar problem under no-hint conditions.

Task 1 · Methodology Defense

Setup: Present the student with a brief description of a research question — one they haven't studied — and ask them to design a methodology for investigating it. They have 20 minutes and no reference materials.

  • Pass: Student identifies appropriate variables, proposes controls, names at least one source of confounding bias, and explains how their design would handle it.
  • Fail: Student produces steps that describe activity ("I would survey 50 people") without identifying what the design is intended to rule out.
  • Failure mode to watch: Students who have memorized the vocabulary of methodology without understanding its function — they will use words like "control group" correctly but be unable to explain what the control is controlling for.

Task 2 · Failure Documentation Review

Setup: The student submits their failure documentation from their track work this year. An evaluator reads it cold — without knowing the project — and asks: "What went wrong, why did it go wrong, and what changed as a result?"

  • Pass: The documentation allows the evaluator to reconstruct the reasoning error or external cause, and clearly shows how the student's subsequent work changed because of it.
  • Fail: The documentation describes what happened without analyzing why — or the "lesson learned" is generic ("I should have planned better") rather than specific to this failure.
  • Failure mode to watch: Curated documentation that reports only failures with clean lessons — real failure documentation should include uncertainty, partial understanding, and questions that remain open.

Task 3 · Stake-Tolerance Under Real Consequence

Setup: Evaluator reviews how the student has handled a moment this year when their work had real consequences — a mentor who pushed back, a design that failed in front of someone, a policy proposal that was rejected. The student is asked: "Walk me through what you were thinking at that moment."

  • Pass: Student can give a specific, honest account of their reasoning and emotional state at the moment of failure or pressure, including what they wanted to do versus what they actually did.
  • Fail: Student narrates the event from a retrospectively sanitized perspective — the failure is described as a learning experience from the start, with no authentic account of what it felt like to be wrong in a real context.
  • Failure mode to watch: The student has learned to perform resilience rather than demonstrate it — they know what the "right answer" sounds like when describing how they handled failure.

Task 4 · Direction Reassessment

Setup: The student is given a scenario: they have invested three months in a direction for their track project, and new information has emerged that calls the direction into question. What do they do?

  • Pass: Student identifies what specific information would be sufficient to justify a direction change, distinguishes between discomfort and genuine disconfirmation, and can describe what criteria they would use to make the decision.
  • Fail: Student either immediately abandons the direction (sunk cost ignored entirely) or defends the original direction without engaging with the new information.
  • Failure mode to watch: The student treats this as a question about resilience ("I wouldn't give up") rather than a question about evidence and decision criteria.
Operational Layer · Grade 10

Gating Criteria

What a student must demonstrate — not just complete — before advancing to Grade 11.

Required Demonstrations

  • Has produced one piece of real track work — research paper, apprenticeship portfolio entry, engineering build documentation, or policy proposal — that was evaluated by someone outside the school with relevant standing
  • Has documented at least one genuine failure in their track work this year, with analysis of cause and documented change in subsequent work
  • Can defend the methodology of their track work — explaining what it was designed to rule out, not just what steps were followed
  • Has identified their primary track with a stated rationale that goes beyond preference ("I like it") to engagement evidence ("here is what I kept returning to")
  • Has operated under real stakes at least once this year — a moment when failure had an external consequence, not just an internal one

What Does Not Count as Mastery

  • Completing the assigned track activities without producing work that was evaluated externally
  • Writing about failure without the documentation showing that subsequent work actually changed
  • Selecting a track because it is the strongest or most comfortable — track choice must be based on calling signals, not performance confidence
  • Defending methodology using vocabulary without being able to explain the function — what is being controlled, what is being ruled out, and why
  • Performing stake-tolerance rather than demonstrating it — a student who describes handling real pressure correctly but whose documentation shows avoidance behavior has not passed this gate

The gating question: Can this student operate when the stakes are real — when failure has consequences outside the classroom — and do they have the methodology to learn from it rather than just survive it?

Operational Layer · Grade 10

Adversarial Exercises

Sovereignty requires training against the actual conditions that undermine independent thinking.

Exercise 1 · The Expert You Shouldn't Fully Trust

Setup: The student's mentor, apprenticeship supervisor, or research advisor holds a strong opinion that is directly relevant to the student's work. The student is asked: "Do you agree with their position? What is your evidence?"

This exercise targets one of the most common sovereignty failures in this age group: the substitution of a respected person's judgment for one's own. Students who have been trained to evaluate arguments will often suspend that training when the argument comes from someone they respect and depend on. The mentor relationship is exactly the context where this failure is most likely — and most consequential for Grade 11 and beyond.

What this trains: Distinguishing between an expert's domain authority and their position on a specific claim — recognizing that expertise in a field does not automatically confer correctness on every opinion within that field.

Exercise 2 · The Sunk Cost Interrogation

Setup: The student identifies the direction in their track work where they have invested the most time and effort. They are then presented with a set of findings — some real, some constructed — that create genuine doubt about that direction. They must make a documented decision: continue, pivot, or abandon.

Sunk cost reasoning is particularly potent in Grade 10 because this is the first year students have invested real time and social capital in a direction. The investment is no longer hypothetical. This exercise forces the student to articulate their decision criteria before they are in a high-stakes version of this situation in Grade 11.

What this trains: Separating "how much I have invested" from "whether this direction is still correct" — the core cognitive move that protects long-term work from compounding errors.

Exercise 3 · The Confirmation Audit

Setup: The student reviews their research design, engineering constraint documentation, or policy evidence base and is asked: "What evidence would disconfirm your central claim? Did you look for it?" They then have one week to find the strongest case against their own position.

Confirmation bias in one's own research is nearly invisible to the person running the research. By Grade 10, students have invested enough in their work that they genuinely want their hypotheses to be confirmed. This exercise makes the search for disconfirming evidence a formal requirement, not an afterthought.

What this trains: Actively constructing the case against one's own position as a standard part of the research process — the habit that distinguishes genuine inquiry from sophisticated advocacy.

Why adversarial training matters at Grade 10: The stakes are real for the first time this year, which means the temptation to protect one's work from scrutiny is also real for the first time. Students who learn to apply adversarial pressure to their own thinking while the consequences of being wrong are still manageable will carry that habit into Grade 11 and 12, when the consequences are not.

Embodied Sovereignty · Grade 10

Apprenticeship Begins

The vocational track opens apprenticeship this year. Students drawn toward land skills, agriculture, or wilderness work use the same structure — working directly with practitioners outside the school for the first time with real stakes.

Grade 10 Focus Skills

  • Practitioner apprenticeship: students in this strand are matched with a farmer, wilderness guide, herbalist, or similar practitioner
  • First extended self-sufficiency exercise: multi-day, fully planned and documented
  • Systems thinking applied to land: food webs, water cycles, and ecological infrastructure understood as interdependent systems
  • Domain identification: students begin to sense which of the six pillars draws them most — food, water, medicine, navigation, shelter, community

The same rule that governs track movement in the cognitive curriculum applies here: no qualifying test, no formal re-enrollment. A student who wants to go deeper into land skills has a conversation with their advisor and starts.
