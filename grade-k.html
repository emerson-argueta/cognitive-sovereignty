Phase One · Kindergarten

Structure Enters the Picture

Play remains the vehicle — but now it has direction.

Kindergarten builds on the emotional foundation of Pre-K by introducing the first explicit logical structures: cause-and-effect, sequencing, and the basic claim-and-reason format. These arrive through story, play, and conversation — never through drill.

The most important linguistic distinction of the year is 'because' versus 'so': understanding that these words point in opposite directions — one toward explanation, one toward consequence — is the earliest form of directional logic that will later become predicate reasoning.

The goal is not a child who can recite facts. It is a child who asks 'why did that happen?' and waits for a real answer.

Core Domain

Story as the First Logic Map

Beginning, middle, end — the earliest argument structure.

What Students Practice

  • Identifying beginning, middle, and end in stories — sequence as logical structure
  • Cause and effect in narratives: 'The pig was afraid because the wolf blew the house down'
  • 'Because' vs 'so': 'She was cold because it was winter' vs 'It was winter, so she was cold'
  • Predicting what comes next — hypothesis formation through story
  • Identifying when a story 'doesn't make sense' and articulating why
  • Retelling a story in sequence — the first argument summary
Core Domain · Emotional Logic

Connecting Feelings to Decisions

Feelings have causes. So do decisions. Both can be traced.

What Students Practice

  • 'She did that because she felt...' — linking character behavior to emotional state
  • The same event can make different people feel different things — perspective as a logical variable
  • Noticing their own feeling before reacting — the Heatmap practice continues from Pre-K
  • Drawing a 'feeling map' of a story: where did the character's emotion change, and why?
  • Distinguishing between 'I feel' and 'I want' — separating state from demand

🌡 The Feeling Map — Kindergarten Version

Students draw simple timelines of a story's emotional arc: happy → scared → relieved. This is their first visualization exercise and their first heatmap simultaneously.

The teacher asks: 'When you heard that part, did YOUR body feel anything?' This connects the character's heatmap to the student's own — the first step toward the interoception practice that defines the full curriculum.

Core Domain · Pattern Recognition

Patterns in Everything

From beads on a string to the rhythm of a story.

What Students Practice

  • Visual and physical patterns: ABAB, AABB, ABCABC — identifying, extending, and creating
  • Sequences with missing elements: 'What comes next?'
  • Patterns in stories: repeated phrases, recurring events, predictable structures
  • Simple if/then through daily routine: 'If it rains, then we stay inside'
  • Categorizing by multiple attributes: 'These are big AND red'
Core Domain · Visualization

Drawing What a Story Means

Not what it looks like — what it means.

What Students Practice

  • Drawing cause-and-effect pairs: the cause on the left, the effect on the right, an arrow between them
  • Before/after diagrams: what did the character want, what did they do, what happened?
  • Feeling maps: a simple timeline of a character's emotional journey through a story
  • Building physical models of story events using blocks or clay

When a Kindergartner draws an arrow from 'pig scared' to 'pig builds stronger house,' they are creating their first argument diagram. The Toulmin Flowchart in 7th grade is the same cognitive act at higher resolution.

Operational Layer · Kindergarten

Evaluation Primitives

Each capacity is only real when a teacher can observe it on an unfamiliar story or problem — not on a text the class has discussed before.

Task 1 — Cause and Effect in a New Story

Setup: Read a short picture book the class has never encountered. At a key plot moment, ask: "Why did that happen?" and "What happened because of that?"

Pass: Child gives a causal explanation using "because" — linking the event to a prior cause, not just naming what happened next. Can answer both directions: what caused the event, and what the event caused.

Fail: Child answers "why" questions with "and then" answers — accurately retells the sequence but does not identify the causal link.

Failure mode to watch: Sequential narration presented as causal explanation — "the wolf came and then the house fell" is not the same as "the house fell because the wolf blew it."

Task 2 — 'Because' vs 'So' on an Unfamiliar Sentence Pair

Setup: Present two new sentences (not from any class story) that are logically related. Ask the child to use "because" to connect them, then use "so" to connect them. Then ask: "Which direction does 'because' point?"

Pass: Child correctly constructs both versions of the sentence, and can explain (in their own words) that "because" looks backward toward the cause while "so" looks forward toward the result. Does not need to use the words "backward" or "forward" — any accurate spatial or directional description counts.

Fail: Child uses "because" and "so" interchangeably, or can construct the sentences correctly but cannot explain the directional difference when asked.

Failure mode to watch: Students who have memorized sentence-frame patterns from class practice but cannot apply them to new content — correct production without comprehension.

Task 3 — Story Retelling in Sequence with Causal Links

Setup: After reading a new short story once (no discussion), ask the child to retell it. Do not prompt with questions. Listen for whether causal language appears spontaneously.

Pass: Child retells beginning, middle, and end in correct order and uses at least one causal connector ("because," "so," "that's why") without being prompted. Key events are in the right sequence, not just a list of things that happened.

Fail: Retelling is a flat list of events in order without any causal language — accurate but purely sequential.

Failure mode to watch: A retelling that sounds complete but skips the cause-effect relationship at the story's turning point — the child narrates the surface but misses the logic.

Task 4 — Perspective: Same Event, Different Feelings

Setup: Read a story scene where two characters experience the same event (e.g., a surprise, a storm, a change). Ask: "How did [character A] feel about that? How did [character B] feel? Why might they feel differently?"

Pass: Child correctly identifies different emotional responses for the two characters and offers at least one reason why they might differ — referencing something about the characters' situations, wants, or prior experiences in the story.

Fail: Child assigns the same feeling to both characters, or says one character's reaction is "wrong," or cannot offer any reason for the difference.

Failure mode to watch: The child who correctly names two different feelings but cannot explain why — identifying difference without understanding it as a variable is partial, not complete.

Operational Layer · Kindergarten

Gating Criteria

What a student must demonstrate — not just complete — before advancing to Grade 1.

Required Demonstrations

  • Identifies cause and effect in a story never read in class — using "because" correctly, not just sequentially
  • Correctly constructs both "because" and "so" versions of a new sentence pair and can explain the directional difference in their own words
  • Retells an unfamiliar short story in correct sequence with at least one spontaneous causal connector
  • Draws a two-box cause-and-effect diagram for an event in a new story — with an arrow pointing the right direction
  • Produces the sentence "I think X because Y" about their own opinion on a new topic — not a rehearsed class topic
  • Recognizes that two characters can feel differently about the same event and offers a reason grounded in the story

What Does Not Count as Mastery

  • Correctly answering cause-and-effect questions about a story the class has discussed and analyzed — familiar material tests memory, not the skill
  • Using "because" in a sentence frame provided by the teacher — the frame is scaffolding, not evidence of understanding
  • A retelling that is sequentially accurate but causally empty — sequence is not the target skill
  • Drawing a diagram that copies the class example — the diagram must be constructed for new content
  • Answering perspective questions about characters the class has explicitly discussed from multiple viewpoints

The gating question: When this child encounters a new story, do they automatically ask "why did that happen?" — or do they need a teacher to prompt the question? Kindergarten is successful when the causal question is the child's first instinct, not their response to instruction.

What Kindergarten Builds

  • 2

  • 3

  • 4

Embodied Sovereignty · Kindergarten

Naming the World

Three plants, two water sources, one season tracked. The beginning of land literacy.

What Students Do

  • Identify 3–5 common local plants by sight and say one true thing about each
  • Track one season collectively — class weather/season journal
  • Water sourcing awareness: where does water come from before the tap? Class rain collection jar
  • First garden bed started and tended

Teacher Framing

  • Children at this age form attachments to individual living things before they can think about systems
  • The goal is not ecological literacy — it is ecological relationship. Name the plant. Wonder about the plant. Visit the plant again

Assessment

  • Can the child name 3 local plants and say something true about each from direct observation — not from a label or a teacher prompt?
  • Can they point to where rain goes after it hits the ground?

Louv ('Last Child in the Woods,' 2005) documents the developmental cost of severed nature contact. This year is not preparation for future skills — it is irreplaceable relationship-forming in the window when it is most natural.
