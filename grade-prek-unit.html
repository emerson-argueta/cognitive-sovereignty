What Do Animals Need to Stay Alive?

Before argument structure, before logic gates, before evidence standards — there is noticing. This unit teaches three-year-olds to observe carefully, sort what they see, and say "because" for the first time. The animals are the hook. The habit is the point.

Unit Design · Pre-K

What This Unit Builds

Four weeks. Three animals. One question that never fully resolves.

The Core Problem — "What do animals need to stay alive?" — is chosen because children already have strong intuitions about it. They have fed a dog. They have watched a bird. They have seen a fish in a tank. The unit doesn't introduce an alien question; it gives structure to what children already know and feel.

Week 1 anchors the question in the child's own body and experience. Weeks 2–4 apply the same observational structure to three animals in sequence. By Week 4, children are comparing — noticing that a dog, a bird, and a fish all need food and water but differ in other ways. Comparison is the earliest form of structured reasoning.

What you will see by Week 4: Children who spontaneously say "because" when asked why an animal needs something. Children who sort picture cards by category without being told the rule. Children who notice when a drawing is missing something — "that fish doesn't have water." These are the earliest observable forms of causal reasoning and gap detection.

The Five Lenses at Pre-K

  • Observation — looking carefully, noticing details, drawing what is actually there
  • Sorting & Pattern — grouping things that go together, finding the rule
  • Drawing — externalizing thought onto paper; the Pre-K version of writing
  • Building & Play — constructing animal habitats with blocks and natural materials
  • Talking About Why — "because" sentences said aloud; dictated to teacher
Week 1 · Pre-K Unit

What Do WE Need Every Day?

Start with the child's own body. The animal question begins here.

Before asking what animals need, children must be able to answer for themselves. This week establishes the category of "needs" through direct experience. Food, water, sleep, warmth, safety — these words are introduced not as vocabulary but as lived facts the children can name from their own morning.

LensDaily FocusWhat Children DoWhy It Matters
ObservationBody check-inMorning circle: teacher asks "What did your body need this morning before school?" Children name food, sleep, a hug. Teacher draws each answer on a shared chart.Establishes "need" as a real category distinct from "want." Children begin noticing their own experience as data.
Sorting & PatternNeed vs. want sortPicture cards: apple, toy car, water bottle, stuffed animal, blanket, video game. Children sort into two piles: "need to stay alive" / "nice to have." Teacher does not correct — asks "why did you put that there?"The sorting process forces children to articulate a criterion. Early categorization is the foundation of all classification and logic work.
DrawingMy morning drawingEach child draws one thing their body needed this morning. Teacher writes the child's dictated label below the drawing.Drawing externalizes thought. The act of choosing what to draw requires the child to commit to one answer — the earliest form of claim-making.
Building & PlayFree buildBlock area seeded with pictures of homes, food, water. Children build freely. Teacher asks: "What does your building have inside for the people who live there?"Play-based construction lets children model concepts physically before they can articulate them verbally.
Talking About WhyBecause practiceTeacher models: "I need water because my body gets thirsty." Children complete the sentence: "I need ___ because ___." Dictated to teacher if needed.The word "because" is the single most important word in this curriculum. Week 1 introduces it in the safest possible context — the child's own experience.

By end of Week 1

  • Can name at least two things their body needs (not wants) to stay alive
  • Has sorted at least one picture card and attempted to say why
  • Has said "because" in a sentence at least once — aloud or dictated
  • Has a drawing in their observation journal with a teacher-written label
Week 2 · Pre-K Unit

What Does a Dog Need?

The first animal. Children know dogs. The question is whether they can observe carefully enough to build a claim.

A dog is chosen first because almost every child has direct experience with one — their own, a neighbor's, a relative's. The goal is not to teach facts about dogs. Children already know that dogs eat and drink and sleep. The goal is to help children notice that they know this from observation, and to articulate what they noticed.

LensDaily FocusWhat Children DoWhy It Matters
ObservationDog picture studyLarge photograph of a dog eating, drinking, sleeping — shown one at a time. "What do you notice?" Children name what they see, not what they assume. Teacher records observations on chart paper.Distinguishing observation ("I see a bowl of water") from assumption ("dogs like water") is the foundational move of evidence-based reasoning.
Sorting & PatternDog needs sortPicture cards: dog food bowl, leash, dog toy, water bowl, dog bed, fancy collar, tennis ball. Children sort into needs vs. nice-to-have. Discussion: "Could a dog survive without the toy? Without the water?"Repeated sorting with a new subject builds the category of "need" as transferable — not specific to the child's own experience.
DrawingDraw what a dog needsEach child draws a dog and at least one thing the dog needs to stay alive. Teacher writes dictated labels. Children who finish early draw what the dog wants but does not need.Adding the "needs vs. wants" layer to the drawing task requires children to apply the Week 1 distinction in a new context.
Building & PlayBuild a home for a dogBlock/materials center: children build a space where a dog could live. Teacher prompts: "Does your dog have water? Where does it sleep? What would happen if there was no food?"Building a habitat requires thinking systematically about needs. The teacher's "what would happen" questions introduce the earliest form of conditional reasoning.
Talking About WhyBecause — dog version"A dog needs ___ because ___." Children complete sentences aloud. Teacher asks follow-up: "How do you know?" Children attempt an observation-based answer: "Because I saw my dog drink from his bowl."The "how do you know?" question introduces evidence as distinct from reason — a two-step structure that will become Claim → Reason → Evidence in Tier 1.

By end of Week 2

  • Can name three things a dog needs to stay alive (from observation, not just guessing)
  • Can distinguish one thing a dog needs from one thing a dog wants
  • Has completed a "because" sentence about the dog at least once
  • Attempted "how do you know?" — pointed to an observation as the answer
Week 3 · Pre-K Unit

What Does a Bird Need?

A new animal. Familiar structure. One new complication: birds live outside.

Birds introduce a significant new element: the natural environment as habitat. A dog lives with people; a bird mostly does not. This forces children to think about needs being met without human help — by the world itself. It also introduces the word "habitat" as a place where needs get met.

LensDaily FocusWhat Children DoWhy It Matters
ObservationBird in nature studyPhoto or short video clip of a wild bird eating seeds, drinking from a puddle, sitting in a nest. "What do you notice?" Teacher lists observations without comment. Then: "Is there anything the bird needs that you don't see in the picture?"The "what's missing?" question is the Pre-K version of gap detection — noticing what information is absent. This directly precedes the Tier 1 skill of noticing a missing reason.
Sorting & PatternDog vs. bird comparisonTwo columns on chart paper. Children place picture cards under "dog needs" or "bird needs" — or in both columns if the need is shared. Food and water go in both. A nest goes under bird only.Comparison is structured reasoning. Noticing that some needs are shared and some are specific is the earliest form of set logic — the same cognitive move as a Venn diagram.
DrawingDraw a bird's homeChildren draw where a bird lives and what it finds there. Label: "A bird needs ___ because ___." Teacher scribes dictation.Drawing the habitat rather than the animal shifts attention to context — where needs are met, not just what the needs are.
Building & PlayBuild a bird habitatNatural materials (sticks, leaves, small rocks, fabric scraps). Children build a bird habitat. Teacher: "Would this bird have enough food? What would happen in winter when the seeds are gone?"The winter question introduces change over time — a pattern in the environment. This connects to the Kindergarten moon unit and to the broader curriculum emphasis on noticing change.
Talking About WhyCompare: dog and birdTeacher asks: "Does a bird need the same things as a dog? What is the same? What is different?" Children answer in "because" sentences. Key insight to surface: both need food and water, but they get it from different places.Noticing similarity-in-difference is a core reasoning move. Both the dog and the bird need water, but one gets it from a bowl and one from a puddle — same need, different context.

By end of Week 3

  • Can identify at least two needs shared by both dog and bird
  • Can identify at least one need that is different (e.g., a nest vs. a dog bed)
  • Has used the word "habitat" at least once in conversation
  • Has noticed something "missing" from a picture — pointed to absent evidence
Week 4 · Pre-K Unit

What Does a Fish Need?

The third animal. The most different. The comparison now has three columns.

A fish lives underwater — a habitat that children cannot directly inhabit. This forces children to reason about needs in a context they cannot personally verify through their own body experience. It is the first time in the unit that observation must substitute entirely for first-hand knowledge. The three-way comparison at the end of the week is the unit's culminating cognitive exercise.

LensDaily FocusWhat Children DoWhy It Matters
ObservationFish tank studyIf possible: observe a live fish tank for 5 minutes in silence. Children draw what they see. Then: "What does the tank have in it? What would happen if we took the water out?" Teacher draws the "no water" outcome on chart paper to make the consequence visible.Observing a fish removes the familiar (pet dog, backyard bird) and requires careful attention to an unfamiliar environment. The "what if we removed X?" question is the earliest version of a controlled-variable thought experiment.
Sorting & PatternThree-animal sortAll three sets of need cards (dog, bird, fish) on the table. Children sort into shared needs and animal-specific needs. Teacher makes three columns + one shared column. What goes in the middle? Food, water — all three need these.The three-way sort surfaces the universal: all living things need food and water. This generalizable conclusion is the unit's core claim and models inductive reasoning from specific examples to a general rule.
DrawingMy favorite animal's needsChildren choose any of the three animals and draw everything that animal needs to stay alive. Teacher scribes: "A ___ needs ___, ___, and ___ to stay alive because ___."Free choice within a constrained frame. Children apply the unit's structure to their preferred example — the earliest form of transferring a framework to a new instance.
Building & PlayBuild all three habitatsStation activity: three build zones set up for dog habitat, bird habitat, fish habitat. Children rotate. At the end, the class walks the three stations: "What's the same? What's different?"The gallery walk structure trains children to evaluate and compare work — a social version of the comparison reasoning practiced all week.
Talking About WhyThe big becauseClass chart: "All animals need ___ because ___." Children contribute. Teacher helps them land on: food, water, shelter, safety. Final question: "Is there any animal that doesn't need food? Why not?" Children reason from the pattern they have observed across three animals.Generalizing from three examples to "all animals" is the unit's culminating inference. It is explicitly inductive — and age-appropriately presented as a strong pattern, not an absolute rule.

By end of Week 4

  • Can name at least three needs shared by all three animals (food, water, shelter/home)
  • Can say why a fish would die without water — using "because"
  • Has participated in the three-animal comparison chart
  • Has a drawing showing a complete animal habitat with labeled needs
Unit Assessment · Pre-K

What Mastery Looks Like

No tests. No grades. Observable behaviors only.

Assessment at Pre-K is entirely observational. The teacher watches for three behaviors across the four weeks — not in a single sitting, but as a pattern across daily interactions.

Observable Indicator 1 — Spontaneous "Because"

  • What to look for: Child uses "because" unprompted when explaining something — not just in structured activities but during free play, snack, or transitions.
  • Example: "I don't want to go outside because it's cold" or "The fish died because there was no water."
  • Why it matters: Spontaneous use indicates internalization — the child has adopted causal language as a natural way of talking, not just a game played during circle time.

Observable Indicator 2 — Noticing What's Missing

  • What to look for: Child points out when something necessary is absent from a picture, a drawing, or a classmate's habitat build. "That bird doesn't have any food." "Where does the dog drink?"
  • Why it matters: Gap detection — noticing absent information — is the earliest form of argument evaluation. It is what Tier 1 formalizes as "they made a claim but didn't say why."

Observable Indicator 3 — Transfer to New Animal

  • Transfer test: Show the child a picture of an animal not studied in class (e.g., a rabbit). Ask: "What does this animal need to stay alive?" A child who has internalized the unit's framework will apply the pattern — food, water, shelter — without needing to have studied rabbits specifically.
  • Failure mode to watch: Children who can answer correctly for dog, bird, and fish but say "I don't know" for the new animal have memorized three specific cases rather than extracted the general principle. Return to the three-animal comparison chart and ask: "Do you think a rabbit is more like a dog, a bird, or a fish?"

What does not count as mastery: Correctly completing a worksheet about animal needs. Reciting "food, water, shelter" when directly asked. Performing the task during the unit week but not applying it during free play or in conversation about unrelated animals. Mastery at this age is behavioral — it shows up in how the child talks, not in structured performance tasks.

Gating Connection · Pre-K

How This Unit Connects to Gating Criteria

Pre-K gating is about readiness for Kindergarten, not content mastery.

Gating Criteria Addressed by This Unit

  • Uses "because" in spontaneous speech — this unit introduces and practices causal language every day across four weeks in multiple contexts.
  • Can sort objects by a self-identified rule — the needs/wants sort and the three-animal comparison directly exercise this skill with increasingly complex materials.
  • Draws to externalize thinking — every week includes a structured drawing task where the drawing must contain intentional content, not just free expression.
  • Notices when something important is missing — Week 3's "what's not in the picture?" prompt and Week 4's "what would happen without water?" both train gap detection in an age-appropriate form.

A child who completes this unit has not learned facts about dogs, birds, and fish. They have learned to observe, sort, compare, and say "because." Those are the four cognitive moves that all subsequent curriculum depends on.
