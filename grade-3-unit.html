Tier 3
How Do We Know Dinosaurs Were Real If Nobody Alive Has Seen One?

Dinosaurs are universally compelling to eight-year-olds — and the epistemological question they raise is serious. How does a chain of evidence from fossils, geology, and comparative anatomy add up to confident knowledge about animals that went extinct 65 million years ago? This unit uses that chain to teach source evaluation and Boolean logic as practical tools for tracing and verifying knowledge.

Unit Design · Tier 3

What This Unit Builds

Five weeks tracing how humans come to know things they cannot directly observe.

The dinosaur question is chosen because it makes explicit a challenge that applies to enormous portions of human knowledge: how do we know things we can't directly see? The answer — that we build evidence chains, evaluate the reliability of each link, and apply logical operators to combine evidence — is the same answer that applies to historical claims, scientific claims, and many everyday arguments.

Boolean logic (AND, OR, NOT) is introduced not as computer science but as the natural language of compound evidence evaluation. "Is this source reliable?" becomes: "Is it from an expert (true/false) AND does it cite evidence (true/false)?" Students learn that combining two weak sources with OR does not produce strong evidence — while combining two independent strong sources with AND does.

What you will see by Week 5: Students who ask "where did you get that?" before accepting any factual claim. Students who can distinguish a museum paleontology database from a personal blog and explain why one is more reliable. Students who use "AND" and "OR" correctly when evaluating whether a compound claim is supported.

Week 1 · Tier 3 Unit

What Counts as Evidence?

Before evaluating sources, students must be able to define evidence. Not all evidence is equal — and some things that feel like evidence are not.

LensDaily FocusWhat Students DoReasoning Connection
Literacy & WritingTypes of evidenceStudents sort a set of "evidence cards" into categories: physical evidence (fossil, bone), documented observation (scientist's published field notes), testimony (witness account), hearsay (someone said that someone said), and opinion (what a person thinks without supporting data). For dinosaurs: which types of evidence do we actually have?Not all evidence is equally strong. Physical evidence that cannot be changed after the fact is generally stronger than testimony, which is subject to memory error and bias. Students build this hierarchy before applying it to sources.
MathematicsTimeline — 65 million yearsStudents work with a scaled number line to place key events: dinosaur extinction (65 mya), first humans (0.3 mya), ancient Egypt (0.005 mya), today. How far back is 65 million years on a line where 1 cm = 1 million years? Students calculate: 65 cm vs. 3 mm for human history.The scale of geological time is not intuitive. Making it physical — the dinosaur extinction is 65 cm away while all of human history is 3 mm — builds genuine proportional intuition and motivates the epistemological question: how can we know things from that long ago?
VisualizationEvidence chain diagramTeacher introduces the evidence chain: a series of connected arrows where each link represents one piece of evidence and how it supports the next. Example: fossil found → bone density analysis → comparison to living birds → conclusion about diet. Students copy the structure and fill it in with teacher guidance.An evidence chain makes the multi-step nature of complex knowledge visible. Most claims about the distant past require chains of five or more inferential steps — each of which can be evaluated independently.
Project WorkWhat do we actually have?Students research (using two teacher-provided sources): what physical evidence of dinosaurs actually exists? Fossil types, locations, numbers of specimens. Students compile a list of the real physical evidence and classify by evidence type from the Week 1 sort.Grounding the abstract question in the actual evidence inventory builds students' understanding that "we have fossils" is not a vague claim — it is a specific, enumerable, geographically distributed body of physical evidence.
Argument/ReasoningThe skeptic's questionTeacher plays a skeptic: "How do I know those fossils aren't fake? How do I know scientists aren't making up the ages?" Students must address each challenge with evidence. Key insight to surface: scientists are not the only ones who have found fossils — farmers, miners, children have found them worldwide. The distribution of discovery is itself evidence against fabrication.Addressing a skeptical challenge systematically is the earliest form of the Red Team exercise that will be formalized in Tier 6. Students learn that skepticism requires a response — not dismissal, but a specific argument addressing the specific doubt.

By end of Week 1

  • Can sort evidence types by strength and explain the ranking
  • Has placed key dates on a geological timeline to scale
  • Has drawn an evidence chain with at least three links
  • Has responded to one skeptical challenge with a specific evidence-based answer
Week 2 · Tier 3 Unit

Who Do We Trust and Why?

Source evaluation criteria introduced — applied to three types of dinosaur sources.

LensDaily FocusWhat Students DoReasoning Connection
Literacy & WritingReading three sourcesStudents read three short texts about the same dinosaur fact: (1) a paragraph from a natural history museum's educational site, (2) a paragraph from a personal blog about dinosaurs, (3) a paragraph from a children's novel where a character discusses dinosaurs. Students write: "Which source do I trust most? Why?" Before criteria are given — first record initial judgments.Recording initial judgments before teaching criteria allows students to discover whether their intuitions are well-founded. Most will correctly prefer the museum source — but asking "why?" forces them to articulate criteria they already implicitly use.
MathematicsSource quality checklistTeacher introduces a four-criterion checklist for source evaluation: (1) Is the author identified and credentialed? (2) Is the date of publication given? (3) Are other sources cited? (4) Does the content distinguish between known facts and interpretation? Students apply the checklist to each of the three sources and score them 0–4.A numerical checklist quantifies judgment — making it inspectable and comparable. Students who score the museum source 4/4 and the novel 1/4 now have a principled basis for their initial preference, not just a feeling.
VisualizationSource comparison matrixThree-column table: one column per source. Rows: author, date, citations, fact/interpretation distinction, final score. Students complete the matrix for all three sources. Identify: "Is there any fact that appears in all three sources? Is there any fact that appears in only one?"Cross-source verification — checking whether a claim appears in multiple independent sources — is the most important practical source evaluation skill. A fact that appears in only one source is less certain than one that appears in many.
Project WorkFind a fourth sourceStudents (with teacher guidance) find a fourth source about the same dinosaur fact using teacher-provided links. Apply the checklist. Compare to the three existing sources. "Does the new source agree? Does it add new information or contradict the others?"Adding a new source to an existing comparison extends the source evaluation from a one-time exercise to an ongoing process. Students learn that researchers do not stop at three sources — they keep adding until the picture stabilizes.
Argument/ReasoningSource citation in argumentStudents write a short argument paragraph about one dinosaur fact, including a citation: "According to [museum source], ___. I trust this source because ___." Compare to an argument with no citation: "Which argument would you trust more? Why?"Citing sources in an argument is not just an academic convention — it is an evidence-quality signal. A cited source can be checked; an uncited source cannot. Tier 3 introduces this as a structural feature of good arguments, not just a rule to follow.

By end of Week 2

  • Has scored all three sources on the four-criterion checklist
  • Has found and evaluated a fourth source independently
  • Can identify which facts appear in multiple sources and which appear in only one
  • Has written an argument paragraph with a citation
Week 3 · Tier 3 Unit

How Does Boolean Logic Help Us Evaluate Sources?

AND, OR, NOT applied to source evaluation — combining evidence correctly.

LensDaily FocusWhat Students DoReasoning Connection
Literacy & WritingAND / OR in everyday languageStudents analyze compound sentences: "You may go outside IF it is warm AND not raining." "The answer is correct if it uses evidence OR a valid argument." Students underline the AND/OR and determine what conditions must be true for each whole sentence to be true. Write three compound sentences of their own using AND and OR.Boolean operators in natural language are used constantly but rarely named. Making them explicit — and showing students how to determine truth value from compound conditions — formalizes something they already do intuitively.
MathematicsSimple truth tablesTwo-variable truth tables: columns A, B, A AND B, A OR B, NOT A. Students fill in true/false for each row. Apply to source evaluation: "Is this source from an expert? (A) Does it cite evidence? (B) Should I trust it?" Trust = A AND B (both must be true). Students see that a source can be from an expert but uncited — and the AND means it still fails.Truth tables are the visual representation of Boolean logic. They make clear that AND requires both conditions, OR requires at least one, and NOT reverses. These are not abstract rules — they are the logical structure of how compound claims work.
VisualizationVenn diagram for source criteriaStudents draw a Venn diagram: one circle = "has author credentials," one circle = "cites other sources." The intersection = sources that have both (most trustworthy). Outside both circles = sources that have neither. Students place their four sources on the Venn diagram.A Venn diagram is the visual form of AND — the intersection is the region where both conditions are true. Students who can use Venn diagrams to represent compound conditions have a powerful visual reasoning tool.
Project WorkNOT — what to excludeStudents apply NOT logic: "A source that does NOT cite evidence should NOT be used as primary evidence in an argument." Students go through their source collection and flag any source that fails the NOT-test. Build a "sources to use" list and a "sources to exclude" list with reasons.NOT logic in source evaluation is elimination: what does not meet minimum criteria is removed from consideration before the argument is built. This is the information management version of the Boolean NOT operator.
Argument/ReasoningBoolean source checklistStudents reformulate their Week 2 four-criterion checklist as Boolean expressions: "Is the author credentialed? AND Is the date listed? AND Are sources cited? AND Does it distinguish fact from interpretation?" A source passes only if ALL four are true (all ANDs). Students re-evaluate their sources with this explicit standard.The Boolean formulation makes the criteria non-negotiable: a source that passes 3 of 4 criteria does not "mostly pass" — it fails the compound AND condition. This rigor is age-appropriate and mirrors how professional fact-checkers actually evaluate sources.

By end of Week 3

  • Can complete a simple two-variable truth table for AND, OR, and NOT
  • Has placed sources on a Venn diagram using two evaluation criteria
  • Has applied Boolean AND logic to source evaluation and correctly identified which sources pass
  • Has built a "sources to use" / "sources to exclude" list with Boolean-based reasons
Week 4 · Tier 3 Unit

How Do Scientists Build a Knowledge Chain?

Mapping the full evidence chain from fossil to confident conclusion — evaluating each link.

LensDaily FocusWhat Students DoReasoning Connection
Literacy & WritingReading a research summaryTeacher provides a simplified summary of how paleontologists determined that birds are descended from dinosaurs: bone structure comparison, feathered fossil discovery, cladistic analysis. Students identify each link in the evidence chain and annotate: "This is the evidence. This is what it proves. This is what it doesn't prove."Reading scientific argument — following the chain of inference rather than just accepting the conclusion — is the most important literacy skill for understanding complex knowledge claims. Annotation forces students to trace the logic, not just absorb the conclusion.
MathematicsChain strength calculationIf each link in an evidence chain is 90% reliable, and there are five links, the overall chain is 0.9^5 = 59% reliable. If each link is 99% reliable with five links: 0.99^5 = 95%. Students calculate chain reliability for different link qualities. Takeaway: strong chains require strong links, and more links reduce overall certainty.This is a profound probabilistic insight: the more inferential steps between evidence and conclusion, the more uncertainty accumulates — even if each step seems very reliable. This motivates scientists to seek shorter chains of more direct evidence.
VisualizationEvidence chain map — dinosaurs to birdsStudents draw the full evidence chain from "fossil found" to "birds are dinosaurs" using their reading annotations. Each link labeled with the type of evidence and a reliability rating. Mark the weakest link.Identifying the weakest link in an evidence chain is a critical skill: it reveals where the argument is most vulnerable to challenge, and where more evidence would most improve the overall conclusion.
Project WorkBuild your own chainStudents choose one dinosaur fact (size, diet, speed, social behavior) and build a five-link evidence chain from the physical evidence to the conclusion. Each link must be supported by a cited source. Present to a partner: "Attack my weakest link."Building an evidence chain independently, then inviting attack on the weakest point, is the highest-level critical thinking exercise in Tier 3. It combines source evaluation, Boolean logic, and argument structure in one task.
Argument/ReasoningConfidence levelsStudents rate their confidence in three dinosaur facts on a scale of 1–5: (1) Dinosaurs existed. (2) T. rex could run at 25 km/h. (3) T. rex hunted in packs. For each rating, students explain what evidence exists and what would change their rating.Graduated confidence — rating certainty rather than just true/false — is a sophisticated epistemological move. Scientific knowledge is not binary. Teaching students to hold proportionate confidence to evidence is one of the curriculum's most important long-term goals.

By end of Week 4

  • Has mapped a five-link evidence chain for one dinosaur fact with reliability ratings
  • Has calculated chain reliability for at least two scenarios
  • Has built an independent evidence chain and identified its weakest link
  • Has rated confidence in three facts and explained the reasoning
Week 5 · Tier 3 Unit

Can We Trust Our Own Argument?

Final synthesis — students audit their own evidence chain and present a complete, source-cited argument.

LensDaily FocusWhat Students DoReasoning Connection
Literacy & WritingThe "How We Know" essayStudents write a three-paragraph essay: (1) What we know about [chosen dinosaur aspect]. (2) How we know it — the evidence chain. (3) How confident we should be and why. Each paragraph must cite at least one source that passed the Boolean evaluation criteria from Week 3.A structured essay with explicit confidence levels and cited sources is the Tier 3 culminating argument form. It builds directly on the paragraph argument from Tier 2 and prepares students for the Toulmin structure in Tier 5.
MathematicsConfidence summaryStudents create a "confidence dashboard" for their essay: a simple bar chart showing confidence level for each key claim. The claim with the lowest confidence is the one most in need of more evidence — students write one sentence about what additional evidence would increase their confidence.Visualizing confidence levels across claims in a single argument makes argument quality spatial and comparable. Students who can see which claims are weakest can prioritize future research.
VisualizationComplete evidence mapFinal evidence chain map: all sources labeled and rated, all links connected, weakest link highlighted, Boolean evaluation results noted at the source level. This is the visual summary of the full argument.The complete evidence map is the Tier 3 equivalent of the Tier 1 three-box argument map — a visual external representation of the full logical structure of the argument.
Project WorkPeer auditStudents exchange essays and evidence maps with a partner. Audit task: (1) Is every claim supported by a cited source? (2) Does every source pass the Boolean evaluation? (3) Is the confidence level appropriate given the evidence chain? Auditor writes one comment for each criterion.Peer audit at Tier 3 formalizes argument evaluation as a social practice. Being audited by a peer — not just a teacher — creates a genuine accountability structure and teaches students to read arguments critically, not just to produce them.
Argument/ReasoningThinking Journal — what changedFinal Thinking Journal entry: "Before this unit, I thought knowing things from the past was about ___. Now I think it is about ___. The most surprising thing I learned about how we know things is ___."Metacognitive reflection on the epistemological process itself is the unit's highest-level goal. Students who can articulate how knowledge is built — not just what knowledge exists — have understood the deepest lesson of Tier 3.

By end of Week 5

  • Has written a three-paragraph "How We Know" essay with citations and confidence levels
  • Has created a confidence dashboard bar chart for the essay's key claims
  • Has audited a peer's essay against the three audit criteria
  • Has written a Thinking Journal entry about the epistemological process itself
Unit Assessment · Tier 3

What Mastery Looks Like

Transfer Task — New Knowledge Claim

  • Setup: Present the student with a brief paragraph about a historical claim (not dinosaurs — e.g., "Scientists believe the ancient Egyptians used ramps to build the pyramids"). Ask: "How would you evaluate whether this is well-supported? What would you look for?"
  • Pass: Student describes a process: find sources, apply the four-criterion checklist (possibly informally), look for multiple independent sources, check whether the claim or the evidence chain has weak links. Does not need to actually evaluate the claim — needs to describe the evaluation process.
  • Fail: Student says "I would look it up" without specifying how they would evaluate what they find. Looking up information and evaluating it are different skills.

Boolean Evaluation Check

  • Setup: Show two short source descriptions. Ask: "Which should I use in an argument about [topic]? Why?"
  • Pass: Student applies at least two Boolean criteria and explains the AND logic — not "this one seems better" but "this one has credentials AND cites sources, so it passes both required conditions."
Gating Connection · Tier 3

How This Unit Connects to Gating Criteria

Gating Criteria Addressed

  • Evaluates a source using at least three explicit criteria — the four-criterion checklist and its Boolean formulation are practiced across Weeks 2–5.
  • Applies AND, OR, NOT correctly to compound truth claims — Boolean logic introduced in Week 3 and applied to source evaluation through the rest of the unit.
  • Builds and evaluates a multi-link evidence chain — evidence chains introduced in Week 1, built independently in Week 4, and used in the final essay.
  • Holds graduated confidence proportional to evidence quality — confidence levels and the confidence dashboard in Weeks 4 and 5 directly address this criterion.

The Tier 3 gating question: Can this student trace how a piece of knowledge was established — identifying the sources, the inferential chain, and the appropriate confidence level? Students who can do this for dinosaurs can do it for any knowledge domain.
